1.1 What is Survival Analysis?

Survival analysis (also known as duration analysis in economics and reliability analysis in engineering) is an area of statistics that models and studies the time until an event of interest occurs.

Why Standard Regression is Insufficient

For some subjects the event of interest cannot be observed for various reasons:

  • The event has not yet occurred at the end of the study
  • A different event occurs before the event of interest (competing event)
  • A subject withdraws or is lost to follow-up
Goal of survival analysis: To model time-to-event data appropriately, and to make correct inference that accounts for these special features (censoring, truncation) — which standard regression methods ignore.

1.2 Common Functions in Survival Analysis

Let T be a non-negative continuous random variable representing the time until the event of interest. Knowing any one of the five functions below is sufficient to determine all others.

Survival Function S(t) Cumulative Hazard H(t) Hazard Function h(t) Mean Residual Life mrl(t) Distribution F(t)

Survival Function

S(t) = P(T > t) = 1 − F(t)
  • Probability that a randomly selected individual survives beyond time t
  • Decreasing function; takes values in [0, 1]
  • S(0) = 1 and S(∞) = 0

Cumulative Hazard Function

H(t) = −log S(t) ⟺ S(t) = exp(−H(t))
  • Increasing function; takes values in [0, +∞]

Hazard Function (Hazard Rate)

h(t) = lim[Δt→0] P(t ≤ T < t+Δt | T ≥ t) / Δt = f(t) / S(t) = −d/dt log S(t) = d/dt H(t)
  • Measures the instantaneous risk of the event right after time t, given the individual is alive at t
  • Always positive, but not necessarily monotone — can take many shapes
DistributionHazard ShapeExample Application
ExponentialConstant h(t) = λMemoryless processes
Weibull (ρ < 1)Decreasing (DFR)Infant mortality, early failures
Weibull (ρ > 1)Increasing (IFR)Wear-out failures, aging
Bathtub shapeDecreasing then increasingHuman life course, electronics
Exam tip: The hazard function is the most important summary of survival data precisely because its flexible shape can capture many real-world phenomena that the distribution function F(t) obscures.

Mean Residual Life Function

mrl(t) = E(T − t | T > t) = ∫[t to ∞] S(s) ds / S(t)
  • The expected remaining lifetime for an individual who has survived to age t

Mean Lifetime

E(T) = mrl(0) = ∫[0 to ∞] s·f(s) ds = ∫[0 to ∞] S(s) ds

1.3 Incomplete Data: Censoring

Censoring occurs when for certain individuals, the time to the event is only known to lie within a certain interval — a bound is known but not the exact event time.

Important distinction: With censoring, the subject IS in the sample and we have at least partial information. With truncation, the subject is entirely absent — no information at all.

Right Censoring (most common)

Only a lower bound for the survival time is known. Two latent variables:

T = true survival time C = censoring time Observed data: Y = min(T, C) and δ = I(T ≤ C) δ = 1 → event was observed δ = 0 → observation is censored
TypeDescriptionCensoring time
Type IAll subjects followed for a fixed time periodSame for all censored subjects
Type IIStudy ends after a predetermined r events occur= failure time of the r-th subject
RandomSubjects enter/leave at different times; various reasons for censoringRandom variable C
Independence assumption: For random right censoring, we assume T and C are independent. This is necessary because Y = min(T,C) means the joint relationship between T and C is not identifiable from data (Tsiatis, 1975). Independence is the most natural assumption and holds in many contexts.

HIV Study Example (Random Right Censoring)

Study enrolled patients Jan 2005–Dec 2006; ended Dec 2008. Event = death due to AIDS. Causes of censoring: death due to other cause, loss to follow-up, still alive at end of study.

Left Censoring

The subject has already experienced the event before entering the study — only an upper bound is known.

Y' = max(T, C') δ' = I(T > C') Example: Children entering a malaria study may have already had malaria → T < C', so only an upper bound for infection time is available

Interval Censoring

The event is only known to occur within an interval (L, U). The exact survival time is never observed — unlike right or left censoring where at least the observed time is exact.

Occurs when: Diagnostic tests are used at scheduled visits rather than continuous monitoring. Example: malaria is detected between the last negative and first positive test — T ∈ (last negative, first positive).

1.4 Incomplete Data: Truncation

Truncation occurs when individuals of a subset of the population of interest do not appear in the sample at all. No information is recorded for them — they are entirely absent.

Left Truncation

Subjects must satisfy a condition (reach truncation time L) before they can enter the study. Individuals who experience the event before L are permanently excluded.

Data: (T, L) observed only if T ≥ L Example 1: HIV study — subjects who die between HIV infection and seroconversion never qualify for inclusion. Example 2: Insurance — accidents below a cost threshold are never declared (left truncation from the insurer's perspective).

Right Truncation

Only subjects who have already experienced the event are included in the sample. Those with long survival times (or competing events) are never recorded.

Data: (T, R) observed only if T ≤ R Example: AIDS incubation study — only AIDS patients are enrolled, so those with T > R (long incubation / died of other cause first) are never seen in the data.

✍️ Short Answer Questions

Short Answer 1
Define the hazard function h(t) formally (including the limit definition) and explain in plain language what it measures. Why is the hazard function considered the most informative summary of survival data?
Model Answer:

Formal definition:
h(t) = lim[Δt→0] P(t ≤ T < t+Δt | T ≥ t) / Δt = f(t) / S(t) = −d/dt log S(t)

Plain language interpretation: h(t) measures the instantaneous risk of experiencing the event in the next infinitesimally small time interval, given that the individual has already survived to time t. Unlike the density f(t), the hazard is conditional on current survival — it describes the "force of mortality" at age t for those still alive at t.

Why it is most informative: The hazard function can take a wide variety of shapes (constant, increasing, decreasing, bathtub, hump-shaped) that correspond to distinct real-world phenomena:
  • Constant h(t): Exponential — memoryless, no aging effect
  • Decreasing h(t): Weibull with ρ < 1 — "infant mortality" or early failure
  • Increasing h(t): Weibull with ρ > 1 — aging or wear-out
  • Bathtub shape: High early risk (defects), low mid-life risk, rising late-life risk (aging)
The survival function S(t) often looks similarly shaped across distributions, making it less discriminating. The hazard provides a more sensitive diagnostic of the underlying failure mechanism.
Short Answer 2
Explain the difference between right censoring, left censoring, and interval censoring using concrete examples from the course. For each type, state what is observed and what is unknown about the survival time T.
Model Answer:

Right censoring:
Observed: Y = min(T, C), δ = I(T ≤ C). Unknown: whether the event eventually occurred after the last observation.
Example: In the HIV study, patient 4 is alive at study end (Dec 2008) after 37 months. We know T > 37 (lower bound), but the true death time is unknown.

Left censoring:
Observed: Y' = max(T, C'), δ' = I(T > C'). Unknown: the exact time of event — only that it occurred before the study entry date.
Example: Children (ages 2–10) entering a malaria study may already have malaria antibodies. If infected before enrolment, we know T < entry age (upper bound), but not the exact infection time.

Interval censoring:
Observed: (L, U) such that L < T < U. The exact time T is NEVER known — not even approximately.
Example: A malaria diagnostic test is negative at month 3 and positive at month 6. We know the infection occurred in (3, 6), but not the exact day. This is typical when assessments are made at scheduled visits rather than continuously.
Short Answer 3
Distinguish between left truncation and right truncation. Give one example of each from the course notes (not the same as above), and explain why truncated subjects cannot simply be treated as censored observations.
Model Answer:

Left truncation: Subjects must reach a threshold condition L before entering the study. Those who experience the event before time L are never recorded. Data: (T, L) observed only if T ≥ L.
Example (insurance): Car accidents involving costs below a certain threshold are never declared to the insurer. The insurer's dataset only contains accidents with high enough cost to trigger a claim — those with T < threshold are left-truncated.

Right truncation: Only subjects who have already experienced the event are included. Data: (T, R) observed only if T ≤ R.
Example (AIDS): When studying HIV-seroconversion to AIDS incubation time using an AIDS patient registry, patients with long incubation periods (T > R) or who die from other causes before developing AIDS are never recorded.

Why truncation ≠ censoring:
With censoring, the subject IS in the sample and we have a time bound. With truncation, the subject is entirely absent — we have zero information about them. If we incorrectly treated left-truncated subjects as censored, we would pretend they entered the study and were observed up to L, when in reality they never entered. This creates a systematic, non-random missingness that introduces bias into survival estimates. The truncation mechanism must be explicitly modelled by conditioning the likelihood on the condition T ≥ L (left) or T ≤ R (right).
Short Answer 4
Prove that E(T) = ∫[0 to ∞] S(s) ds starting from the standard definition E(T) = ∫[0 to ∞] s·f(s) ds. What assumption is required for this result to hold?
Model Answer:

Proof by integration by parts:
E(T) = ∫[0→∞] s·f(s) ds

Let u = s and dv = f(s) ds, so du = ds and v = F(s) = 1 − S(s).
Then: E(T) = [s·(1 − S(s))]|[0→∞] − ∫[0→∞] (1 − S(s)) ds

The boundary term: as s → ∞, s·(1 − S(s)) = s·F(s). This equals zero if E(T) is finite (i.e., s·S(s) → 0 as s → ∞). At s = 0, the term is 0.

Therefore: E(T) = 0 − ∫[0→∞] (1 − S(s)) ds + ∫[0→∞] ds

But ∫[0→∞] (1 − S(s)) ds and ∫[0→∞] 1 ds diverge individually; using the correct IBP result directly:

E(T) = ∫[0→∞] s f(s) ds = −[s·S(s)]|[0→∞] + ∫[0→∞] S(s) ds = 0 + ∫[0→∞] S(s) ds

Hence E(T) = ∫[0 to ∞] S(s) ds ✓

Required assumption: T must be non-negative and have finite expectation — specifically, lim[s→∞] s·S(s) = 0. This holds whenever E(T) < ∞.
Short Answer 5
A researcher ignores censoring in the German Credit Data and simply computes the sample mean of observed times (including censored ones) as an estimate of the mean time to default. Explain in detail why this produces a biased estimate and in which direction the bias goes. What should the researcher do instead?
Model Answer:

Why the naive mean is biased:
The German Credit Data has a 70.7% censoring rate — 707 out of 1,000 credits did not default during the study period. These censored observations have observed times Y = min(T, C) that are smaller than their true (unobserved) default times T. Including Y for censored subjects in a simple average treats their last-seen time as if it were their true event time, systematically understating the time to default for those subjects.

Direction of bias: The naive mean is downward biased (too small). Because censored subjects' true default times T would have been longer than their observed Y = C, using Y instead of T underestimates the true average time to default.

What the researcher should do instead:
  • Use the Kaplan-Meier estimator to estimate the full survival function Ŝ(t), which properly accounts for censoring at each event time.
  • Estimate E(T) as ∫[0→τ] Ŝ(s) ds (restricted mean survival time up to the largest observation τ), or use the area under the KM curve.
  • If a parametric model is appropriate, fit an Exponential, Weibull, or other distribution by maximum likelihood using the proper censored likelihood, then use E(T) = 1/λ or the appropriate formula.
Both approaches formally account for the incomplete information in censored observations and yield consistent estimates of the true mean time to default.
