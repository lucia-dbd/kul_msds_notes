1. Inferential Statistics: Introduction

Scientific research often aims to test whether a hypothesis about a population is correct. Since studying an entire population is rarely feasible, we use a sample and must account for inevitable sampling variability when drawing conclusions.

Three main inferential frameworks are distinguished in this course:

With $\mu_L$ denoting the Limburg mean and a sample of $n = 30$ babies: $H_0: \mu_L = 3.3$ vs $H_A: \mu_L \neq 3.3$, assuming $X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_L, \sigma_L^2)$.

2. Hypothesis Formulation

Definition (Hypothesis): A statement about a population parameter. Testing always involves a null hypothesis $H_0$ and an alternative $H_1$ (or $H_A$).
Definition (Simple vs Composite):
A simple hypothesis completely specifies the population distribution.
A composite hypothesis does not — it leaves some freedom.

Example: $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ is simple. $H_1: \theta > \theta_0$, $H_1: \theta < \theta_0$, $H_1: \theta \neq \theta_0$ are all composite.

3. The Critical Region & Neyman–Pearson Framework

A statistical test rests on three elements: (1) null and alternative hypotheses, (2) a rejection region based on a test statistic and critical value, (3) Type I and Type II errors.

Definition (Critical Region): The set of sample values for which $H_0$ is rejected: \[W = \{\mathbf{y} : T(\mathbf{y}) > c\}\] The test statistic $T(\mathbf{y})$ has an exact or asymptotic distribution $D$ under $H_0$, from which the critical value $c$ is derived.
Neyman–Pearson Lemma: For testing two point hypotheses $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta = \theta_1$, the uniformly most powerful (UMP) test rejects $H_0$ when: \[W = \left\{\mathbf{y} : \frac{L(\theta_0 \mid \mathbf{y})}{L(\theta_1 \mid \mathbf{y})} < c\right\}\] where $c$ satisfies $P(W \mid H_0) = \alpha$.
Definition (Consistent Test): A test is consistent if $\lim_{n \to \infty} \beta_n(\theta) = 1$ for all $\theta \in H_1$ — power approaches 1 as $n \to \infty$.
Definition (UMP Test): Test $A$ is uniformly most powerful if for any other test $B$ with $\alpha_A = \alpha_B = \alpha$: \[\beta_A(\theta) \geq \beta_B(\theta) \quad \forall\, \theta \in H_1\] UMP tests do not always exist, particularly for two-sided alternatives.

4. Type I and Type II Errors, Size, and Power

$H_0$ Rejected$H_0$ Not Rejected
$H_0$ TrueType I Error ❌Correct ✓
$H_0$ FalseCorrect ✓Type II Error ❌
Definition (Size / Significance Level): \[\alpha = P(W \mid H_0) = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ true})\] Also called the nominal size. In science, Type I errors are often more serious, so we keep $\alpha$ small (typically 0.05 or 0.01).
Definition (Power): \[\beta = P(W \mid H_1) = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ false})\] $(1 - \beta)$ is the probability of a Type II error. For composite $H_1$, power is a function $\beta(\theta)$ for $\theta \in H_1$.
⚠ Notes on Statistical Tests:
(1) Statistical significance ≠ practical importance. A p-value measures evidence against $H_0$, not effect size.
(2) Use two-sided tests unless a direction is a priori justified — one-sided tests reject $H_0$ more easily.
(3) Science never proves or disproves with 100% certainty; it only assesses the likelihood that something is true or false.
(4) Formulate the medical/scientific hypothesis in terms of $H_0$.

5. The One-Sample t-Test

Setting: $\sigma^2$ Unknown

When $X_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ unknown, we estimate it with the sample standard deviation and switch from z-scores to t-scores.

Test statistic: \[t = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{S/\sqrt{n}}, \qquad S = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2}\] Under $H_0$ and normality: $t \sim t_{n-1}$ (Student's t with $n-1$ degrees of freedom).
Critical region (two-sided): $W = \{\mathbf{y}: |t(\mathbf{y})| > t_{1-\alpha/2,\, n-1}\}$
William Gosset ("Student", 1876–1937): Published the t-distribution in 1908 under a pseudonym to protect trade secrets at Guinness Brewery. Key insight: when $\sigma$ is unknown and replaced by $S$, the ratio $(\bar{X} - \mu)/(S/\sqrt{n})$ follows a $t_{n-1}$ distribution — heavier-tailed than the standard normal to account for the additional estimation uncertainty. As $\text{df} \to \infty$, $t_\nu \to \mathcal{N}(0,1)$.
Birth Weight — Full Calculation:
$n=30$, $\bar{X} = 3.482$, $S = 0.56$, $\mu_0 = 3.3$, $\alpha = 0.05$, $t_{0.975,29} = 2.04$.

Hypothesis test: $|t| = \left|\frac{3.482 - 3.3}{0.56/\sqrt{30}}\right| = \frac{0.182}{0.1022} = 1.78 < 2.04 \Rightarrow$ Do not reject $H_0$.

p-value: $P(|T_{29}| \geq 1.78 \mid \mu_L = 3.3) = 0.086 > 0.05$.

95% CI: $\left(3.48 - 2.04 \cdot \frac{0.56}{\sqrt{30}},\; 3.48 + 2.04 \cdot \frac{0.56}{\sqrt{30}}\right) = (3.1075,\; 3.8525)$.
Since $3.3 \in (3.1075, 3.8525)$, consistent with not rejecting $H_0$.

Assumptions of the t-Test

(1) Data are normally distributed OR sample size is large ($n > 25$, by CLT).
(2) Observations are independent.

Hybrid Decision Procedure (Common Practice)

Compute $|t| = \left|\frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{S/\sqrt{n}}\right|$ → compute p-value $= P(|T_{n-1}| \geq |t_{\text{obs}}|)$ → reject $H_0$ if p-value $< \alpha$.

6. p-Values, Significance Testing, and Confidence Interval Duality

p-value (Fisher's significance testing): The probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one obtained, assuming $H_0$ is true. A smaller p-value indicates greater incompatibility of the data with $H_0$. \[p = P(|T_{n-1}| \geq |t_{\text{obs}}| \mid H_0)\]
⚠ Critical Misconceptions about p-values:
✗ "p = 0.086 means there is an 8.6% chance $H_0$ is true." → Wrong. p-value is $P(\text{data} \mid H_0)$, not $P(H_0 \mid \text{data})$.
✗ "p > 0.05 proves $H_0$." → Wrong. Failure to reject ≠ accepting $H_0$.
✗ "Statistical significance = practical importance." → Wrong. Large $n$ can make trivial effects significant.

CI–Test Duality

A $100(1-\alpha)$% confidence interval consists of all $\mu_0$ for which $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ would not be rejected at level $\alpha$. Equivalently, reject $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ at level $\alpha$ iff $\mu_0 \notin \text{CI}_{1-\alpha}$. This duality is exact for the t-test.

7. The Holy Trinity: Three Significance Tests Based on ML

Let $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\boldsymbol{\theta}_1, \boldsymbol{\theta}_2)$ with $\boldsymbol{\theta}_2$ under test. Denote $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ = unrestricted MLE, $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0$ = restricted MLE under $H_0$. For composite $H_0: \boldsymbol{\theta}_2 = \boldsymbol{\theta}_{20}$, or generally $H_0: \boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}$.

1. Likelihood Ratio Test (LR / Deviance): \[d = -2\left[\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0 \mid \mathbf{y}) - \ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mid \mathbf{y})\right]\] Idea: Measures the vertical drop in log-likelihood from the unconstrained maximum to the constrained maximum. Uses information from both $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0$.
2. Wald Test:
Simple $H_0$: $w = (\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_0)^T \mathbf{I}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_0)$
General $H_0: \boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}$: $w = \boldsymbol{\psi}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^T\left[\mathbf{J}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\hat{\mathbf{I}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{-1}\mathbf{J}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^T\right]^{-1}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$
Idea: Measures the horizontal distance between $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$, weighted by the curvature at $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$. Uses only $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$.
3. Score Test (Rao / Lagrange Multiplier):
Simple $H_0$: $s = \mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)^T \mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)^{-1}\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)$
General $H_0$: $s = \mathbf{S}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0)^T\hat{\mathbf{I}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0)^{-1}\mathbf{S}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0)$
Idea: If $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0$ is near the unconstrained maximum, the score (gradient) at $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0$ should be near zero. Uses only $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0$ — computationally attractive when unconstrained optimisation is hard.
All three tests: Under $H_0$, the statistics are asymptotically $\chi^2(\text{df})$, where
$\text{df} = \#(\text{parameters in full model}) - \#(\text{parameters in null model})$
TestEvaluated AtGeometric InterpretationComputes
LRBoth $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0$Vertical drop in $\ell$Two model fits
Wald$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ only (unrestricted)Horizontal distance from $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ to $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$One model fit
Score$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0$ only (restricted)Slope of $\ell$ at $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_0$One model fit (null)
The three tests are asymptotically equivalent under $H_0$ — differences vanish as $n \to \infty$ when $H_0$ is true. When $H_0$ is false, they may differ substantially in finite samples, but all grow without bound and p-values → 0.

8. Invariance Properties of the Holy Trinity

Fundamental principle: Inferences should not depend on arbitrary elements with no bearing on the decision. Two types of invariance are studied: (1) invariance to equivalent formulations of $H_0$, and (2) invariance to one-to-one transformations of model variables.

Type 1: Invariance to Equivalent Representations of $H_0$

If $\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = 0 \Leftrightarrow \psi_1(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = 0$ (two ways to write the same null), the test result should be identical.

TestInvariant to $H_0$ re-formulation?Invariant to variable transformation?
LR✓ Always✓ Always
Score✓ Always (any $\hat{\mathbf{I}}$)✓ If $\hat{\mathbf{I}}$ is $\hat{\mathbf{I}}_2$ or $\hat{\mathbf{I}}_3$
Wald✗ Not invariant✗ Not invariant in general
⚠ Wald Test Lack-of-Invariance Example:
Test $H_0: \theta = 0$ with $\hat{\theta} = 1$, $\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\theta}) = 0.01$.
$w_\theta = \hat{\theta}^2 / 0.01 = 100 \sim \chi^2(1)$, p-value $\approx 8 \times 10^{-24}$. Strong rejection.

Reparametrise: $\xi = \theta^{25}$. Then $\hat{\xi} = 1$ and by the delta method $\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\xi}) = (25)^2 \cdot 0.01 = 6.25$.
$w_\xi = 1/6.25 = 0.16$, p-value $= 0.69$. No rejection.

Same scientific question — opposite conclusions! The LR test is immune to this.

Type 2: Invariance to One-to-One Variable Transformations

If transformation $g$ on the data induces $\bar{g}$ on parameters and $H_0: \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = 0 \Leftrightarrow H_0^*: \psi_*(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) = 0$, then the test result on $\mathbf{z}$ should match the test result on $\mathbf{z}_*$. Spitzer (1984) showed Wald test statistics for regression coefficients depend on measurement units of $y$ (Box-Cox transformations) — the LR test does not share this deficiency.

CI Inversion and the Holy Trinity

A $100(1-\alpha)$% CI is obtained by inverting a test: all $\theta_0$ not rejected at level $\alpha$. Inverting the Wald test gives the Wald CI. Inverting the score test gives the Wilson interval (for proportions). Inverting the LR test gives the likelihood interval — transformation invariant and generally preferred.

9. Binomial Proportion: The Three Tests Compared

Testing $H_0: \pi = \pi_0$ with binomial data. MLE: $\hat{\pi} = y/n$.

Wald interval: $\hat{\pi} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat{\pi}(1-\hat{\pi})/n}$
Problems: (1) May contain invalid values (e.g., negative probabilities for $n=10, y=1$: $(-0.086, 0.286)$). (2) Not transformation invariant — different scales give different back-transformed CIs.
Score (Wilson) interval: Invert $d^2 = \left[(\hat{\pi} - \pi_0)/\sqrt{\pi_0(1-\pi_0)/n}\right]^2$.
Always lies in $[0,1]$. Better coverage for small $n$. Recommended in practice.
Likelihood (Profile) interval: All $\pi$ satisfying $\Lambda = 2[\ell(\hat{\pi}|y) - \ell(\pi|y)] \leq 3.84$, equivalently $\ell(\pi|y) \geq \ell(\hat{\pi}|y) - 1.92$. Requires numerical solution. Transformation invariant. Generally preferred.
Example ($n=10$, $y=1$, $\hat{\pi} = 0.1$):
Wald (probability scale): $(-0.086, 0.286)$ — invalid!
Wald (log-odds scale, back-transformed): $(0.014, 0.467)$ — valid but not invariant.
Likelihood 95% CI: $(0.006, 0.372)$ — valid, transformation invariant.
The likelihood ratio interval is preferred: (1) always contains admissible values, (2) transformation invariant — computing the interval on the log-odds scale and back-transforming gives an identical interval, (3) better coverage properties.

10. Inference Under Model Misspecification

Correctly Specified Model

Under regularity conditions with correct model $p(y|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})$, the MLE $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{mle}$ is consistent for $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$ and asymptotically normal:

$\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{mle} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\; \mathbf{I}_1(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)^{-1})$

The Fisher information $\mathbf{I}_1(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) = -E[\partial^2 \log p / \partial\boldsymbol{\theta}\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^T]$ can be estimated by $\hat{\mathbf{I}}_1$, $\hat{\mathbf{I}}_2$, or $\hat{\mathbf{I}}_3$ (expected, outer product, or observed information). The three are equivalent under correct specification via the Information Matrix Equality: $-\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) = \mathbf{B}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)$.

Misspecified Model: The Sandwich Variance

If the true DGP is $g(y|\mathbf{z},\gamma_0) \neq p(y|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})$, the MLE converges to the pseudo-true value $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$ minimising the Kullback-Leibler divergence: \[I(f:g,\boldsymbol{\theta}) = E_g\left[\log \frac{g(y|\mathbf{z},\gamma_0)}{p(y|\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right]\]
Asymptotic distribution under misspecification: \[\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{mle} - \boldsymbol{\theta}^*) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}[\mathbf{0},\; \mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)]\] where $\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) = \mathbf{A}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)\mathbf{B}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)\mathbf{A}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)$ is the sandwich variance–covariance matrix.

$\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = E\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \log p}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^T}\right]$ (expected Hessian, "bread"), $\quad\mathbf{B}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = E[\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^T]$ (outer product of scores, "meat")

Under correct specification: $-\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) = \mathbf{B}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)$ (information matrix equality), so $\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{I}^{-1}$.

The Holy Trinity Under Misspecification

TestUnder misspecification, $H_0$ null dist'n?Remedy
LR✗ NOT $\chi^2(\text{df})$ in generalNo simple fix — avoid or use corrected versions
Wald (robust)✓ $\chi^2(\text{df})$ with sandwich varianceReplace $\hat{\mathbf{I}}^{-1}$ with $\hat{\mathbf{C}}_n$

⚠ Critical result: Under misspecification, the LR test statistic does not asymptotically follow $\chi^2(\text{df})$ under $H_0$. However, the robust Wald and Score tests (using the sandwich variance) remain asymptotically $\chi^2(\text{df})$ and are asymptotically equivalent to each other under $H_0$.
Practical advice: Use sandwich (robust) standard errors as a default in applied work, or at minimum as a sensitivity check. The LR test, despite being the most invariant, is the least robust to misspecification. When misspecification is suspected (e.g., over-dispersion in Poisson regression), prefer robust Wald or Score tests.
