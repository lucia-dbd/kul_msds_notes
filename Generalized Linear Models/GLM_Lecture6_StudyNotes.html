1. (Quasi)Complete Separation

(Quasi)complete separation is a fundamental problem that can arise when fitting logistic (and other binary) regression models using maximum likelihood estimation. Understanding it deeply is critical for applied and theoretical exam questions.

Definition

Complete Separation: A situation where the responses ($y=0$ vs $y=1$) can be perfectly separated by a hyperplane in the predictor space. Formally, there exists a vector $\boldsymbol\beta$ such that $\mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta > 0$ for all $y_i=1$ and $\mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta < 0$ for all $y_i=0$.

Quasi-complete Separation: Perfect separation holds with the additional possibility that some observations lie exactly on the separating hyperplane (i.e., the inequality is non-strict for at least one observation).

Toy Example from the Lecture

Data: $y = (0,0,0,0,1,1,1,1)$, $x_1 = (1,2,3,3,5,6,10,11)$, $x_2 = (3,2,-1,-1,2,4,1,0)$

Notice that $y$ is perfectly predicted by $x_1$ alone: $$y = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x_1 > 3 \\ 0 & \text{if } x_1 \leq 3 \end{cases}$$ Therefore $P(y=1 \mid x_1 \leq 3) = 0$ and $P(y=1 \mid x_1 > 3) = 1$. This is a case of quasi-complete separation (obs. 3 and 4 lie exactly at the boundary $x_1 = 3$ with $y=0$).

Why the MLE Fails

When complete or quasi-complete separation occurs, the likelihood function is monotone increasing in the coefficient of the separating variable — there is no finite maximum. The MLE for that coefficient is literally $+\infty$ (or $-\infty$).

Key consequence: The iterative IWLS algorithm does not converge in the usual sense. Software packages produce a finite number (e.g., $\hat\beta_{x_1} = 15.288$ in R or $4.4921$ in SAS) but these are artifacts of the iteration limit. The corresponding standard errors balloon to thousands, making Wald tests completely unreliable ($p \approx 1$).

⚠ Exam trap: In the SAS output with complete separation, the Wald test gives p ≈ 0.94 (non-significant!) while the Likelihood Ratio Test gives p = 0.0039. This discrepancy is diagnostic: it arises because the Wald statistic depends on the (inflated) standard error, while the LRT compares log-likelihoods directly. Never trust the Wald test when separation is suspected.

Causes of Separation in Practice

Three main root causes appear in the lecture:

(1) Coding errors — a mislabeled predictor that is essentially a recoded version of the outcome. (2) Small or moderate sample size — with a larger sample, one would observe $y=0$ cases in the region $x_1 > 3$, breaking the separation. (3) Another version of the response included as a covariate — e.g., including a binarised version of the outcome as a predictor.

The lecture emphasises: "(Quasi)Complete separation does happen in practice with a non-negligible probability." It is not a rare theoretical curiosity.

2. Detecting Separation and Infinite Estimates

Lesaffre & Albert (1989) Iterative Ratio Procedure

Algorithm:
  1. Refit the model, progressively increasing the allowed number of IWLS iterations at each step.
  2. At each step, divide the current estimated asymptotic standard errors by those from the first fit to form ratios.
  3. If the sequence of ratios for a given parameter diverges (grows without bound), its MLE is $\pm\infty$.

Five strategies are discussed in the lecture. Understanding why each one is or is not satisfactory is essential for the exam.

4. Firth's Penalized Likelihood (Bias-Reduced GLM)

David Firth (1993) proposed a general solution applicable to all GLMs, motivated by the goal of reducing the $O(n^{-1})$ bias of MLEs.

The Modified Score Equations

Standard ML finds $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ by solving $\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{0}$ (score equations).

Firth's modified score function adds a penalty term: $$s^*(\beta_r) = s(\beta_r) + \frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left[\mathbf{I}^{-1}(\boldsymbol\beta)\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta)}{\partial \beta_r}\right] = 0$$ for $r = 1, \ldots, p$, where $\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta)$ is the Fisher information matrix.

Connection to Penalized Likelihood

The modified score $\mathbf{S}^*(\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{0}$ corresponds to maximising a penalized log-likelihood and likelihood:

$$\ell^*(\boldsymbol\beta) = \ell(\boldsymbol\beta) + \frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta)| \qquad L^*(\boldsymbol\beta) = L(\boldsymbol\beta)\,|\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta)|^{1/2}$$

The penalty $|\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta)|^{1/2}$ is Jeffreys' invariant prior for this problem. Its influence is asymptotically negligible (it vanishes as $n \to \infty$), so for large samples the Firth estimator and the MLE converge.

Properties of the Firth Estimator

  1. Always yields finite estimates — completely eliminates the separation problem.
  2. The $O(n^{-1})$ bias of the MLE is removed (second-order unbiased).
  3. Has smaller MSE than the MLE in finite samples (reduced variance + removed bias).
  4. Standard errors come from the diagonal of $\mathbf{I}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})^{-1}$.
  5. Inference uses LRT and profile likelihood CIs based on $\ell^*(\boldsymbol\beta)$ — not Wald tests.

Inference After Firth: LRT, Not Wald

⚠ Critical subtlety from the lecture: When using Anova(modPML, test="LR", type=3) in R, the function internally computes LRT as: Deviance(reduced model via ML) − Deviance(full model via Firth). This mixes two different likelihoods and can yield negative LRT statistics (as in the PI example: LR Chi-sq = −0.27). Always compare two Firth-fitted models directly:

modPML.nPI = glm(HG ~ NV + EH, ..., method="brglmFit")
lrt.PI = deviance(modPML.nPI) - deviance(modPML)  # = 0.633, p = 0.426
This correctly tests whether PI should be retained (answer: no, $p = 0.43$).

5. Pseudo-R² Measures for Binary Regression

Unlike linear regression, the OLS $R^2$ cannot be meaningfully applied to binary responses — it is theoretically impossible for it to reach 1 even with a perfect fit. Several pseudo-$R^2$ measures have been proposed.

McFadden (1974)

$$R^2_{\text{McF}} = 1 - \frac{\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})}{\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}}_0)}$$ where $\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_0)$ = intercept-only log-likelihood, $\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})$ = full model log-likelihood.

Interpretation: The intercept-only log-likelihood plays the role of the total sum of squares. The ratio measures proportional reduction in "error variance". Note: $R^2_{\text{McF}}$ does not reduce to OLS $R^2$ as a special case for linear models.

Cox & Snell (1989) — "Generalized R²"

$$R^2_{CS} = 1 - \left(\frac{L(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_0)}{L(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})}\right)^{2/n}$$

Key property: For linear models, $R^2_{CS}$ exactly equals the OLS $R^2$ — this is why it is called a "generalized" (not pseudo) $R^2$. However, its upper bound is $1 - L(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_0)^{2/n}$, which can be far below 1 (e.g., as low as 0.48 in some settings).

Nagelkerke (1991) — Corrected Cox-Snell

$$R^2_{\text{Nagel}} = \frac{1 - \left(\frac{L(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_0)}{L(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})}\right)^{2/n}}{1 - L(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_0)^{2/n}}$$

Divides $R^2_{CS}$ by its maximum possible value, rescaling to $[0,1]$. This is the most commonly reported pseudo-$R^2$ in medical/social science software.

Donner party data (polynomial model): McFadden = 0.209, Cox-Snell = 0.249, Nagelkerke = 0.334. The animal experiment (log-log link) gives McFadden ≈ 0.930, Nagelkerke ≈ 0.9998 — reflecting near-perfect fit.
⚠ The Nagelkerke R² can be misleading because it ranges to 1 by construction even if the model does not fit perfectly. Mittlböck and Schemper (1996) reviewed 12 pseudo-R² measures and found no single dominant choice. Use these as supplementary descriptors, not primary inferential tools.

6. Model Building: General Principles

The overarching goal is: "Find a model that fits the data reasonably well without unnecessary complexity." Model building blends art and science — there are no fixed algorithmic rules, only guiding principles.

Two types of models: (1) Mechanistic models — derived from subject-matter theory (e.g., PK/PD pharmacokinetic models). (2) Empirical models — data-driven with simple structural restrictions (linear, factorial, etc.). Modern machine learning offers highly flexible empirical models.

Principles P1–P10

PrincipleContent
P1Use prior scientific knowledge: research questions, theory, prior study results, common sense
P2Include interactions only when theoretically justified; higher-order interactions require very strong rationale
P3Prefer hierarchical models (principle of marginality): if an interaction $AB$ is in the model, include main effects $A$ and $B$; if $x^2$ is included, include $x$; always include intercept
P4Distinguish observational (complex selection) from experimental studies (model construction less critical — design determines model)
P5A single study is not conclusive. Replication is paramount
P6Groups of predictors (e.g., Big Five personality factors; dummies for a categorical variable) move in and out of the model together
P7Be aware of problems with automatic selection (stepwise, forward, backward): each test is conditional on previous tests; distributional theory incomplete; multiple comparison inflation; no model uncertainty measure
P8Model construction is iterative and creative
P9Post-selection inference is an active research debate — no settled consensus on how to do it correctly
P10Prediction objectives differ from explanation objectives — model averaging may be preferred to model selection for prediction

Explanation vs Prediction

Explanation ≈ scientific research: understanding mechanisms, testing hypotheses. Multiple roughly-equivalent models may coexist; model selection is not about finding "the" best model.

Prediction ≈ engineering: all that matters is predictive accuracy. Model choice should be evaluated on prediction quality. For prediction, model averaging (e.g., Bayesian model averaging) often outperforms selecting a single model.

7. Model Selection Criteria

After fitting competing models, formal comparison can be done via hypothesis tests (for nested models) or information criteria (for nested and non-nested models).

MethodApplicable toFormula
Wald testNested models$W = \hat\beta / SE(\hat\beta)$, or quadratic form
LRTNested models$-2[\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\text{reduced}) - \ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\text{full})] \sim \chi^2_{df}$
AICNested and non-nested$-2\log(L_{\max}) + 2 \times (\text{# params})$
BICNested and non-nested$-2\log(L_{\max}) + \log(n) \times (\text{# params})$
Smaller is better for both AIC and BIC. The penalty term penalizes model complexity (Occam's razor). BIC penalizes more heavily than AIC for larger samples (since $\log(n) > 2$ when $n > 7$), favouring simpler models.
⚠ AIC vs BIC: They optimise different targets and will not always select the same model. AIC selects the model closest to the "true" data-generating process in Kullback-Leibler divergence sense. BIC is consistent (selects the true model with probability 1 as $n \to \infty$ if it is in the candidate set). The lecturer's personal preference is AIC.

8. AIC and Akaike Weights

A single AIC value is meaningless in isolation. AIC values are only informative when compared across a set of competing models fitted to the same data.

Akaike Weights

Given $R$ candidate models, with AIC values $\text{AIC}_1, \ldots, \text{AIC}_R$:

1. Find $\text{AIC}_{\min} = \min_i \text{AIC}_i$
2. Compute $\Delta_i = \text{AIC}_i - \text{AIC}_{\min}$ for each model $i$
3. Compute Akaike weights: $$w_i = \frac{\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^R \exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_j)}$$
$w_i$ approximates the posterior probability that model $i$ is the best Kullback-Leibler model, given the data and the candidate set.

9. Case Studies Summary

Endometrial Cancer Study

Setting: 79 endometrial cancer cases. Response: HG (histology grade 0–II vs III–IV). Predictors: NV (neovasculization, binary), PI (pulsatility index), EH (endometrium height).

Problem: No patient with NV=1 has HG=0 → quasi-complete separation for NV.

ML results: $\hat\beta_{NV} = 5.80$ (SE = 402.2, p ≈ 0.99) — nonsense.

Firth results: $\hat\beta_{NV} = 1.66$ (SE = 0.75, p = 0.026) — NV is significantly associated with high-grade histology. EH is also significant (p = 0.0006). PI is not significant (p = 0.43 by LRT).

Hessian matrix: $\mathbf{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = -\mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{X}$ where $\hat{\mathbf{V}} = \mathrm{diag}[n_i\hat\pi_i(1-\hat\pi_i)]$. The observed information is $\mathbf{I}_n^*(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = \mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{X}$ and $\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = (\mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}$.

Donner Party Historical Data

Setting: 1846–47 wagon train stranded in Sierra Nevada. Outcome: survival. Predictors: age (continuous), sex (binary, female=1).

Best model by AIC: fem * poly(age,2) — sex-stratified quadratic age effect (Akaike weight = 0.82).

Nagelkerke R²: Main-effects model = 0.17; Polynomial interaction model = 0.33. Adding the non-linear sex-age interaction doubles the explained variance.

McFadden R²: 0.209 for the interaction model — typically values 0.2–0.4 indicate excellent fit in logistic regression.

Short Answer 1 · Core Concept
Explain the phenomenon of (quasi)complete separation in logistic regression. Why does it cause the MLE to fail? Illustrate with the toy example from the lecture.
Model answer:

(Quasi)complete separation occurs when a linear combination of predictors can perfectly classify all observations into the two response categories. In the lecture's toy example, the predictor $x_1$ alone perfectly separates $y$: all observations with $x_1 \leq 3$ have $y=0$ and all with $x_1 > 3$ have $y=1$. This creates a case of quasi-complete separation (observations 3 and 4 lie exactly at the boundary).

The MLE fails because the log-likelihood is a strictly increasing function of $\beta_{x_1}$ — as $\beta_{x_1} \to \infty$, the predicted probability $\hat\pi_i \to 1$ for all $y_i=1$ observations and $\hat\pi_i \to 0$ for all $y_i=0$ observations, driving the log-likelihood toward its maximum of 0. There is no finite value where the score equation $S(\beta_{x_1}) = 0$ holds. The IWLS algorithm notices this by producing astronomically large standard errors and a near-zero residual deviance — neither of which reflects genuine convergence to a maximum.

The procedure works by iteratively increasing the maximum allowed IWLS iterations and at each step dividing the estimated standard errors by those from the first fit. If the sequence of ratios diverges for a particular parameter, its MLE is infinite.

Short Answer 3 · Firth's Procedure
Describe Firth's (1993) penalized likelihood approach. What is the modified score function? Why does this guarantee finite estimates, and what is the connection to Jeffreys' prior?
Model answer:

Firth's approach modifies the standard score equations $S(\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{0}$ by adding a correction term: $$s^*(\beta_r) = s(\beta_r) + \frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left[\mathbf{I}^{-1}(\boldsymbol\beta)\frac{\partial\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta)}{\partial\beta_r}\right] = 0$$ This is equivalent to maximising the penalized log-likelihood $\ell^*(\boldsymbol\beta) = \ell(\boldsymbol\beta) + \frac{1}{2}\log|\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta)|$. The penalty $|\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta)|^{1/2}$ is Jeffreys' invariant prior — the reference prior that is invariant under reparametrization.

Why finite estimates are guaranteed: As $\beta_r \to \infty$ under separation, the fitted probabilities approach 0 or 1, causing $\hat\pi_i(1-\hat\pi_i) \to 0$ for all observations. This makes the information matrix $\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta) \to 0$, and hence $|\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta)|^{1/2} \to 0$. The log of this term $\to -\infty$, creating a counter-force that pulls the solution back to a finite maximum of $\ell^*$.

Properties: Second-order unbiased (removes the $O(n^{-1})$ MLE bias); smaller finite-sample variance than MLE; inference uses LRT and profile likelihood CIs based on $\ell^*$.

Short Answer 4 · R Output Analysis
A colleague runs the Endometrial Cancer probit model in R using Anova(modPML, test="LR", type=3) and reports that the LRT for NV gives Chi-square = 8.75 (p = 0.003) but the LRT for PI gives Chi-square = −0.27 (p = 1.00). Model answer:

The negative chi-square for PI is a tell-tale sign that Anova() from the car package is mixing two different likelihood functions. Specifically, it is computing:

LRT = Deviance(reduced model fitted by standard ML) − Deviance(full model fitted by Firth's penalized likelihood)

These two deviances are not computed from the same likelihood function. The ML deviance and Firth deviance are incommensurable — subtracting them can yield negative values, which have no valid chi-squared interpretation.

Correct approach: Fit both the full model (NV + PI + EH) and the reduced model (NV + EH) using method="brglmFit", then compute: 
modPML.nPI = glm(HG~NV+EH, family=binomial("probit"),
                  method="brglmFit", data=endometrial)
lrt.PI = deviance(modPML.nPI) - deviance(modPML)  # = 0.633
pchisq(0.633, 1, lower.tail=FALSE)  # p = 0.426
Conclusion: PI is not significant ($p = 0.43$) and can be dropped from the model.

Short Answer 5 · Pseudo-R²
Compare and contrast the McFadden, Cox-Snell, and Nagelkerke pseudo-R² measures. For the Donner Party data with the polynomial interaction model, the values are McFadden = 0.209, Cox-Snell = 0.249, Nagelkerke = 0.334. What do these tell us about model fit?
Model answer:

McFadden: $R^2_{\text{McF}} = 1 - \ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})/\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_0)$. Measures proportional reduction in log-likelihood relative to the intercept-only model. Does not reduce to OLS $R^2$ for linear models. Values of 0.2–0.4 are conventionally considered excellent fit in logistic regression.

Cox-Snell: $R^2_{CS} = 1 - (L_0/L)^{2/n}$. Is a genuine generalization of OLS $R^2$ (reduces to it for linear models). However, its upper bound is $1 - L_0^{2/n} < 1$, which can be as low as 0.48. This makes cross-model comparison on the [0,1] scale misleading.

Nagelkerke: $R^2_{\text{Nagel}} = R^2_{CS} / (1 - L_0^{2/n})$. Forces the range to [0,1] by dividing by the maximum. Most commonly reported in software but lacks the clean theoretical interpretation of Cox-Snell.

For Donner Party: McFadden = 0.209 suggests good fit (above the 0.2 threshold). Nagelkerke = 0.334 means 33% of the "explainable" variability is explained. The values are consistent with moderate predictive power — survival in the Donner Party was partly predictable from age and sex but involved substantial individual-level randomness.

Short Answer 6 · Model Building Principles
Explain the principle of marginality (P3) in model building. Give two examples where violating it creates interpretational problems. Also discuss the tension between Principles P2 (include interactions based on theory) and P7 (beware automatic selection).
Model answer:

Principle of Marginality (P3): A model is hierarchical if whenever it contains a higher-order term (interaction or polynomial), it also contains all lower-order terms from which it is composed. This is also called the "marginality principle" or "heredity constraint."

Example 1 — Interaction without main effects: A model with $\beta_3 x_1 x_2$ but without $\beta_1 x_1$ or $\beta_2 x_2$. If you shift the origin of $x_1$ by adding a constant $c$, the interaction term becomes $\beta_3(x_1+c)x_2 = \beta_3 x_1 x_2 + \beta_3 c x_2$, which introduces a $x_2$ main effect. The model's meaning is not invariant to location shifts — a fundamental interpretational defect.

Example 2 — Quadratic without linear: $\beta_2 x^2$ without $\beta_1 x$. Shifting $x \to x+c$ introduces a linear term. The fitted curve changes its location on the x-axis in a non-interpretable way.

Tension P2 vs P7: P2 says interactions should be theory-driven (not exhaustively tested). P7 warns that automatic inclusion/exclusion creates cascading conditional tests. The resolution: set up candidate models a priori based on scientific reasoning (P1, P2), respect hierarchy within each candidate model (P3), and compare the pre-specified candidate models via AIC or LRT rather than using automated stepwise procedures.

Short Answer 8 · Explanation vs Prediction
The lecture states: "Explanation is like doing scientific research. Prediction is like doing engineering development." Elaborate on this distinction. When would you choose AIC for model selection vs when would you consider model averaging? Use examples from the course.
Model answer:

Explanation (scientific research): The goal is to understand which predictors causally or associationally relate to the outcome, to test hypotheses, and to build scientific knowledge. Here, model selection should be guided by theory (P1), and there may legitimately be multiple "roughly equally good explanatory models" — the task is not to find "the" best model but to quantify evidence for competing scientific hypotheses. Example: In the animal experiment, both loglog and logit have biological support; the scientific discussion should address which is more mechanistically plausible.

Prediction (engineering): The goal is maximizing predictive accuracy on new data. Interpretability is secondary. Model choice is evaluated on out-of-sample performance metrics (e.g., AUC, Brier score, log-loss). Example: Predicting hospital readmission — what matters is accuracy, not understanding why each predictor contributes.

Model averaging for prediction: Rather than committing to one model, Akaike-weighted predictions combine the forecasts of all candidate models: $\hat{y}^{\text{avg}} = \sum_i w_i \hat{y}_i$. This typically outperforms single-model selection by accounting for model uncertainty, analogous to pooling multiple expert opinions rather than choosing one expert. This is the basis of ensemble methods in machine learning.

Short Answer 10 · Critical Thinking
A reviewer of your paper criticizes: "You should have used stepwise selection to find the best model rather than pre-specifying your candidate models." Using the principles from the lecture, construct a defense of the pre-specification approach and a critique of automatic stepwise selection.
Model answer:

Defense of pre-specification:
(1) Scientific grounding (P1): Pre-specifying candidate models forces the researcher to articulate prior scientific knowledge and hypotheses. The models tested reflect biological plausibility, not data-driven chance.
(2) Valid inference: Tests on pre-specified hypotheses maintain their nominal Type I error rate. Stepwise selection invalidates the distributional assumptions of all subsequent tests (P7).
(3) Model uncertainty is quantifiable: Akaike weights over a pre-specified candidate set give a transparent picture of evidence distribution across plausible theories — not possible with stepwise.
(4) Replicability (P5): Pre-specified models can be exactly replicated and updated by future studies with new data.

Critique of stepwise selection:
(1) Each test is conditional on the outcome of previous tests; the joint distribution of the test sequence is not controlled.
(2) Severe multiple testing inflation — with many predictors, spurious inclusions are common.
(3) The "final model" from stepwise is treated as if it were the only model ever tested, providing no measure of model uncertainty.
(4) The optimism of fit statistics (AIC, R²) for the final stepwise model is inflated — it was selected to look good on this particular dataset.
(5) Poor external validity: stepwise models are notoriously unstable across samples — small perturbations in the data produce very different final models.
Likely follow-up questions:
• Are there situations where stepwise selection might be considered acceptable? (E.g., purely exploratory analysis with no confirmatory inference.)
• How does Bayesian variable selection (e.g., SSVS, spike-and-slab priors) address some of these limitations?
• P9 states inference after model selection is an "active research debate." Can you name one methodological approach that attempts to correct for this? (E.g., selective inference / PoSI, data splitting, LASSO with debiasing.)
