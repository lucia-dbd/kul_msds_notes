1. Historical Origins & the Regression Concept

Francis Galton (1822–1911) — cousin of Charles Darwin — introduced the term regression to mediocrity (1875, formalized 1886) to describe the phenomenon that sons of tall fathers tend to be tall, but not as tall as their fathers, and sons of short fathers tend to be short, but not as short. The population "regresses" toward the mean across generations. The standardised simple regression equation Galton recognised: $$\frac{y - \bar y}{s_y} = r \cdot \frac{x - \bar x}{s_x}$$

Today, "regression" refers broadly to any model for the conditional mean $E(Y \mid X)$. Linear regression is the foundational model in statistics and the building block for Generalized Linear Models (GLMs).

2. The Simple Linear Regression Model

Formal Model: Given $n$ pairs $(y_i, x_i)$, the Simple Linear Regression (SLR) model is: $$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n$$

Combining assumptions 2, 3, 6, 8: $$y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i,\; \sigma^2)$$ The regression line $E(Y \mid X = x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$ describes how the mean of $Y$ changes with $x$.
⚠ Interpretation of Parameters:
$\beta_1$ = expected change in $y$ per unit increase in $x$ (slope).
$\beta_0$ = expected value of $y$ when $x = 0$ (intercept — may be non-interpretable if $x = 0$ is outside the data range).

3. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Estimation

The Least Squares Criterion finds estimates $\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1$ that minimise the sum of squared residuals:

$$Q(\hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i)^2$$

Taking partial derivatives and setting to zero yields the Normal Equations:

$$\sum y_i = n\hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 \sum x_i \qquad \sum x_i y_i = \hat\beta_0 \sum x_i + \hat\beta_1 \sum x_i^2$$
OLS Estimators (closed form): $$\hat\beta_1 = b_1 = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2} = \frac{SS_{xy}}{SS_{xx}}, \qquad \hat\beta_0 = b_0 = \bar y - b_1 \bar x$$ where $SS_{xy} = \sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i - \bar y)$ and $SS_{xx} = \sum(x_i - \bar x)^2$.
The fitted (predicted) value for observation $i$ is $\hat y_i = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_i$. The residual is $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$. Residuals are observable approximations of the unobservable errors $\varepsilon_i$.
Computational shortcut: The OLS slope can also be written as $b_1 = r \cdot (s_y / s_x)$ where $r$ is the sample correlation coefficient. This connects OLS to Galton's original regression equation.

4. Estimating the Error Variance $\sigma^2$

The Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) is the minimum value of the criterion $Q$: $$SSE = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2$$ It measures how well the fitted line tracks the data. A smaller SSE means a better fit.
Unbiased Estimator of $\sigma^2$: $$s^2 = MSE = \frac{SSE}{n-2}$$ The denominator is $n-2$ because two degrees of freedom are lost when estimating $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$.
⚠ Why $n-2$ and not $n$? The residuals $e_i$ satisfy two linear constraints: $\sum e_i = 0$ and $\sum x_i e_i = 0$ (from the normal equations). These two constraints consume two degrees of freedom from the $n$ residuals, leaving $n-2$ free to estimate $\sigma^2$.

5. Properties of OLS Estimators: The Gauss-Markov Theorem

Gauss-Markov Theorem: If $E(\varepsilon_i) = 0$, $\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2$, and $\text{cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j) = 0$ for $i \neq j$, then $\hat\beta_0$ and $\hat\beta_1$ are Best Linear Unbiased Estimators (BLUE) of $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$. That is, among all linear unbiased estimators, they have minimum variance.
This theorem does NOT require normality. It requires only zero-mean, constant-variance, uncorrelated errors. Normality is an additional assumption needed for exact (finite-sample) distributional results.

Exact Expectations and Variances

ParameterExpectationVariance
$\hat\beta_1$$\beta_1$ (unbiased)$\sigma^2 / SS_{xx}$

Note: Variance of $\hat\beta_1$ decreases as $SS_{xx}$ increases — spreading predictors further apart (larger range of $x$) yields more precise slope estimates.

$\hat\beta_1$ is a linear combination of the $y_i$'s: $\hat\beta_1 = \sum w_i y_i$ where $w_i = (x_i - \bar x)/SS_{xx}$. Under normality of errors, $\hat\beta_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\beta_1, \sigma^2/SS_{xx})$ exactly.

6. Decomposition of Variation: SSTO = SSR + SSE

Fundamental Identity: $$\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar y)^2}_{SSTO} = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2}_{SSR} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}_{SSE}$$
TermNamedfMeaning
$SSTO$Total Sum of Squares$n-1$Total variability in $Y$
$SSR$Regression Sum of Squares$1$Variation explained by the model
$SSE$Error Sum of Squares$n-2$Variation not explained by the model
Degree of freedom logic: SSTO loses 1 df (deviations sum to zero). SSE loses 2 df (two parameters estimated). SSR has 1 df (one regression parameter beyond the intercept). Importantly $1 + (n-2) = (n-1)$ ✓.
Geometric intuition: When all points lie exactly on the fitted line, $SSE = 0$ and $SSTO = SSR$, giving $R^2 = 1$. When the fitted line is horizontal (no slope), $SSR = 0$ and $SSTO = SSE$, giving $R^2 = 0$.

7. Coefficient of Determination $R^2$

$$R^2 = \frac{SSR}{SSTO} = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SSTO} = \frac{\sum_i(\hat y_i - \bar y)^2}{\sum_i(y_i - \bar y)^2}$$
$R^2$ is the proportion of total variation in $Y$ explained by the linear regression on $X$. It satisfies $0 \leq R^2 \leq 1$. In SLR, $R^2 = r^2$ where $r$ is the sample Pearson correlation coefficient.
⚠ Important caveats about $R^2$:
1. $R^2$ does not measure whether the model is correctly specified.
2. $R^2$ increases mechanically as predictors are added — use adjusted $R^2$ for model comparison in multiple regression.
3. High $R^2$ does not imply causation.
4. For regression through the origin, $R^2$ can be negative when computed as $1 - SSE/SSTO$.

8. Inference for Regression Parameters

Sampling Distribution of $\hat\beta_1$

Under normality, $\hat\beta_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\beta_1,\, \sigma^2/SS_{xx})$ exactly. Since $\sigma^2$ is unknown, we replace it with $MSE$ and use the t-distribution: $$t = \frac{\hat\beta_1 - \beta_1}{s(\hat\beta_1)} \sim t(n-2), \quad \text{where } s(\hat\beta_1)^2 = \frac{MSE}{SS_{xx}}$$
$(1-\alpha)$ Confidence Interval for $\beta_1$: $$\hat\beta_1 \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\, n-2}\; s(\hat\beta_1)$$ Test $H_0: \beta_1 = c$: $t = (\hat\beta_1 - c)/s(\hat\beta_1)$. Reject at level $\alpha$ if $|t| > t_{1-\alpha/2,\, n-2}$.
The key scientific test is $H_0: \beta_1 = 0 \Leftrightarrow$ no linear relationship between $X$ and $Y$. The same CI/test framework applies to $\beta_0$ using $s(\hat\beta_0)^2 = MSE\left(\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar x^2}{SS_{xx}}\right)$, with $n-2$ df.

Why Central Limit Theorem Also Works

Even without normality, the CLT guarantees that $\hat\beta_1 = \sum w_i y_i$ is approximately normal for large $n$, since it is a weighted sum of independent observations. Thus t-tests are approximately valid for large samples regardless of the error distribution.

9. Inference for the Mean Response vs. Prediction Intervals

Two distinct inferential goals arise when using the fitted line at a new value $x = x_h$:

GoalTargetInterval FormulaWidth
Confidence Interval (CI)Mean response $E(y \mid x_h) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_h$$\hat y_h \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\sqrt{MSE\left[\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h-\bar x)^2}{SS_{xx}}\right]}$Narrower
Prediction Interval (PI)A single new observation $y_{h,\text{new}}$$\hat y_h \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\sqrt{MSE\left[1 + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h-\bar x)^2}{SS_{xx}}\right]}$Wider
The PI is always wider than the CI because it must account for two sources of uncertainty: (1) uncertainty in estimating the mean response $\hat y_h$ (same as CI), and (2) the inherent variability $\sigma^2$ of an individual observation around the mean. The prediction variance is: $$\text{Var}(y_{h,\text{new}} - \hat y_h) = \sigma^2 + \sigma^2\left(\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h-\bar x)^2}{SS_{xx}}\right) = \sigma^2\left(1 + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h-\bar x)^2}{SS_{xx}}\right)$$
⚠ Both intervals widen as $x_h$ moves away from $\bar x$. Predictions become increasingly uncertain the farther one extrapolates beyond the range of the data. Extrapolation outside the observed $x$-range is statistically dangerous.
Lemma (Prediction distribution): If the linear regression model is valid, then: $$\frac{y_{h,\text{new}} - \hat y_h}{S_{\text{pred}}} \sim t(n-2), \quad \text{where } S_{\text{pred}}^2 = MSE\left(1 + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_h-\bar x)^2}{SS_{xx}}\right)$$

F-Test for Regression Significance: $$F = \frac{MSR}{MSE} \sim F_{1,\, n-2} \text{ under } H_0: \beta_1 = 0$$ Reject $H_0$ if $F > F_{1-\alpha;\, 1,\, n-2}$. Under $H_0$, $E(MSR) = E(MSE) = \sigma^2$, so $F \approx 1$. Under $H_A$, $E(MSR) > \sigma^2$, so $F \gg 1$.
F-test and t-test equivalence in SLR: For simple linear regression, $F = t^2$, so the F-test and t-test for $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$ are exactly equivalent. The advantage of the F-test is that it generalises naturally to multiple regression.

11. Regression Through the Origin

When the physical context guarantees $E(y \mid x = 0) = 0$, we fit the no-intercept model:

$$y_i = \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i$$
OLS Estimator (no intercept): $\hat\beta_1 = \dfrac{\sum x_i y_i}{\sum x_i^2}$

Unbiased variance estimator: $MSE = \dfrac{\sum e_i^2}{n-1}$ (only 1 df lost)
⚠ Cautions for Regression Through the Origin:
1. Residuals do NOT sum to zero — residual plots are harder to interpret.
2. $SSE$ can exceed $SSTO$, making $R^2 = 1 - SSE/SSTO$ potentially negative.
3. Unless you are very confident the true line passes through the origin, the intercept model is safer.
4. Even if the true function passes through the origin, it need not be linear.
Hubble's Law: Galaxy recession velocity $y$ vs. distance $x$: $y = \beta_1 x$ (Hubble's constant). From R: $\hat\beta_1 = 76.58$ km/s/Mpc, 95% CI $\Rightarrow$ age of universe $\approx [11.5, 14.3]$ billion years — statistically disproving creationist claims.

12. Multiple Linear Regression

General Linear Model: $$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \beta_2 x_{2i} + \cdots + \beta_{p-1} x_{p-1,i} + \varepsilon_i$$ Matrix form: $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ where $\mathbf{X}$ is the $n \times p$ design matrix (first column all 1's), $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is $p \times 1$, and $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$.
Why linear regression is justified: Taylor's theorem guarantees that any sufficiently smooth function $f(\mathbf{x})$ can be locally approximated by a linear function. So even when the true relationship is nonlinear, a linear model is often a useful local approximation.

OLS in Matrix Form

Normal equations: $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$ $$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$$ (requires $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ to be invertible, i.e., no perfect multicollinearity)
Properties of the MLR OLS Estimator:
— Unbiased: $E(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \boldsymbol{\beta}$
— Variance: $\text{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \sigma^2(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}$
— Gauss-Markov: $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}$ is BLUE under homoscedastic, uncorrelated errors.
— Consistent: $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol{\beta}$ as $n \to \infty$.
— Asymptotic normality: $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \boldsymbol{\beta}) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{M}_{XX}^{-1})$ where $\mathbf{M}_{XX} = \text{plim}(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}/n)$.

Interpretation of $\beta_k$ in Multiple Regression

$\beta_k$ represents the expected change in $y$ per unit increase in $x_k$, holding all other predictors constant (ceteris paribus). This partial-effect interpretation makes MLR fundamentally different from looking at multiple SLR models separately.

13. Connection to Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Under the normality assumption $y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma^2)$, the log-likelihood is:

$$\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma^2 \mid \mathbf{y}, \mathbf{X}) = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol{\beta})^2 + \text{const}$$
Maximising the log-likelihood with respect to $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is equivalent to minimising $\sum(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol{\beta})^2$ — the OLS criterion. Therefore, OLS = MLE under normal errors. The MLE framework is more general (GLMs, non-normal responses) and connects regression to the "Holy Trinity" of tests from Chapter 2.
Score and Hessian: $$\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T(\mathbf{y} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$$ Fisher information: $\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}/\sigma^2$, so $\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma^2(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1})$ exactly under normality.
The MLE framework allows the Wald, LR, and Score tests from Chapter 2 to be applied directly to regression coefficients. The F-test in regression is a special case of the LR test statistic under the normal linear model.
