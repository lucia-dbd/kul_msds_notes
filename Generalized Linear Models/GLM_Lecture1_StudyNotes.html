1. Randomness & Probability Distributions

Randomness is defined as the occurrence of events for which no final cause is found. It appears in physics (quantum mechanics), biology (evolution), psychology (synchronicity), and mathematics/statistics.

From Frequency to Probability Distributions

A histogram of measurements has class widths. As the number of observations grows indefinitely, the class width can be made arbitrarily small, and in the limit a smooth probability density curve arises.

Key Distributions

Normal Distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$: Two parameters, symmetric, Mode = Median = Mean.
$$f(x) = \frac{e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$$ 68% of data within $\pm 1\sigma$; 95% within $\pm 2\sigma$; 99.7% within $\pm 3\sigma$.

Many real-world measurements follow the normal distribution (body length, IQ, temperature), explained ultimately by the Central Limit Theorem.

2. Inferential Statistics & Approaches

Inferential statistics makes quantitative statements about population characteristics from a sample, because examining entire populations is often impossible.

The Frequentist Approach

Any experiment can be seen as one of an infinite sequence of repetitions. Unknown parameters are treated as fixed but unknown constants — not random variables. Inference requires that correct conclusions are drawn with high probability across this notional set of repetitions.

Standard Notation

We write $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)^T$ for the data vector. The $y_i$ have distribution $p(y \mid \theta)$ and are assumed conditionally independent given $\theta$:

$$p(\mathbf{y} \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(y_i \mid \theta)$$

3. Point Estimation

A point estimator $\hat{\theta}(\mathbf{y})$ is a single-value "best guess" of the unknown parameter $\theta$. It has a sampling distribution inherited from the distribution of $\mathbf{y}$.

Mean Squared Error (MSE)

$$\text{MSE}[\hat{\theta}] = E_\theta[(\hat{\theta}-\theta)^2] = \text{Var}_\theta[\hat{\theta}] + \text{bias}[\hat{\theta}]^2$$

A good estimator concentrates its sampling distribution near the true $\theta$.

Bias

Bias: $\text{bias}[\hat{\theta}] = E_\theta[\hat{\theta}] - \theta$. An estimator is unbiased when bias $= 0$.
Example — Sample Mean & Sample Variance
For i.i.d. data: $\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum y_i$ is unbiased for $\mu$. But $S^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2$ satisfies $E[S^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2 < \sigma^2$ — it is biased.
⚠ Warning
Unbiasedness is a debatable property. A biased estimator can have a smaller MSE than an unbiased one if its variance is sufficiently reduced. Example: the biased $S^2$ (divisor $n$) is consistent and has lower MSE in some situations.

Consistency

Definition
$\hat{\theta}_n$ is consistent for $\theta$ if $\lim_{n\to\infty} P(|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta| > \varepsilon) \to 0$ for any $\varepsilon > 0$.
Law of Large Numbers: $\bar{y}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$ for i.i.d. data.

Bias vs. Consistency — Important Distinctions

EstimatorUnbiased?Consistent?Notes
$\hat{\mu} = y_1$ (first obs.)YesNoNever uses additional data
$S^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2$No (asymptotically yes)YesBias $\to 0$ as $n\to\infty$
⚠ Pathological Example
For Poisson $\lambda$, the only unbiased estimator of $e^{-2\lambda}$ is $(-1)^y$ — which can be negative and is useless in practice. This illustrates that unbiasedness is not always a sensible criterion.

4. Central Limit Theorem & Delta Method

Central Limit Theorem (Univariate)
If $y_1, \ldots, y_n$ are i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, then: $$\sqrt{n}(\bar{y}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2), \quad \text{equivalently} \quad \bar{y}_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$$
Central Limit Theorem (Multivariate)
If $\mathbf{y}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{y}_n$ are i.i.d. vectors with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance $\Sigma$: $$\sqrt{n}(\bar{\mathbf{y}}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$$

Why is the world normal? Observable traits (e.g., body length) are the sum of many independent small effects (genetic, economic, …). By the CLT, their sum converges to a normal distribution.

Delta Method

Delta Method
Let $\sqrt{n}(\mathbf{y}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \xrightarrow{d} \mathbf{z}$ and $\psi: \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^k$ differentiable with Jacobian $\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{y})$. Then: $$\sqrt{n}[\psi(\mathbf{y}_n) - \psi(\boldsymbol{\mu})] \xrightarrow{d} \mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\mathbf{z}$$ If $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}_p(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$: $$\sqrt{n}[\psi(\mathbf{y}_n) - \psi(\boldsymbol{\mu})] \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}_k\!\left(\mathbf{0},\ \mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\,\Sigma\,\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\mu})^T\right)$$
Example — Titanic Survival Odds
With $\hat{\pi} \sim \mathcal{N}(\pi, \pi(1-\pi)/n)$, define $g(x) = \ln\frac{x}{1-x}$ (log-odds). By the Delta Method: $$\ln(\hat{\Theta}_{\text{survival}}) \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\ln\Theta_{\text{survival}},\; \frac{1}{\pi(1-\pi)n}\right)$$ Note: no unbiased estimator of the log-odds exists.

Normal Approximation to the Binomial

For $y \sim \text{Bin}(n, \pi)$ with large $n$: $y \approx \mathcal{N}(n\pi,\, n\pi(1-\pi))$.

5. Confidence Intervals

A confidence interval is a stochastic interval $[u(\mathbf{y}), v(\mathbf{y})]$ designed to include the true parameter $\theta$ with a specified probability $1-\alpha$.

$$P(\theta \in [u(\mathbf{y}), v(\mathbf{y})]) = 1 - \alpha$$
Coverage probability: $1-\alpha$ is the long-run fraction of intervals (over repeated sampling) that contain $\theta$. For any particular realized interval, $\theta$ either is or isn't inside it — there is no probability for a fixed interval.

95% CI for $\mu$: $$\text{CI}_{0.95} = \left(\bar{X} - T_{0.975,n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}},\; \bar{X} + T_{0.975,n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}}\right)$$
⚠ Common Misconception
A 95% CI does not mean there is a 95% chance $\theta$ lies in the computed interval. The interval is random; $\theta$ is fixed. The correct statement: "95% of all such intervals constructed from repeated sampling will contain $\theta$."

Many 95% CIs are only asymptotically correct. For small samples, their actual coverage should be verified by simulation.

6. Likelihood & Maximum Likelihood Estimation

The Likelihood Function

Given data $\mathbf{y}$, the likelihood $L(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}) = p(\mathbf{y} \mid \theta)$ is the joint density viewed as a function of $\theta$. For i.i.d. data:

$$L(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i \mid \theta), \qquad \ell(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(y_i \mid \theta)$$

The likelihood plays a central role in all statistical paradigms: Bayesian, frequentist, likelihood, and information-theoretic (AIC).

MLE

The Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) $\hat{\theta}$ maximises $L(\theta \mid \mathbf{y})$, equivalently the log-likelihood $\ell(\theta \mid \mathbf{y})$. Intuitively: choose parameter values that give the observed data the highest probability.

Score Function & Hessian

$$\mathbf{S}(\theta) = \frac{\partial \ell(\theta)}{\partial \theta}, \qquad \mathbf{H}(\theta) = \frac{\partial^2 \ell(\theta)}{\partial \theta \partial \theta^T}$$

The MLE solves $\mathbf{S}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}$. Key properties of the score at the true $\theta_0$: $E[\mathbf{S}(\theta_0)] = 0$ and $\text{Var}[\mathbf{S}(\theta_0)] = \mathbf{I}(\theta_0)$.

Fisher Information

$$\mathbf{I}(\theta) = -E[\mathbf{H}(\theta)] = n\,\mathbf{I}_1(\theta), \qquad \mathbf{I}_1(\theta) = -E\!\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta\partial\theta^T}\log p(y\mid\theta)\right]$$

Information Matrix Equality (IME): Under regularity, $E[\mathbf{s}(\theta_0)^T\mathbf{s}(\theta_0)] = \mathbf{I}_1(\theta_0)$, so $\text{Var}[\mathbf{S}(\theta_0)] = \mathbf{I}(\theta_0)$.

Three Estimators of $\mathbf{I}(\theta_0)$

EstimatorFormulaName
$\hat{\mathbf{I}}_1(\hat\theta)$$-\mathbf{H}(\hat\theta)$Observed information
$\hat{\mathbf{I}}_2(\hat\theta)$$\sum_i \mathbf{s}_i(\hat\theta)^T\mathbf{s}_i(\hat\theta)$Outer product of scores
$\hat{\mathbf{I}}_3(\hat\theta)$$\mathbf{I}(\hat\theta)$Expected information at MLE

Cramér–Rao Bound

Cramér–Rao Bound
For any unbiased estimator $T(\mathbf{y})$ of $\psi(\theta)$: $$\text{cov}[T(\mathbf{y})] \geq \mathbf{J}(\theta)\,\mathbf{I}(\theta)^{-1}\,\mathbf{J}(\theta)^T$$ For $\psi(\theta)=\theta$: $\text{cov}[\hat\theta] \geq \mathbf{I}(\theta)^{-1}$. An estimator achieving this bound is (fully) efficient and is the minimum variance unbiased (MVU) estimator.

Asymptotic Properties of MLE

Under regularity conditions:
  • Consistency: $\hat\theta_{\text{mle}} \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0$
  • Asymptotic Normality & Efficiency: $\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_{\text{mle}} - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_1(\theta_0)^{-1})$
  • Equivariance: If $\hat\theta_{\text{mle}}$ is the MLE of $\theta$, then $\psi(\hat\theta_{\text{mle}})$ is the MLE of $\psi(\theta)$ for any 1-to-1 $\psi$.

Newton–Raphson Algorithm

Iterative method to solve $\mathbf{S}(\theta)=\mathbf{0}$:

$$\theta^{k+1} \approx \theta^k - \mathbf{H}(\theta^k)^{-1}\mathbf{S}(\theta^k)$$

Fisher's scoring replaces $-\mathbf{H}(\theta^k)$ with its expectation $\mathbf{I}(\theta^k)$. Convergence is quadratic but can fail at stationary points, with poor initial estimates, or when the Hessian overshoots.

7. Hypothesis Testing

Framework

A hypothesis is a statement about a population parameter. We test a null hypothesis $H_0$ against an alternative $H_1$.

$H_0$ Rejected$H_0$ Not Rejected
$H_0$ trueType I Error ($\alpha$)Correct
$H_0$ falseCorrect (Power $\beta$)Type II Error ($1-\beta$)
Size (significance level): $\alpha = P(\text{reject}\,H_0 \mid H_0\text{ true})$
Power: $\beta(\theta) = P(\text{reject}\,H_0 \mid \theta \in H_1)$
Consistency of a test: $\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta_n(\theta) = 1$ for all $\theta \in H_1$.

Three Approaches

ApproachToolKey Feature
Neyman–PearsonCritical regionPre-data; controls Type I error; defines power
Significance testing$p$-valuePost-data; compatibility of data with $H_0$
Confidence intervalsCIReject $H_0: \theta=\theta_0$ iff $\theta_0 \notin \text{CI}_{1-\alpha}$

The $t$-test (Gosset / "Student")

For $X_1,\ldots,X_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with unknown $\sigma$:

$$t = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1} \text{ under } H_0$$

Assumptions: data normally distributed OR $n > 25$; observations are independent.

Two-sided critical region: $W = \{\mathbf{y}: |t(\mathbf{y})| > c\}$ where $c = T_{1-\alpha/2, n-1}$.

⚠ Statistical vs Practical Significance
A statistically significant result says nothing about practical importance. Also: formulate the hypothesis in terms of $H_0$; use two-sided tests unless the effect can only go in one direction.

Uniformly Most Powerful (UMP) Tests

Neyman–Pearson Lemma
For simple $H_0: \theta=\theta_0$ vs $H_1: \theta=\theta_1$, the UMP test has rejection region: $$W = \left\{\mathbf{y}: \frac{L(\theta_0\mid\mathbf{y})}{L(\theta_1\mid\mathbf{y})} < c\right\}$$ UMP tests do not always exist for composite alternatives.

8. The Holy Trinity of Tests

Three large-sample tests based on the likelihood — all asymptotically equivalent under $H_0$.

Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT)

$$d = -2\left[\ell(\hat\theta_0 \mid \mathbf{y}) - \ell(\hat\theta \mid \mathbf{y})\right] \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(df)$$ $df = $ (# params full model) $-$ (# params null model)

Wald Test

Simple: $w = (\hat\theta - \theta_0)^T\mathbf{I}(\hat\theta)(\hat\theta - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(df)$

Composite: $w = (\hat\theta_2 - \theta_{20})^T [\mathbf{I}^{\theta_2\theta_2}(\hat\theta)]^{-1}(\hat\theta_2 - \theta_{20})$

Score Test (Rao / Lagrange Multiplier)

$$s = \mathbf{S}(\hat\theta_0)^T\,\mathbf{I}(\hat\theta_0)^{-1}\,\mathbf{S}(\hat\theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2(df)$$

Invariance Properties

TestInvariant to equivalent formulations of $H_0$?Invariant to 1-1 variable transforms?
LRTYes (always)Yes (always)
ScoreYes (always)Yes (if $\hat{\mathbf{I}} = \hat{\mathbf{I}}_2$ or $\hat{\mathbf{I}}_3$)

⚠ Wald Test Lack of Invariance
Testing $H_0: \theta=0$ can yield $p \approx 8\times10^{-24}$ with the Wald test, but reparametrising as $\xi = \theta^{25}$ (same hypothesis!) gives $p = 0.69$. This is a fundamental flaw of the Wald test.

Confidence Intervals from Tests (Univariate)

For a scalar $\theta$, a $100(1-\alpha)\%$ CI consists of all $\theta_0$ for which $H_0:\theta=\theta_0$ would not be rejected at level $\alpha$. This duality connects tests to CIs.

9. Inference Under Misspecification

If the assumed model $p(y\mid\mathbf{x},\theta)$ differs from the true DGP $g(y\mid\mathbf{z},\gamma_0)$, the MLE converges not to $\theta_0$ but to:

$$\theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \; I(f:g,\theta) = E_g\!\left[\log\frac{g(y\mid\mathbf{z},\gamma_0)}{p(y\mid\mathbf{x},\theta)}\right]$$

This is the Kullback–Leibler Information Criterion (KLIC). The MLE converges to the pseudo-true value $\theta^*$.

Sandwich Variance

$$\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_{\text{mle}} - \theta^*) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}[\mathbf{0},\, \mathbf{C}(\theta^*)]$$ $$\mathbf{C}(\theta^*) = \mathbf{A}^{-1}(\theta^*)\,\mathbf{B}(\theta^*)\,\mathbf{A}^{-1}(\theta^*)$$

where $\mathbf{A}(\theta) = E[\partial^2 \log p / \partial\theta\partial\theta^T]$ and $\mathbf{B}(\theta) = E[\mathbf{s}(\theta)^T\mathbf{s}(\theta)]$.

Under correct specification: $\theta^*=\theta_0$ and $-\mathbf{A}^{-1}(\theta_0)=\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\theta_0)$, so the sandwich reduces to the standard Fisher information-based variance.

Under misspecification: Use the robust Wald or robust Score tests. The LRT is not asymptotically $\chi^2$ under misspecification.
⚠ Key Result
Under model misspecification: robust Wald and robust Score tests remain asymptotically equivalent ($(w-s)\xrightarrow{p}0$). However, the LRT loses its $\chi^2$ null distribution.
