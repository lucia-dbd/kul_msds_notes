1. Randomness

Definition: Randomness refers to the occurrence of events for which no deterministic cause can be identified. It arises across many scientific fields: quantum mechanics (physics), the theory of evolution (biology), synchronicity (psychology), and of course statistics and mathematics.

The concept motivates the entire framework of probability and statistical inference. When outcomes are inherently unpredictable, we need a rigorous mathematical language to describe and quantify uncertainty.

3. Inferential Statistics

Inferential statistics makes quantitative statements about a population based on a sample. Since examining an entire population is often impossible, we draw samples and use them to make decisions or draw conclusions about population parameters.

The fundamental challenge: the sample is observed; the population parameter \(\theta\) is unknown. Statistical inference provides the tools to learn about \(\theta\) from the data.

4. The Frequentist Approach

The frequentist paradigm interprets probability as the long-run relative frequency of an event over an infinite sequence of identical, independent repetitions of an experiment.
  • Unknown parameters are treated as fixed (non-random) but unknown constants.
  • Correct conclusions should be reached with a specified high probability across the notional sequence of repetitions.
  • Contrasts with the Bayesian paradigm, where parameters are treated as random variables with prior distributions.

Notation

We use \(\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)^T\) for the sample. Each \(y_i\) has distribution/density \(p(y \mid \theta)\). Assuming conditional independence:

\[p(\mathbf{y} \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i \mid \theta)\]

The goal is to draw inference about \(\theta\).

5. Point Estimation

A point estimator \(\hat{\theta}(\mathbf{y})\) is a function of the sample data used to produce a single best guess for the unknown parameter \(\theta\). Formally, it is a random variable (because it depends on the random sample \(\mathbf{y}\)).

Mean Squared Error (MSE)

A key criterion for evaluating estimators:

\[\operatorname{MSE}\!\left[\hat{\theta}(\mathbf{y})\right] = E_{\mathbf{y}\mid\theta}\!\left[(\hat{\theta}(\mathbf{y})-\theta)^2\right] = \operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{y}\mid\theta}\!\left[\hat{\theta}(\mathbf{y})\right] + \operatorname{bias}\!\left[\hat{\theta}(\mathbf{y})\right]^2\]

A good estimator has its sampling distribution concentrated close to the true \(\theta\), i.e., small MSE.

⚠ Bias–Variance Trade-off: MSE decomposes into variance and squared bias. Minimising only bias can increase variance, and vice versa. A biased estimator can have lower MSE than an unbiased one!

6. Bias and Consistency

Bias: \[\operatorname{bias}\!\left[\hat{\theta}(\mathbf{y})\right] = E_{\mathbf{y}\mid\theta}\!\left[\hat{\theta}(\mathbf{y})\right] - \theta\] An estimator is unbiased when \(\operatorname{bias} = 0\).

Sample Mean & Sample Variance

For i.i.d. data with \(\mu = E(y)\) and \(\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(y)\):

\[\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i \quad\Rightarrow\quad E(\bar{y}) = \mu \quad \text{(unbiased)}\] \[S^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad E(S^2) = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2 < \sigma^2 \quad \text{(biased)}\]
The unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\) uses denominator \(n-1\): \(\hat{S}^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2\).

Consistency

Definition: \(\hat{\theta}_n\) is consistent for \(\theta\) if \[\lim_{n\to\infty} P\!\left(|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta| > \varepsilon\right) \to 0 \quad \forall\, \varepsilon > 0\] i.e., it converges in probability to the true parameter as \(n \to \infty\).

Law of Large Numbers: The sample mean \(\bar{y}_n\) is consistent for \(\mu\).
PropertyUnbiased?Consistent?Example
Unbiased & consistent✓✓\(\bar{y}_n\) for \(\mu\)
Unbiased, NOT consistent✓✗\(\hat{\mu} = y_1\) (ignores all other data)
Biased but consistent✗✓\(S^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2\) (bias → 0)
Poisson Pathology: To estimate \(\theta = e^{-2\lambda}\) from a single observation, the only unbiased estimator is \(\hat{\theta}(y) = (-1)^y\), which takes values \(+1\) or \(-1\) — clearly not a valid probability! This demonstrates that unbiasedness can be a pathological property.

7. Central Limit Theorem & Delta Method

CLT (Univariate): If \(y_1, \ldots, y_n\) are i.i.d. with \(E(y)=\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(y)=\sigma^2\), then: \[\sqrt{n}(\bar{y}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\] Equivalently: \(\bar{y}_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)\) as \(n \to \infty\).

CLT (Multivariate): For i.i.d. vectors with mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\): \[\sqrt{n}(\bar{\mathbf{y}}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\]

Why is the world normal? Many real-world measurements (height, IQ, temperature) are the sum of many small, independent effects. The CLT guarantees these averages are approximately normal.

Delta Method: Let \(\mathbf{y}_n\) be a sequence with \(E(\mathbf{y}_n) = \boldsymbol{\mu}\) and \(\sqrt{n}(\mathbf{y}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \to_d \mathbf{z}\). For a smooth transformation \(\boldsymbol{\psi}: \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^k\) with Jacobian \(\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{y}) = \partial\boldsymbol{\psi}/\partial\mathbf{y}\): \[\sqrt{n}\!\left[\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{y}_n) - \boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\right] \xrightarrow{d} \mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\,\mathbf{z}\] If \(\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}_p(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\), then the limit is \(\mathcal{N}_k\!\left(\mathbf{0},\, \mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\mu})\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\mu})^T\right)\).
Titanic Survival Odds Example: Let \(\hat{\pi}\) be the estimated survival probability. Then by CLT: \(\hat{\pi} \sim \mathcal{N}(\pi, \pi(1-\pi)/n)\). The log-odds \(g(x) = \ln(x/(1-x))\) has derivative \(g'(x) = 1/(x(1-x))\). By the Delta Method: \[\ln(\hat{\Theta}_{survival}) \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\ln(\Theta_{survival}),\; \frac{1}{\pi(1-\pi)n}\right)\] Note: No unbiased estimator for the log-odds exists.

Normal Approximation to Binomial

If \(y \sim \operatorname{Bin}(n, \pi)\) and \(n\) is large: \(y \approx \mathcal{N}(n\pi,\; n\pi(1-\pi))\).

8. Confidence Intervals

A confidence interval (CI) is a stochastic interval \([u(\mathbf{y}),\, v(\mathbf{y})]\) designed to contain the true parameter \(\theta\) with pre-specified probability \(1-\alpha\): \[P\!\left(\theta \in [u(\mathbf{y}),\, v(\mathbf{y})]\right) = 1 - \alpha\] The probability \(1-\alpha\) is the coverage probability; classically \(\alpha = 0.05\).

CI for the Mean

\[CI_{1-\alpha} = \left(\bar{X} - T_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}},\quad \bar{X} + T_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\]
⚠ Common Misconception: The 95% CI does NOT mean "there is a 95% probability that \(\theta\) is in this specific interval." Once the data are observed, \(\theta\) either is or is not in the interval. The 95% refers to the long-run proportion of such intervals that would contain \(\theta\) over repeated sampling.
  • Wider CI → higher confidence level (e.g., 99% vs 95%)
  • Narrower CI → larger sample size \(n\)
  • Many 95% CIs are valid only asymptotically; small-sample validity should be checked via simulation

9. Likelihood and Maximum Likelihood Estimation

The likelihood function \(L(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathbf{y}) = p(\mathbf{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})\) is the joint probability (density) of the observed data, viewed as a function of the parameters. For i.i.d. data: \[L(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathbf{y}) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})\]

Log-likelihood & MLE

Working with the log-likelihood \(\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathbf{y}) = \log L(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathbf{y}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(y_i \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})\) is mathematically more tractable. The MLE \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\) maximises \(L\) (equivalently, \(\ell\)).

Score Function & Hessian

\[\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{\partial \ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial \boldsymbol{\theta}} \quad \text{(Score function; gradient of log-likelihood)}\] \[\mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{\partial^2 \ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^T} \quad \text{(Hessian; matrix of second derivatives)}\]

The MLE solves the score equation: \(\mathbf{S}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) = \mathbf{0}\).

Fisher Information

\[\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \equiv \mathbf{I}_n(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -E\!\left[\mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]\] For i.i.d. data: \(\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = n\,\mathbf{I}_1(\boldsymbol{\theta})\), where \(\mathbf{I}_1(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) is the information from one observation.

Information Matrix Equality (IME): \(E\!\left[\mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)^T \mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)\right] = \mathbf{I}_1(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)\)
Hence: \(\operatorname{Var}[\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)] = \mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)\)

Three Estimators of Fisher Information

EstimatorFormulaName
\(\hat{\mathbf{I}}_1(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\)\(-\mathbf{H}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\)Observed information matrix
\(\hat{\mathbf{I}}_2(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\)\(\sum_i \mathbf{s}_i(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^T \mathbf{s}_i(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\)Outer product of scores
\(\hat{\mathbf{I}}_3(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\)\(\mathbf{I}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\)Expected information at MLE

Asymptotic Properties of the MLE

Under regularity conditions, the MLE satisfies:
  • Consistency: \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{mle}} \xrightarrow{p} \boldsymbol{\theta}_0\)
  • Asymptotic Normality: \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{mle}} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \mathbf{I}_1(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)^{-1})\)
  • Efficiency: Achieves the Cramér-Rao bound asymptotically (asymptotically unbiased)
  • Equivariance: For 1-to-1 \(\psi = \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\): \(\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{mle}} = \psi(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{mle}})\)
\[\therefore\quad \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{mle}} \approx \mathcal{N}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0,\; \mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)^{-1}\right) \text{ for large } n\]
⚠ MLEs for finite samples: MLEs have no optimal finite-sample properties. Other estimators may outperform the MLE in small samples. Their attractive properties (consistency, efficiency, normality) are all asymptotic.

Regularity Conditions

  • Identifiability: different \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) → different distributions
  • Range of data cannot depend on unknown parameters
  • True parameter must lie in the interior of the parameter space
  • Number of parameters must not grow too fast with \(n\)

Newton-Raphson Algorithm

\[\boldsymbol{\theta}^{k+1} \approx \boldsymbol{\theta}^k - \mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k)^{-1}\,\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k)\]

Convergence is quadratic near the root. Failure modes include: singular Hessian, poor initial estimate, overshooting in poorly-behaved likelihood surfaces. Fisher's scoring replaces \(-\mathbf{H}\) with its expected value \(\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\).

Surgery Example (Binomial): Surgeon operates \(n=12\) patients with \(y=9\) successes.
Log-likelihood: \(\ell(\pi \mid 9) = \log\binom{12}{9} + 9\log\pi + 3\log(1-\pi)\)
Score equation: \(\frac{d\ell}{d\pi} = \frac{y}{\pi} - \frac{n-y}{1-\pi} = 0 \Rightarrow \hat{\pi} = y/n = 0.75\)
Fisher info: \(I(\pi) = n/[\pi(1-\pi)]\)
Asymptotic 95% CI for \(\pi\): \([0.51,\; 0.99]\)

10. Cramér-Rao Bound

Cramér-Rao Bound (CRB): For any unbiased estimator \(\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{y})\) of \(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\): \[\operatorname{cov}\!\left[\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{y})\right] \ge \mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{-1}\,\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^T\] where \(\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \partial\boldsymbol{\psi}/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}\) is the Jacobian. For the simple case \(\psi(\theta) = \theta\): \[\operatorname{Var}\!\left[\hat{\theta}(\mathbf{y})\right] \ge \mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{-1}\]
  • An estimator attaining the CRB is called fully efficient (= Minimum Variance Unbiased, MVU)
  • The CRB is a lower bound — no unbiased estimator can have lower variance
  • In some problems, no unbiased estimator achieves the bound
  • The MLE achieves the CRB asymptotically
⚠ Key Insight: The CRB establishes a fundamental limit on precision for unbiased estimators. If you insist on unbiasedness, you cannot do better than this bound. But allowing bias (as the MLE does for finite samples) might give better MSE.
