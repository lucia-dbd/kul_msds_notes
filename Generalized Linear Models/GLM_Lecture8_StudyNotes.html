1. The GLM Framework

Linear regression, logistic regression, and Poisson regression share a common structure. The Generalized Linear Model (GLM) framework unifies them under a single formulation consisting of two parts:

Systematic part: The mean $\mu_i = E(y_i)$ is linked to the linear predictor $\eta_i = \mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta$ through a monotone, twice-differentiable response function $h$ (or equivalently its inverse, the link function $g = h^{-1}$): $$\mu_i = h(\eta_i) \qquad \text{or} \qquad \eta_i = \mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta = g(\mu_i)$$ Distribution part: The conditional distribution of $y_i$ belongs to the canonical exponential family: $$f(y_i \mid \theta_i) = \exp\!\left(\frac{y_i\theta_i - b(\theta_i)}{a(\phi, w_i)} + c(y_i, \phi, w_i)\right)$$

A specific GLM is completely determined by the choice of exponential family and link function. The framework unifies computational procedures across all members.

Components of the Canonical Form

Weight conventions with $a(\phi, w_i) = \phi/w_i$: $w_i = 1$ for ungrouped (individual) data; $w_i = 1/n_i$ when the response is a sum of $n_i$ observations; $w_i = n_i$ when the response is the mean of $n_i$ observations.

3. Two Fundamental Identities

By differentiating $\int f(y \mid \theta)\,dy = 1$ with respect to $\theta$, two key relationships are derived:

Identity 1 (score has zero expectation): $$E\!\left(\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\theta}\right) = 0$$ Combined with $\partial\ell/\partial\theta = [y - b'(\theta)]/a(\phi,w)$, this gives: $$\boxed{E(y) = b'(\theta) = \mu}$$
Identity 2 (information = variance of score): $$E\!\left(\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\theta^2}\right) + E\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\theta}\right)^2\right] = 0$$ Combined with $\partial^2\ell/\partial\theta^2 = -b''(\theta)/a(\phi,w)$, this gives: $$\boxed{\text{Var}(y) = b''(\theta)\,a(\phi,w) = V(\mu)\,a(\phi,w)}$$
Canonical link emerges naturally: Since $E(y) = b'(\theta) = \mu$, the function $g_c = b'^{-1}$ maps $\mu \mapsto \theta$. When we set $\eta = g_c(\mu) = \theta$, the link function equates the linear predictor to the natural parameter. This is the canonical link.

Variance function $V(\mu) = b''(\theta)$ encodes how the variance depends on the mean — a key structural feature that differs across GLM families.

Non-canonical links can be used: the probit link for Bernoulli data ($\mu = \Phi(\eta)$), the log link for Gamma data ($\mu = e^\eta$, removing positivity constraints on $\boldsymbol\beta$), the identity link for Poisson ($\mu = \eta$, though this can produce negative fitted means). The choice of link affects convergence properties and interpretation.

Offsets

An offset is a known constant included in the linear predictor without a coefficient to estimate. The classic example is in Poisson rate models: if $y_i \sim \text{Pois}(\mu_i)$ with $\mu_i = n_i\lambda_i$ (where $n_i$ is person-years and $\lambda_i$ is the rate), then:

$\log(\mu_i) = \log(n_i) + \mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta$

Here $\log(n_i)$ is the offset — it shifts the intercept without being estimated. In R: glm(y ~ x + offset(log(n)), family=poisson).

5. MLE in GLMs: Score, Information, and IWLS

For $N$ independent observations from a GLM with $E(y_i|\mathbf{x}_i) = \mu_i = h(\mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta)$, the log-likelihood is:

$\ell(\boldsymbol\beta \mid \mathbf{X}) = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{y_i\theta_i - b(\theta_i)}{a(\phi, w_i)} + c(y_i, \phi, w_i)$
Score vector: $$\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{y} - \boldsymbol\mu)$$ Expected Fisher information matrix: $$\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}$$ where $\mathbf{D} = \text{diag}(h'(\eta_i))$, $\boldsymbol\Sigma = \text{diag}(\sigma_i^2)$ with $\sigma_i^2 = \text{Var}(y_i)$, and $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{D}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{D} = \text{diag}(h'(\eta_i)^2/\sigma_i^2)$.

Iterative Algorithms

AlgorithmUpdate RuleUses
Newton-Raphson$\boldsymbol\beta^{k+1} \approx \boldsymbol\beta^k - \mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol\beta^k)^{-1}\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol\beta^k)$Observed Hessian $\mathbf{H}$

IWLSEquivalent to Fisher scoring; solves a weighted least squares problem at each stepSame as Fisher scoring
Canonical link advantage: For canonical link functions, the expected and observed information matrices coincide: $\mathbf{I}(\theta) = -\mathbf{H}(\theta)$, the log-likelihood is always concave, and the MLE is unique (if it exists). For non-canonical links, multiple maxima are possible.

Asymptotic Distribution of the MLE

For canonical links, if regressors have compact support and $(\mathbf{X}_n^T\mathbf{X}_n)^{-1} \to \mathbf{0}$: $$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_n \sim N\!\left(\boldsymbol\beta,\; (\mathbf{X}_n^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}_n)^{-1}\right)$$ In practice, $\mathbf{W}$ is replaced by $\hat{\mathbf{W}}$ (plugging in $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$). For non-canonical links, additional regularity conditions are needed.
⚠ Convergence caveat: Even when $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is invertible, the weighted information matrix $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}$ may fail to be invertible — for example, in logistic regression with perfect separation. For non-canonical links, convergence to a unique maximum is not guaranteed.

6. Dispersion Parameter Estimation

The dispersion parameter $\phi$ drops out of the score equations — so $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ does not depend on $\phi$. Since ML estimation of $\phi$ is often impractical, it is estimated by the method of moments:

Moment estimator (Pearson-based): $$\hat\phi = \frac{1}{N-p}\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{w_i(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2}{V(\hat\mu_i)}$$ where $p$ is the number of regression coefficients, and $V(\mu)$ is the variance function. This estimator is valid under the assumption that the model is correctly specified and most $n_i > 1$.
Special cases: For the normal model, $\hat\phi = s^2$ (the classical sample variance), even with $n_i = 1$. For Bernoulli and Poisson, $\phi = 1$ (no dispersion parameter). The relationship $\hat\phi = X^2/(N-p)$ connects the moment estimator to the Pearson chi-square statistic.

7. Model Selection and Fit Criteria

Nested Models

Use the Wald, score (Rao), or likelihood ratio test (LRT). The LRT comparing a model with $p_1$ parameters to one with $p_0 < p_1$ parameters uses $D_0 - D_1 \sim \chi^2(p_1 - p_0)$ under $H_0$.

Non-Nested Models: AIC

$\text{AIC} = -2\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) + 2p$

Lower AIC indicates better balance of fit and complexity. AIC cannot be used for quasi-likelihood models (no proper likelihood; R reports AIC: NA for quasi families).

8. The Gamma GLM — Janka Hardness Case Study

The Janka hardness dataset (Williams, 1959) records the hardness and density of 36 timber samples. Key data features: the relationship between density and hardness is clearly curved, and variance increases with density — indicating heteroscedasticity.

Diagnosing the Linear Model's Failures

A quadratic linear model ($\text{Hardness} \sim \text{Density} + \text{Density}^2$) shows significant curvature but the residuals-vs-fitted plot and Q-Q plot reveal increasing variance and right skewness. Formal tests confirm:
NCV score test: $\chi^2 = 14.95$, $p = 0.00011$  |  Breusch-Pagan: $p = 0.036$

Centering Warning

⚠ Collinearity in polynomial regression: Without centering, the intercept and linear term appear non-significant ($p > 0.5$) in the quadratic model even though both are needed. After centering (subtracting the median density), all terms become highly significant and coefficients remain stable when a cubic term is added. This illustrates that significance of lower-order terms should always be assessed in the centered form.

Box-Cox Analysis

The Box-Cox transformation search gives $\hat\lambda \approx 0.47$ (close to $0.5$, i.e., square root) for the linear model, and $\hat\lambda \approx 0.15$ (close to $0$, i.e., log) for the quadratic model. This suggests two competing needs: a square-root transformation for linearity, and a log transformation for variance stabilisation. John Nelder's insight: a Gamma GLM with a square-root link achieves both simultaneously.

Gamma GLM with Square-Root Link

Model: $y_i \sim \text{Gamma}(\nu, \mu_i)$ with $\sqrt{\mu_i} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i$ (centered density), i.e., $\mu_i = (\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i)^2$.
Variance function: $\text{Var}(y_i) = \mu_i^2/\nu \propto \mu_i^2$ — variance grows as the square of the mean (constant coefficient of variation).
R output (Gamma + square-root link, centered density):
Intercept: 33.961 (SE 0.279)  |  Density: 0.768 (SE 0.022)
Dispersion $\hat\phi = 0.00972$; Residual deviance = 0.329 on 34 df; $D^* = 0.329/0.00972 = 33.8 \approx 34 = N-p$ ✓

The Gamma model produces virtually identical fitted values to the quadratic normal model, but provides much more appropriate — and asymmetric — prediction intervals (tighter for low-density, wider for high-density timber).

9. Quasi-Likelihood Models

When the empirical variance does not match the GLM's assumed variance — overdispersion ($\phi > 1$) or underdispersion ($\phi < 1$) — the distribution is misspecified. Quasi-likelihood provides a principled remedy.

Core idea: Instead of deriving $\text{Var}(y_i)$ from a distributional assumption, directly specify a variance function $\text{Var}_q(y_i)$. The quasi-score equations take the same form as the GLM score equations, but with the user-specified variance: $$\sum_i \frac{y_i - \mu_i}{\text{Var}_q(y_i)}\frac{\partial\mu_i}{\partial\beta_j} = 0 \quad \forall j$$ These are also called Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE).

Log Quasi-Likelihood

$q_i(\mu_i) = \int_{y_i}^{\mu_i} \frac{y_i - z}{\text{Var}_q(z)}\,dz + C$

The total quasi-log-likelihood $q(\boldsymbol\mu) = \sum_i q_i(\mu_i)$ behaves like a log-likelihood but requires only the variance function, not a full distributional specification.

Scale Models — The Most Common Case

A scale model sets $\text{Var}_q(y_i) = \phi V(\mu_i)$, where $V(\mu)$ is the variance function from the corresponding GLM. For scale models:
  • $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_q = \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ (same point estimates as the GLM)
  • $\text{sd}(\hat\beta_{qk}) = \sqrt{\phi}\cdot\text{sd}(\hat\beta_k)$
Examples: Quasi-binomial: $\text{Var}_q(\pi_i) = \phi\,\pi_i(1-\pi_i)/n_i$; Quasi-Poisson: $\text{Var}_q(\lambda_i) = \phi\lambda_i$.
⚠ Consequences of ignoring dispersion:
Overdispersion ($\phi > 1$): Standard errors are too small → inflated Type I error rate (spurious significance).
Underdispersion ($\phi < 1$): Standard errors are too large → reduced power (missed true effects).
Low-Iron Teratology Data — overdispersion detected:
Standard binomial model: Residual deviance = 168.91 on 53 df — far exceeding the df, indicating overdispersion.
DHARMa dispersion test: ratioObsSim = 1.077, $p = 0.016$.
Quasi-binomial fit: $\hat\phi = 2.94$ — SEs are inflated by $\sqrt{2.94} \approx 1.71$. Variables significant under the standard binomial become non-significant (e.g., hb: $p$ changes from 0.032 to 0.216).

Mean Structure Requirement

Even in quasi-likelihood models, the mean structure $E(y_i) = \mu_i = h(\mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta)$ must be correctly specified for $E[\mathbf{S}_q(\boldsymbol\beta)] = 0$ and for $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_q$ to be a consistent estimator. Only the variance function can be misspecified (with efficiency loss but still consistency, thanks to the sandwich estimator).

10. The Sandwich (Robust) Estimator

Even if the working variance $\text{Var}_q(y_i)$ is misspecified, the quasi-MLE $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_q$ remains asymptotically normal. The covariance matrix must then be estimated robustly:

Sandwich estimator: $$\hat{\mathbf{A}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{W}}_q\mathbf{X})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{V}}_q(\mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{W}}_q\mathbf{X})^{-1}$$ where the "meat" is: $$\hat{\mathbf{V}}_q = \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\frac{\partial\mu_i}{\partial\boldsymbol\beta}\right)^T \frac{(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2}{V_q^2(\hat\mu_i)}\left(\frac{\partial\mu_i}{\partial\boldsymbol\beta}\right)$$ This is a consistent estimator of the true covariance matrix of $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_q$, even when the working variance is wrong. If the variance is correctly specified, $\hat{\mathbf{A}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{W}}_q\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ (the standard GLM covariance estimator).
Why "sandwich"? The structure $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{V}_q (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ has the bread $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ surrounding the meat $\mathbf{V}_q$. In R, coeftest(model, vcov=sandwich) applies this. The estimator is also called the robust estimator or the Huber-White estimator.
⚠ Efficiency trade-off: Even with the sandwich estimator, efficiency is lost when the working variance is far from the truth. To minimise this loss, one should choose the working variance function as close to the true variance as possible. Consistency is preserved regardless; efficiency is not.

11. Beta-Binomial Model

When binary observations within a cluster are correlated (common in litter experiments, teratology, clustered trials), the standard binomial model underestimates variability. The Beta-Binomial provides a proper distributional alternative.

Hierarchical model: Conditional on $p$, $y_i \sim \text{Bern}(p)$ independently; $p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$. Then the marginal sum $y = \sum y_i$ has: $$E(y) = m\mu, \quad \text{Var}(y) = m\mu(1-\mu)\big[1 + (m-1)\rho\big]$$ where $\mu = \alpha_1/(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)$ and $\rho = 1/(\alpha_1+\alpha_2+1)$ is the intraclass correlation (also the overdispersion parameter).
Key features:
• When $\rho = 0$: reduces to the standard binomial model.
• Variance exceeds the binomial variance by factor $[1+(m-1)\rho]$ — this is the overdispersion.
• Has a closed-form marginal density (in terms of Gamma functions).
• The mean $\mu_i$ can be linked to covariates via any binary link: $\text{logit}(\mu_i) = \mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta$.
• The overdispersion $\rho$ can be modelled as a function of covariates or held constant.
• The beta-binomial is not in the natural exponential family (even for known $\rho$) → all parameters estimated via full ML.
• In R: aod::betabin(cbind(y, n-y) ~ covariates, ~ 1).
Teratology data comparison: The standard binomial model gives SEs that are too small. The quasi-binomial ($\hat\phi = 2.94$) inflates SEs uniformly by $\sqrt{2.94}$. The beta-binomial (homogeneous $\rho$) gives $\hat\rho = 0.236$ and SEs close to the quasi-binomial. The group-varying $\rho$ model shows that overdispersion is concentrated in the placebo group ($\hat\rho = 0.327$), while injected groups have near-zero overdispersion. AIC of group-varying beta-binomial = 195 vs. 198 for homogeneous — modest improvement.

Short Answer 1 · GLM Structure
Describe the two components of a GLM. How does this framework unify linear, logistic, and Poisson regression? What is the role of the canonical parameter $\theta$ and the function $b(\theta)$?
Model Answer:
A GLM has two components. The systematic part describes the mean: $\mu_i = h(\eta_i) = h(\mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta)$, where $h$ is the response function and $g = h^{-1}$ is the link function. The distribution part requires $y_i$ to belong to the canonical exponential family: $f(y_i|\theta_i) = \exp\{[y_i\theta_i - b(\theta_i)]/a(\phi, w_i) + c(y_i, \phi, w_i)\}$.

The framework unifies models by varying the family and link: linear regression uses Normal + identity link; logistic uses Bernoulli + logit link; Poisson regression uses Poisson + log link. All use the same IWLS estimation algorithm.

The canonical parameter $\theta$ is the "natural" parameterisation of the distribution — it is the quantity that appears linearly in the exponent. The function $b(\theta)$ is the log-normalising constant that ensures the density integrates to 1; crucially, its derivatives generate the moments: $b'(\theta) = E(y) = \mu$ and $b''(\theta) = V(\mu)$, the variance function. This makes $b(\theta)$ the central object connecting distribution, mean, and variance in the GLM framework.

Short Answer 2 · Two Fundamental Identities
Derive the mean and variance formulas for the exponential family from the two fundamental identities. What is the variance function and why is it important?
Model Answer:
Starting from $\int f(y|\theta)\,dy = 1$, differentiating once with respect to $\theta$ gives $E(\partial\ell/\partial\theta) = 0$ (Identity 1). Since $\partial\ell/\partial\theta = [y - b'(\theta)]/a(\phi, w)$, Identity 1 yields: $$\frac{\mu - b'(\theta)}{a(\phi,w)} = 0 \implies \boxed{E(y) = \mu = b'(\theta)}$$ Differentiating twice gives $E(\partial^2\ell/\partial\theta^2) + E[(\partial\ell/\partial\theta)^2] = 0$ (Identity 2). Since $\partial^2\ell/\partial\theta^2 = -b''(\theta)/a(\phi,w)$: $$-\frac{b''(\theta)}{a(\phi,w)} + \frac{\text{Var}(y)}{a(\phi,w)^2} = 0 \implies \boxed{\text{Var}(y) = b''(\theta)\cdot a(\phi,w)}$$ The variance function $V(\mu) = b''(\theta)$ captures how variance depends on the mean — a structural feature distinguishing GLM families. For Normal: $V(\mu)=1$ (constant variance); Poisson: $V(\mu)=\mu$ (variance equals mean); Binomial: $V(\mu)=\mu(1-\mu)$; Gamma: $V(\mu)=\mu^2$ (constant CV). Choosing a model involves choosing the right $V(\mu)$.

Short Answer 3 · MLE and IWLS
Explain how the MLE for GLM regression coefficients is computed via IWLS. What are the advantages of canonical links for estimation? What can prevent convergence?
Model Answer:
The GLM log-likelihood gives score $\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol\mu)$ and information $\mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}$ where $\mathbf{W} = \text{diag}(h'(\eta_i)^2/\sigma_i^2)$. Fisher scoring updates: $\boldsymbol\beta^{k+1} \approx \boldsymbol\beta^k + (\mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})$. This is identical to solving a weighted least squares problem at each step — the "working response" is $\mathbf{z} = \hat{\boldsymbol\eta} + \hat{\mathbf{D}}(\mathbf{y}-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})\cdot\hat\sigma_i^{-2}$.

Canonical link advantages: (1) Expected and observed information coincide, so Fisher scoring equals Newton-Raphson exactly. (2) The log-likelihood is globally concave, guaranteeing the MLE is unique whenever $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}$ is invertible. (3) No risk of multiple local maxima.

Convergence failures: Even when $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is invertible, $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}$ may not be — e.g., perfect separation in logistic regression drives $\hat\pi \to 0$ or $1$, making $w_i = \hat\pi_i(1-\hat\pi_i) \to 0$ for some observations. For non-canonical links, multiple maxima are possible.

Short Answer 4 · Dispersion Parameter
Why is the dispersion parameter $\phi$ not estimated via maximum likelihood in most GLMs? How is it estimated, and how does this estimation connect to model fit?
Model Answer:
In the score equations, $\phi$ appears as a common factor: $\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol\beta) = (1/\phi)\sum_i w_i(y_i-\mu_i)/V(\mu_i)\cdot\partial\mu_i/\partial\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{0}$. Since $\phi > 0$ is a scalar, it cancels from the score equations entirely. Consequently, $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ does not depend on $\phi$ — only the standard errors and test statistics do.

ML estimation of $\phi$ is often impractical due to boundary issues (e.g., the log-likelihood can be unbounded as $\phi \to 0$ for some families). Instead, the method of moments is used: $$\hat\phi = \frac{X^2}{N-p} = \frac{1}{N-p}\sum_i \frac{w_i(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2}{V(\hat\mu_i)}$$ This connects directly to model fit: $X^2/\phi \sim \chi^2(N-p)$ under the correct model, so $E(X^2) = (N-p)\phi$ and the moment estimator is unbiased. For the normal model, $\hat\phi = s^2$ exactly. Large $\hat\phi > 1$ signals overdispersion; small $\hat\phi < 1$ signals underdispersion.

Short Answer 5 · Gamma GLM (Janka)
Walk through the statistical reasoning that led to choosing a Gamma GLM with a square-root link for the Janka hardness data. What does this model assume about the mean-variance relationship, and how does it improve upon ordinary quadratic regression?
Model Answer:
The Janka data exhibit two problems: (1) curvature — a linear model of density is inadequate; (2) heteroscedasticity — variance grows with density. A quadratic linear model addresses (1) but not (2): residuals show a clear fan shape and the Breusch-Pagan test rejects constant variance ($p = 0.036$).

Box-Cox analysis gives the clue: $\hat\lambda \approx 0.47$ (near $0.5$) for the linear model suggests a square-root transformation to linearise; $\hat\lambda \approx 0.15$ (near $0$) for the quadratic model suggests a log transformation to stabilise variance. These two needs conflict in a standard transformation approach.

John Nelder's insight: a Gamma GLM with a square-root link achieves both simultaneously. The model is $\sqrt{\mu_i} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i$, or equivalently $\mu_i = (\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i)^2$ — making $\mu$ quadratic in density while keeping the linear predictor linear. The Gamma distribution's variance function $V(\mu) = \mu^2$ implies $\text{Var}(y) \propto \mu^2$, exactly the mean-proportional variance observed. The estimated $D^* \approx 33.8 \approx 34 = N-p$ confirms good fit.

Advantages over quadratic regression: (1) appropriate heteroscedastic prediction intervals (tighter for low-density, wider for high-density timber); (2) principled model fit assessment via scaled deviance; (3) no violation of normality assumptions at extreme density values.

Short Answer 6 · Quasi-Likelihood
Explain the quasi-likelihood approach. What is a scale model? What are the consequences of overdispersion for inference, and how does quasi-likelihood address them?
Model Answer:
Quasi-likelihood directly specifies a variance function $\text{Var}_q(y_i)$ without assuming a particular distribution. The quasi-score equations $\sum_i (y_i-\mu_i)/\text{Var}_q(y_i)\cdot\partial\mu_i/\partial\beta_j = 0$ (GEE) have the same form as GLM score equations. The quasi-log-likelihood $q_i(\mu_i) = \int_{y_i}^{\mu_i}(y_i-z)/\text{Var}_q(z)\,dz$ behaves like a log-likelihood but is not in general a proper likelihood.

A scale model sets $\text{Var}_q(y_i) = \phi V(\mu_i)$ — inflating the GLM variance by a dispersion factor $\phi$. For scale models, $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_q = \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ (point estimates unchanged) but $\text{sd}(\hat\beta_{qk}) = \sqrt{\phi}\cdot\text{sd}(\hat\beta_k)$.

Overdispersion consequences: If $\phi > 1$ and we ignore it (fit standard Poisson/binomial), SEs are underestimated by a factor $\sqrt{\phi}$. z-statistics are inflated, p-values are too small — inflated Type I error. In the teratology example, $\hat\phi = 2.94$: the hemoglobin variable `hb` is "significant" in the standard binomial ($p=0.032$) but non-significant in the quasi-binomial ($p=0.216$) after proper SE inflation.

Short Answer 7 · Sandwich Estimator
Explain the sandwich estimator of the covariance matrix. When is it needed, what does it provide, and what is its cost?
Model Answer:
When the working variance $\text{Var}_q(y_i)$ in a quasi-likelihood model may be misspecified, the naive covariance estimate $(\mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{W}}_q\mathbf{X})^{-1}$ is inconsistent for the true covariance of $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_q$. The sandwich estimator corrects this: $$\hat{\mathbf{A}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{W}}_q\mathbf{X})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{V}}_q(\mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{W}}_q\mathbf{X})^{-1}$$ where the "meat" $\hat{\mathbf{V}}_q = \sum_i (\partial\hat\mu_i/\partial\boldsymbol\beta)(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2/V_q^2(\hat\mu_i)(\partial\hat\mu_i/\partial\boldsymbol\beta)^T$ uses the actual squared residuals.

When needed: Whenever we suspect the working variance structure may be misspecified (e.g., independence assumed but responses are clustered). In R: coeftest(model, vcov=sandwich).

What it provides: Consistency — it converges to the true covariance matrix as $n \to \infty$ regardless of the working variance choice (provided the mean structure is correctly specified).

Cost: Efficiency is lost when the working variance is far from the true variance. If the working variance is correct, the sandwich estimator reduces to $(\mathbf{X}^T\hat{\mathbf{W}}_q\mathbf{X})^{-1}$, so there is no harm in using it when uncertain.

Short Answer 8 · Beta-Binomial
Describe the hierarchical construction of the beta-binomial model. How does it model overdispersion, and what is the role of $\rho$ (the intraclass correlation)?
Model Answer:
The beta-binomial arises from a two-stage hierarchical model. At the cluster level, the success probability $p$ is not fixed but random, following $p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$. Conditional on $p$, the $m$ binary observations in the cluster are independent Bernoulli($p$). Marginalising over $p$: $$E(y_i) = \mu, \quad \text{Var}(y_i) = \mu(1-\mu), \quad \text{corr}(y_i, y_j) = \rho$$ where $\mu = \alpha_1/(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)$ and $\rho = 1/(\alpha_1+\alpha_2+1)$.

For the cluster sum $y = \sum y_i$: $\text{Var}(y) = m\mu(1-\mu)[1+(m-1)\rho]$. This exceeds the binomial variance $m\mu(1-\mu)$ by the factor $[1+(m-1)\rho]$ — the overdispersion caused by within-cluster correlation. When $\rho = 0$, the model reduces to the binomial.

The beta-binomial is not in the natural exponential family, so standard IWLS doesn't apply — all parameters ($\boldsymbol\beta$ for the mean, $\rho$ for the overdispersion) are estimated by full ML. One can also model $\rho$ as a function of covariates (group-varying $\phi$). In the teratology example, the placebo group shows much higher overdispersion ($\hat\rho = 0.327$) than injected groups — suggesting the litter effect is strongest without iron supplementation.

Short Answer 9 · Canonical vs. Non-Canonical Links
Compare the statistical and practical differences between using a canonical link versus a non-canonical link in a GLM. Give examples where a non-canonical link is preferred.
Model Answer:
The canonical link sets $\eta_i = g_c(\mu_i) = \theta_i$ (the natural parameter). This has mathematical advantages: the log-likelihood is globally concave (unique MLE), expected and observed information coincide (Newton-Raphson = Fisher scoring), and sufficient statistics have a simple form. Examples: logit for Binomial, log for Poisson, identity for Normal, inverse for Gamma.

Non-canonical links sacrifice these mathematical conveniences but may be substantively better: (1) Probit link for Binomial: arises from a latent normal variable model; more natural in some psychometric and econometric contexts. (2) Log link for Gamma: $\mu_i = e^{\mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol\beta}$ removes all constraints on $\boldsymbol\beta$ (the canonical inverse link requires $\boldsymbol\beta$ to give positive $-1/\mu_i$, which can be violated during fitting). (3) Square-root link for Gamma (Janka example): makes the linear predictor linear in density, matching the physical relationship, while the Gamma variance function handles heteroscedasticity. (4) Complementary log-log for Binomial: appropriate when the probability of the event approaches 1 sharply (survival data, extreme value models).

The key practical consideration: with non-canonical links, convergence may be slower and multiple maxima are possible. Canonical links offer robustness during fitting; non-canonical links offer subject-matter appropriateness.

Short Answer 10 · Critical Thinking
Critically evaluate the following statement: "If all four methods — standard binomial, binomial with sandwich, quasi-binomial, and beta-binomial — give the same point estimates for $\boldsymbol\beta$, they are essentially equivalent and the choice between them doesn't matter."
Model Answer:
The statement is fundamentally incorrect. While the point estimates $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ are identical across all four methods for scale models (the score equations have the same solution), the methods differ critically in several dimensions:

1. Standard errors and inference: The standard binomial SEs are too small when overdispersion is present ($\phi > 1$), inflating Type I error. The quasi-binomial, sandwich, and beta-binomial give larger, more appropriate SEs — potentially changing which predictors appear significant (as seen with `hb` in the teratology example).

2. Modelling assumptions and testability: The beta-binomial makes explicit distributional assumptions (Beta-distributed random probability) that can be tested and interpreted. It provides a proper likelihood — enabling LRTs to compare overdispersion models and to test whether $\rho$ varies by group. The quasi-likelihood approach makes no such distributional claim and cannot use likelihood-based model comparison.

3. Model selection: AIC is available for the binomial and beta-binomial (proper likelihood), not for quasi-likelihood. This matters when choosing between competing models.

4. Interpretation of overdispersion: The beta-binomial's $\rho$ has a clear mechanistic interpretation (within-cluster correlation due to a random effect). The quasi-binomial's $\phi$ is a phenomenological correction without mechanistic content. For scientific inference about the data-generating process, the beta-binomial provides richer information.

5. Finite-sample performance: The sandwich estimator is asymptotically consistent but can be biased in small samples. The quasi-likelihood estimator is always unbiased for $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}$ when the mean is correctly specified; the beta-binomial ML estimator converges to the true parameters. In small samples, the choice of method matters for the reliability of the SEs.

In summary, identical point estimates mask important differences in the validity of inference, the richness of the model, and the scope for model comparison.
