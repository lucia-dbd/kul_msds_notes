Overview & General Principle

Nonparametric hypothesis testing in survival analysis addresses two broad questions: (1) does a single censored sample arise from a population with a specified hazard function \(h_0(t)\), and (2) do two or more groups share the same hazard function? All tests share a unified structure built on weighted observed-minus-expected statistics.

Two goals of this chapter: testing the hazard of one population against a known \(h_0(t)\), and comparing hazard functions across two or more populations without assuming any parametric family.

The Common Architecture

At each ordered event time \(Y_{(j)}\) (\(j=1,\ldots,r\)) in the (pooled) sample, we measure the discrepancy between what was observed and what would be expected under \(H_0\). A non-negative weight \(w(Y_{(j)})\) scales each contribution. The test statistic is the weighted sum of discrepancies, standardised by its standard deviation.

Critical rule: The weight function must be chosen a priori, before inspecting the data. Choosing the weight post-hoc to minimise the p-value invalidates the test.

One-Sample Tests

Hypotheses

Given a censored sample of size \(n\), test whether the data are consistent with a known hazard function \(h_0(t)\):

\[ H_0: h(t)=h_0(t)\;\forall t\leq Y_{(r)}, \qquad H_1: h(t)\neq h_0(t)\text{ for some }t \]

General Statistic

\[ U = \sum_j w(Y_{(j)})\frac{d_{(j)}}{r_{(j)}} - \int_0^{Y_{(r)}} w(s)\,h_0(s)\,ds, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(U)=\int_0^{Y_{(r)}}\frac{w^2(s)\,h_0(s)}{r(s)}\,ds \]

For large \(n\): \(Z=U/\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(U)}\approx N(0,1)\).

One-Sample Log-Rank Test

Weight function: \(w(t)=r(t)\) (number currently at risk).

With this choice the integral telescopes via \(\int_0^{Y_{(r)}}r(s)\,h_0(s)\,ds=\sum_{i=1}^n H_0(Y_i)\):

\[ U = \underbrace{\sum_j d_{(j)}}_{O} - \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n H_0(Y_i)}_{E} = O - E \]
Key simplification: When \(w(t)=r(t)\), \(\operatorname{Var}(U)=\sum_{i=1}^n H_0(Y_i)=E\). Therefore: \[ Z = \frac{O-E}{\sqrt{E}} \approx N(0,1) \quad\text{under }H_0. \]
# One-sample log-rank: H₀ is Exp(mean=1000), so H₀(t) = t/1000 sp <- exp(-0.001 * schizo$Time) survdiff(Surv(Time, Censor) ~ offset(sp), data = schizo)

Worked example — Schizophrenia data (\(H_0:\text{Exp}(\mu=1000)\))

Two-Sample Tests

Hypotheses

\[ H_0: h_1(t)=h_2(t)\;\forall t, \qquad H_1: h_1(t)\neq h_2(t)\text{ for some }t \]

The 2×2 Table at Each Event Time

GroupEventsNo eventAt risk
1\(d_{(j)1}\)\(r_{(j)1}-d_{(j)1}\)\(r_{(j)1}\)
2\(d_{(j)2}\)\(r_{(j)2}-d_{(j)2}\)\(r_{(j)2}\)
Total\(d_{(j)}\)\(r_{(j)}-d_{(j)}\)\(r_{(j)}\)

General Weighted Statistic

\[ U = \sum_j w(Y_{(j)})\!\left[d_{(j)1} - d_{(j)}\frac{r_{(j)1}}{r_{(j)}}\right] = \sum_j w(Y_{(j)})(O_j - E_j) \]
Variance via hypergeometric: Conditional on \(d_{(j)},r_{(j)1},r_{(j)}\), the count \(d_{(j)1}\sim\text{Hypergeometric}\) under \(H_0\): \[ \operatorname{Var}(U)=\sum_j w^2(Y_{(j)})\cdot d_{(j)}\cdot\frac{r_{(j)1}}{r_{(j)}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{r_{(j)1}}{r_{(j)}}\right)\cdot\frac{r_{(j)}-d_{(j)}}{r_{(j)}-1} \] Under \(H_0\): \(Z=U/\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(U)}\approx N(0,1)\), or \(Z^2\sim\chi^2_1\).

Worked Example — Gender Comparison

# Log-rank (rho=0, default) survdiff(Surv(Time, Censor) ~ Gender, data = schizo) N Observed Expected (O-E)^2/E (O-E)^2/V Gender=1 187 101 123.1 3.97 16.8 Gender=2 93 62 39.9 12.26 16.8 Chisq= 16.8 on 1 degrees of freedom, p= 4e-05
Conclusion: Reject \(H_0\) (\(p=4\times10^{-5}\)). Males (Gender=1): \(O=101 < E=123.1\) — better survival than females.
Reading R output: (O-E)²/V uses the full hypergeometric variance; (O-E)²/E uses only the marginal approximation. For two groups, both (O-E)²/V values equal Chisq. For \(l>2\), the (O-E)²/V values do not sum to Chisq.

Weight Functions in Detail

Log-Rank — \(w(Y_{(j)})=1\)

All event times receive equal weight. This is the score test from the Cox proportional hazards model and is locally most powerful when \(h_1(t)/h_2(t)=\text{const}\). In R: survdiff(..., rho=0) (default).

Gehan–Wilcoxon — \(w(Y_{(j)})=r_{(j)}\)

Generalises the two-sample Wilcoxon rank-sum test to censored data. The risk set \(r_{(j)}\) is largest at the start, giving more weight to early differences. Drawback: depends on the censoring distribution, complicating cross-study comparisons.

Tarone–Ware — \(w(Y_{(j)})=\sqrt{r_{(j)}}\)

Compromise between log-rank and Gehan: emphasises early departures but less aggressively.

Peto–Peto — \(w(Y_{(j)})=\hat{S}_{\text{mod}}(Y_{(j)})\)

\[ \hat{S}_{\text{mod}}(Y_{(j)})=\prod_{k:\,Y_{(k)}\leq Y_{(j)}}\!\left(1-\frac{d_{(k)}}{r_{(k)}+1}\right) \]

The denominator \(r_{(k)}+1\) (not \(r_{(k)}\)) gives better weight properties and reduces sensitivity to censoring. In R: survdiff(..., rho=1).

Fleming–Harrington \(G(p,q)\)

\[ w(Y_{(j)})=\hat{S}(Y_{(j-1)})^p\,(1-\hat{S}(Y_{(j-1)}))^q \]
\(p\)\(q\)EmphasisNote
00EqualLog-rank
\(>0\)0Early\(p=1\): Peto–Peto
0\(>0\)LateUseful when effects build over time
\(=q>0\)\(=p>0\)MiddleMax at \(\hat{S}=0.5\)
Intuition for \(G(0,q)\): Early, \(\hat{S}\approx1\) so \((1-\hat{S})^q\approx0\) — near-zero weight. Late, \(\hat{S}\) is small so \((1-\hat{S})^q\approx1\) — large weight. Perfectly targets late differences (e.g. delayed immunotherapy effects).
Decision guide (pre-specify):
• Proportional hazards expected → log-rank.
• Treatment acts quickly → Gehan or \(G(p>0,0)\).
• Effects build over time (immunotherapy, vaccine) → \(G(0,q>0)\).
• No prior knowledge → log-rank (standard default).

K-Sample Test and Test for Trend

Comparing \(l\) Populations

\[ H_0: h_1(t)=\cdots=h_l(t)\;\forall t \]

For group \(k\): \(U_k=\sum_j w(Y_{(j)})[d_{(j)k}-d_{(j)}r_{(j)k}/r_{(j)}]\).

Linear dependence: \(\sum_{k=1}^l U_k=0\) exactly. The \(l\times l\) covariance matrix is singular. Work with \(\mathbf{U}=(U_1,\ldots,U_{l-1})^\top\): \[ \mathbf{U}^\top\,\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathbf{U})^{-1}\,\mathbf{U}\approx\chi^2_{l-1}\quad\text{under }H_0. \]

Worked example — Marital status (\(l=3\))

survdiff(Surv(Time, Censor) ~ Marital, data = schizo) N Observed Expected (O-E)^2/E (O-E)^2/V Marital=1 105 55 67.0 2.14 3.71 Marital=2 72 37 55.1 5.93 9.24 Marital=3 103 71 41.0 22.00 30.69 Chisq= 31.4 on 2 degrees of freedom, p= 1e-07
Conclusion: \(p=10^{-7}\) — reject \(H_0\). Marital=3 (alone): \(O=71\gg E=41\) — worst survival. Note: the individual (O-E)²/V values (3.71 + 9.24 + 30.69 = 43.6 ≠ 31.4) do not sum to Chisq when \(l>2\) because off-diagonal covariances are ignored.

Test for Trend

When \(l\) groups have a natural ordering, a trend test with linear scores \(c_k=k\) exploits that ordering:

\[ U=\sum_{k=1}^l c_k U_k,\quad Z=\frac{U}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(U)}}\approx N(0,1)\quad\text{(one-sided)} \]

This is a 1-df test, more powerful than the \((l-1)\)-df omnibus test when the true alternative is monotone.

Power trade-off: The trend test gains power for monotone alternatives at the cost of losing power for non-monotone alternatives (e.g. U-shaped or crossing hazards).

Stratified Tests and Matched Pairs

Motivation

If variable \(B\) is both unequally distributed across treatment groups and independently associated with survival, it confounds the comparison. A stratified test controls for \(B\) by computing O and E within each stratum, then combining.

Stratified Statistic

\[ U_{k\bullet}=\sum_{b=1}^m U_{kb},\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(U_{k\bullet},U_{k'\bullet})=\sum_{b=1}^m \operatorname{Cov}(U_{kb},U_{k'b}) \]
Core principle: O and E are computed using only within-stratum risk sets and event times, then summed across strata. This removes between-stratum confounding.
survdiff(Surv(Time, Censor) ~ Group + strata(B), data = mydata)

Worked example — Gender stratified by marital status

Unstratified:
Chisq = 16.8, \(p=4\times10^{-5}\)
Stratified by marital:
Chisq = 10.8, \(p=0.001\)

After stratification, chi-squared drops from 16.8 to 10.8 — part of the gender effect was confounded by marital status. A significant residual gender effect remains (\(p=0.001\)).

Matched Pairs Test

Special case: \(m\) strata each of size 2. Let \(D_j\) = number of pairs where the subject from group \(j\) dies first.

\[ Z=\frac{D_1-D_2}{\sqrt{D_1+D_2}}\approx N(0,1) \]
Remarkable result: Regardless of the weight function chosen, the matched pairs statistic always reduces to \(Z=(D_1-D_2)/\sqrt{D_1+D_2}\). The weight cancels algebraically because within-stratum risk sets of size 2 reduce to ±½ per informative pair.
Uninformative pairs: Pairs where neither subject has yet experienced the event (no ordering can be established) contribute nothing to \(D_1\) or \(D_2\) and are ignored.

✏️ Short Answer Questions

Short Answer 1 · One-Sample Log-Rank
Starting from the general weighted statistic, show step-by-step how choosing \(w(t)=r(t)\) leads to \(U=O-E\) where \(E=\sum_i H_0(Y_i)\), and why \(\operatorname{Var}(U)=E\).
Step 1 — Observed term. With \(w(t)=r(t)\), the sum \(\sum_j r_{(j)}\cdot d_{(j)}/r_{(j)}=\sum_j d_{(j)}=O\).

Step 2 — Expected term. \(r(s)=\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf{1}\{Y_i\geq s\}\), so by Fubini: \[\int_0^{Y_{(r)}}r(s)\,h_0(s)\,ds=\sum_i\int_0^{Y_i}h_0(s)\,ds=\sum_i H_0(Y_i)=E.\] Each subject \(i\) is in the risk set exactly on \([0,Y_i]\), contributing \(H_0(Y_i)\).

Step 3 — Simplification. \(U=O-E\).

Variance. \(\operatorname{Var}(U)=\int r^2(s)\,h_0(s)/r(s)\,ds=\int r(s)\,h_0(s)\,ds=E\). Therefore \(Z=(O-E)/\sqrt{E}\sim N(0,1)\) — elegantly analogous to a one-sample chi-squared test, and trivially computed from any parametric \(H_0\).
Short Answer 2 · Two-Sample Tests
Explain why \(d_{(j)1}\) follows a hypergeometric (not binomial) distribution under \(H_0\). Identify the finite-population correction (FPC) factor and discuss what happens to it when \(d_{(j)}\ll r_{(j)}\).
Hypergeometric vs Binomial. Under \(H_0\), the \(d_{(j)}\) failures at time \(Y_{(j)}\) are distributed among the \(r_{(j)}\) at-risk individuals without replacement (each subject can die at most once). Sampling without replacement from a finite population of size \(r_{(j)}\) is precisely the hypergeometric setup. If failures were independent Bernoulli trials (with replacement), the binomial would apply.

Variance and FPC. \[\operatorname{Var}(d_{(j)1})=d_{(j)}\cdot\frac{r_{(j)1}}{r_{(j)}}\cdot\frac{r_{(j)2}}{r_{(j)}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{r_{(j)}-d_{(j)}}{r_{(j)}-1}}_{\text{FPC}}\] The FPC \((r_{(j)}-d_{(j)})/(r_{(j)}-1)<1\) reduces the variance below the binomial value, reflecting the constraint that total deaths are fixed at \(d_{(j)}\).

When \(d_{(j)}\ll r_{(j)}\). FPC \(\approx(r_{(j)}-0)/(r_{(j)}-1)\approx1\), so the hypergeometric variance approaches the binomial variance. In typical survival data at any given event time, this approximation is very accurate.
Short Answer 3 · Weight Functions
Compare the log-rank and Gehan–Wilcoxon tests in terms of (i) optimal alternatives, (ii) sensitivity to the censoring distribution, and (iii) practical recommendations. Give a concrete clinical example where each is preferred.
(i) Optimal alternatives. The log-rank (\(w=1\)) is the score test from the Cox PH model and is locally most powerful when \(h_1(t)/h_2(t)=\text{const}\). The Gehan–Wilcoxon (\(w=r_{(j)}\)) is more powerful when groups differ predominantly early — a large, decreasing hazard ratio over time.

(ii) Censoring sensitivity. The log-rank weight (constant 1) is completely unaffected by censoring. The Gehan weight equals the risk set size, which is directly shaped by the censoring pattern. Consequently, Gehan statistics from studies with different censoring mechanisms are not directly comparable — a problem for meta-analysis.

(iii) Examples. Log-rank: comparing overall survival between two chemotherapy arms in an RCT, where a persistent proportional treatment effect is expected throughout follow-up. Gehan: comparing post-operative wound infection rates between two antibiotic prophylaxis protocols, where any protective benefit is expected to manifest immediately and fade quickly — early differences dominate.
Short Answer 4 · K-Sample Test
In the marital status analysis (3 groups), Chisq = 31.4 on df = 2. Explain why the three individual (O-E)²/V values (3.71, 9.24, 30.69) do not sum to 31.4, while for the two-group gender analysis both (O-E)²/V values equal 16.8.
Why they don't sum (3 groups). The omnibus statistic \(\mathbf{U}^\top\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{U}\) uses the full \(2\times2\) covariance matrix, including off-diagonal terms \(\operatorname{Cov}(U_1,U_2)\neq0\). The quantity (O-E)²/V for group \(k\) uses only the diagonal variance \(V_{kk}\), ignoring cross-covariances. Summing all three ignores these interactions, so the sum (43.6) exceeds the correct statistic (31.4). R reports (O-E)²/V as a rough diagnostic for which group drives the discrepancy, not as additive components.

Why they are equal (2 groups). With \(l=2\), the linear constraint \(U_1+U_2=0\) forces \(U_2=-U_1\) and \(\Sigma_{11}=\Sigma_{22}\). The scalar quadratic form becomes \(U_1^2/\Sigma_{11}=U_2^2/\Sigma_{22}=\text{Chisq}\). Both groups yield the same (O-E)²/V, each equal to Chisq, with no off-diagonal terms to account for in the \(1\times1\) case.
Short Answer 5 · Stratified Tests
The unstratified log-rank for gender gives Chisq = 16.8 (\(p=4\times10^{-5}\)); stratifying by marital status gives Chisq = 10.8 (\(p=0.001\)). Explain the reduction and assess whether the gender effect is "real".
Why the statistic decreased. Marital status is both (a) unequally distributed between genders and (b) independently predictive of survival (its own Chisq = 31.4). It therefore confounds the unstratified gender comparison. By stratifying, we compute O and E within each marital stratum using only within-stratum risk sets. This holds marital status constant, removing between-stratum confounding. The drop from 16.8 to 10.8 (Δ = 6.0 Chisq units) represents the portion of the apparent gender effect attributable to the gender–marital-status association.

Is the gender effect real? Yes — Chisq = 10.8, \(p=0.001\) remains highly significant after stratification. The stratified result gives a cleaner estimate of the true within-marital-status gender effect. Males still have O < E within strata, indicating genuinely better survival independent of marital status. A Cox model including both covariates would confirm and quantify this.
Short Answer 6 · Weight Functions — Censoring Dependence
Explain in detail why the Gehan–Wilcoxon test's dependence on the censoring distribution is a practical problem. How does the Peto–Peto modification address this, and what residual limitation remains?
The problem with Gehan's weight. \(w(Y_{(j)})=r_{(j)}\) is the number at risk at time \(Y_{(j)}\). This is determined not only by the event times but also by the censoring times — if censoring is heavy or occurs early, \(r_{(j)}\) drops rapidly, reducing weight on intermediate event times. Two studies comparing the same two treatments but with different follow-up protocols will produce different \(r_{(j)}\) sequences and thus different test statistics, making Gehan statistics incomparable across trials and problematic for meta-analysis.

Peto–Peto solution. Replace \(r_{(j)}\) with \(\hat{S}_\text{mod}(Y_{(j)})\), an estimate of the common survival function under \(H_0\). This weight decreases smoothly from 1 to 0 following the estimated survival curve, independently of the specific censoring pattern in any given study. Two studies with the same true survival functions but different censoring will produce similar \(\hat{S}_\text{mod}\) sequences, giving more comparable statistics.

Residual limitation. \(\hat{S}_\text{mod}\) is still estimated from data and so is not entirely censoring-free — heavily censored data will produce a noisier \(\hat{S}_\text{mod}\). Moreover, the Peto–Peto weight still emphasises early event times (since \(\hat{S}_\text{mod}\) is largest early), so it retains the early-emphasis characteristic of Gehan.
Short Answer 7 · Trend Test vs Omnibus
Explain the rationale for using linear scores in the trend test. Under what circumstances do you prefer the trend test over the omnibus \(\chi^2_{l-1}\) test, and when does the omnibus test have the advantage?
Rationale for linear scores. If the groups are ordered (dose levels, age categories, disease severity stages), the scientifically natural alternative hypothesis is monotone: hazard increases or decreases in order of the groups. Assigning scores \(c_k=k\) (or any strictly monotone sequence) and forming \(U=\sum_k c_k U_k\) produces a single linear combination of the group-specific statistics. This concentrates all 1 df on the direction of the expected trend, rather than spreading df across all possible group patterns.

When to prefer the trend test. Use the 1-df trend test when the groups have a meaningful natural order and the scientific hypothesis is that the response is monotone in that order (e.g. increasing radiation dose → monotonically increasing tumour suppression; higher education → monotonically better survival). The 1-df test can be substantially more powerful than the \((l-1)\)-df omnibus test when the truth is indeed monotone.

When the omnibus test has the advantage. When there is no natural ordering, or when the true alternative may be non-monotone (e.g. a U-shaped dose–response where moderate doses are beneficial but very high doses are harmful, or when survival curves cross between strata), the omnibus \(\chi^2_{l-1}\) test detects arbitrary patterns. Using the trend test in such cases would "miss" the non-monotone signal and lose power relative to the omnibus. The choice must be pre-specified based on the scientific question.
Short Answer 8 · Pre-specified Analysis Strategy
Design a pre-specified hypothesis testing strategy for a Phase III trial comparing a standard chemotherapy (Group 1) versus a checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapy (Group 2) in advanced non-small cell lung cancer. The follow-up is 3 years. Biological rationale suggests the immunotherapy will show no early benefit (0–6 months) but increasing benefit thereafter. Justify your weight function choice, state your \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) precisely, and discuss power implications.
Hypotheses. \[H_0: h_1(t)=h_2(t)\;\forall t\leq 3\text{ years}\] \[H_1: h_2(t)
Weight function choice: Fleming–Harrington \(G(0,1)\). The weight \(w(Y_{(j)})=1-\hat{S}(Y_{(j-1)})\) is near zero early (when \(\hat{S}\approx1\)) and increases as the survival function falls — exactly aligned with the biological expectation. Pre-registering \(G(0,1)\) in the Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) before database lock is mandatory.

Power implications. Compared to the log-rank: the chosen weight will have substantially higher power if the delayed-effect assumption is correct, because it does not waste df on the early "null" period. Conversely, if the immunotherapy actually acts immediately (unexpectedly), the \(G(0,1)\) test will have lower power than the log-rank. This trade-off is unavoidable — optimal power is always conditional on the assumed alternative. Sensitivity analyses using both log-rank (\(G(0,0)\)) and \(G(0,1)\) should be pre-specified as secondary analyses, with the primary analysis based on the pre-specified \(G(0,1)\) only.
