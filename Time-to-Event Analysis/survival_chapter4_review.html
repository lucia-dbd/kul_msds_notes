📈 Chapter 4 — Proportional Hazards Models

Survival Analysis  ·  Cox Regression  ·  Partial Likelihood  ·  Model Checking  ·  Exam Preparation

Introduction — Why Move Beyond Stratification?

The Cox (1972) proportional hazards model is the most widely used regression model in survival analysis, cited over 18,000 times. It addresses fundamental shortcomings of stratified nonparametric tests.

Three Problems with Stratified Tests as a Covariate Adjustment Strategy

  • (1) Adjusting for many covariates requires exponentially many strata — quickly becoming infeasible.
  • (2) The stratified test yields no estimate or inference for the stratification variable itself.
  • (3) Continuous covariates must be arbitrarily categorised, losing information and introducing cut-point sensitivity.

The Semiparametric Advantage

The Cox model solves all three problems simultaneously. It is semiparametric: the baseline hazard \(h_0(t)\) is left completely unspecified (infinite-dimensional nuisance), while the covariate effects \(\beta\) are parametric and estimable without specifying \(h_0\). This is a major practical advantage — we get valid, efficient inference about relative rates without any distributional assumption about the shape of the hazard over time.

Critical distinction: The Cox model makes no parametric assumption about \(h_0(t)\) but does assume that the ratio of hazards for any two subjects is constant over time. Violating the PH assumption is more serious than misspecifying the baseline shape.

The Proportional Hazards Model

Two-Group Formulation

In the simplest case (Treated vs. Control), the PH model states:

\[ h_T(t) = \psi \, h_C(t), \quad \psi = \frac{h_T(t)}{h_C(t)} = \text{const for all } t \]

If \(\psi < 1\): treatment reduces hazard. If \(\psi > 1\): treatment increases hazard. Under PH, the two survival curves can never cross.

General Multivariate Model

For subject \(i\) with covariate vector \(x_i=(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{ip})^\top\):

\[ h_i(t) = h_0(t)\exp(\beta^\top x_i) = h_0(t)\exp(\beta_1 x_{i1}+\cdots+\beta_p x_{ip}) \]
TermMeaning
\(h_0(t)\)Baseline hazard: hazard when all \(x_{ij}=0\). Completely unspecified.
\(\beta=(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_p)^\top\)\(p\times1\) vector of log-hazard-ratio parameters to be estimated
\(\exp(\beta^\top x_i)\)Relative risk (HR) for subject \(i\) vs. baseline subject

The PH Condition

For any two subjects \(i\) and \(j\): \[ \frac{h_i(t)}{h_j(t)} = \frac{\exp(\beta^\top x_i)}{\exp(\beta^\top x_j)} = \exp\!\left(\beta^\top(x_i-x_j)\right) = \text{constant in } t \] The baseline hazard cancels entirely. This is why \(h_0(t)\) need not be estimated to obtain valid inference about \(\beta\).

Log-Scale Interpretation

\[ \log h_i(t) = \underbrace{\log h_0(t)}_{\text{time-varying intercept}} + \underbrace{\beta^\top x_i}_{\text{no intercept term}} \]

For subjects \(i\) and \(j\): \(\log h_i(t) - \log h_j(t) = \beta^\top(x_i-x_j)\) — constant in \(t\). On the log-hazard scale the two functions are parallel.

No intercept in \(\beta^\top x_i\): Including an intercept would be double-counting — the time-varying intercept role is already played by \(\log h_0(t)\).

Interpreting the Hazard Ratio \(e^{\beta_k}\)

  • Binary covariate \(x_k\in\{0,1\}\): \(e^{\beta_k}\) = rate of event in group \(x_k=1\) relative to group \(x_k=0\), for subjects identical on all other covariates, at any time.
  • Continuous covariate \(x_k\): \(e^{\beta_k}\) = multiplicative change in hazard per one-unit increase in \(x_k\), holding all other covariates constant. For a \(c\)-unit change: \(e^{c\beta_k}\).
Schizophrenia example (fit1): \(\hat\beta_{\text{Gender}}=0.6742\), HR\(=e^{0.6742}=1.9624\). Females have approximately twice the hazard of males with the same EducCat at every time point — an estimated 96% higher mortality. The 95% CI \([1.418,\,2.715]\) excludes 1 (\(p=4.7\times10^{-5}\)). \(\hat\beta_{\text{EducCat}}=0.1208\), HR\(=1.1284\), \(p=0.487\) — Education non-significant; CI \([0.803,\,1.586]\) includes 1.

Fitting the Model — Partial Likelihood

Why Partial Likelihood?

The full likelihood involves both \(\beta\) and the infinite-dimensional \(h_0(t)\). The key insight of Cox (1972) is to profile out \(h_0\): fix \(\beta\), maximise over \(h_0\), find the optimal \(h_0\) as a function of \(\beta\) (the Breslow estimator), substitute back, and maximise the resulting partial likelihood over \(\beta\) alone.

Setup (No Ties)

With \(r\) distinct ordered event times \(Y_{(1)} < \cdots < Y_{(r)}\), the full likelihood is:

\[ \prod_{j=1}^r h_{0(j)}\exp(x_{(j)}^\top\beta) \cdot \prod_{i=1}^n \exp\!\left(-H_0(Y_i)\exp(x_i^\top\beta)\right) \]

Profiling Out \(h_0\): The Breslow Estimator

Setting \(\partial L/\partial h_{0(j)} = 0\) yields:

\[ \hat{h}_{0(j)} = \frac{1}{\sum_{k\in R(Y_{(j)})}\exp(x_k^\top\beta)} \equiv \frac{1}{b_j} \]

where \(R(Y_{(j)})\) is the risk set at \(Y_{(j)}\). The estimator places zero mass outside observed event times.

The Partial Likelihood

\[ L(\beta) = \prod_{j=1}^r \frac{\exp(x_{(j)}^\top\beta)}{\sum_{k\in R(Y_{(j)})}\exp(x_k^\top\beta)} \]
Interpretation of each factor: At event time \(Y_{(j)}\), the factor is the conditional probability — given that one event occurs among those at risk — that it is subject \((j)\) who fails. This is a rank-based, baseline-free object.

Log Partial Likelihood

\[ \ell(\beta) = \sum_{j=1}^r x_{(j)}^\top\beta - \sum_{j=1}^r \log\!\left(\sum_{k\in R(Y_{(j)})}\exp(x_k^\top\beta)\right) \]

Score Function and Information Matrix

\[ U_h(\beta) = \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_h} = \sum_{j=1}^r x_{(j)h} - \sum_{j=1}^r \frac{\sum_{k\in R(Y_{(j)})}x_{kh}\exp(x_k^\top\beta)}{\sum_{k\in R(Y_{(j)})}\exp(x_k^\top\beta)} \]

The observed information \(I(\beta)=-\partial^2\ell/\partial\beta_h\partial\beta_l\) is a weighted sum of variances of the covariate across the risk set at each event time.

Newton-Raphson Maximisation

\[ \hat\beta_\text{new} = \hat\beta_\text{old} + I^{-1}(\hat\beta_\text{old})\,U(\hat\beta_\text{old}) \]

Iterates until \(\hat\beta_\text{old}\) and \(\hat\beta_\text{new}\) are sufficiently close. In R: coxph(Surv(Time, Censor) ~ Covar1 + Covar2, data = d).

Asymptotic Properties

\(\hat\beta\) is consistent and asymptotically normal (Gill, 1984):

\[ V(\hat\beta_h) \approx [I(\hat\beta)]^{-1}_{hh} \]

A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI for the hazard ratio \(\psi_h=\exp(\beta_h)\):

\[ \exp\!\left(\hat\beta_h \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{V(\hat\beta_h)}\right) \]

Partial Likelihood for Tied Observations

Two Types of Ties

  • Censoring time ties with an event time: handled by convention — the censored subject is assumed to be censored just after the event, so they remain in the risk set.
  • Two or more subjects experience the event at the same time: handled by three approximations below.

Breslow (1974) Approximation — Simplest, Default in SAS

\[ L(\beta) = \prod_{j=1}^r \frac{\prod_{l: Y_l=Y_{(j)},\delta_l=1}\exp(x_l^\top\beta)}{\left[\sum_{k: Y_k\geq Y_{(j)}}\exp(x_k^\top\beta)\right]^{d_{(j)}}} \]

Efron (1977) Approximation — Default in R; More Accurate

\[ L(\beta) = \prod_{j=1}^r \frac{\prod_{l: Y_l=Y_{(j)},\delta_l=1}\exp(x_l^\top\beta)}{\prod_{h=1}^{d_{(j)}}\!\left(\sum_{k:Y_k\geq Y_{(j)}}\!\exp(x_k^\top\beta)-\tfrac{h-1}{d_{(j)}}\!\sum_{l: Y_l=Y_{(j)},\delta_l=1}\!\exp(x_l^\top\beta)\right)} \]

Cox (1972) Exact Method — Theoretically Correct, Rarely Used

\[ L(\beta) = \prod_{j=1}^r \frac{\prod_{l: Y_l=Y_{(j)},\delta_l=1}\exp(x_l^\top\beta)}{\sum_{q\in Q_j}\sum_{h\in q}\exp(x_h^\top\beta)} \]

where \(Q_j\) = all \(\binom{r_{(j)}}{d_{(j)}}\) combinations of \(d_{(j)}\) subjects from the risk set. Computationally prohibitive for large tied groups.

Worked Example at \(t=1096\) — Gender Covariate Only

Males: 68 at risk, 2 events (\(x_l=0\)); Females: 12 at risk, 0 events (\(x_l=1\)).

MethodFormulaValue at \(\beta=0\)
Breslow\(\exp(2\times0)/(68+12\exp\beta)^2\)0.000120
Efron\(\exp(2\times0)/[(68+12\exp\beta)(67+12\exp\beta)]\)0.000121
Cox exact\(\exp(2\times0)/[\exp(2\beta)\tbinom{12}{2}+\exp(\beta)\tbinom{12}{1}\tbinom{68}{1}+\tbinom{68}{2}]\)0.000243
Practical conclusion: Breslow and Efron give very similar results in R (ties="efron" default; ties="breslow" optional). The Cox exact method can differ noticeably. In the schizophrenia data, Efron and Breslow produce nearly identical \(\hat\beta\) and p-values.

Hypothesis Testing in the Cox Model

Three Global Tests

All three test \(H_0:\beta=\beta_0\) vs \(H_1:\beta\neq\beta_0\), and all have \(\chi^2_p\) null distributions:

TestStatisticRequirement
Wald\(U_W^2 = (\hat\beta-\beta_0)^\top I(\hat\beta)(\hat\beta-\beta_0)\)Full model only
LR\(U_{LR}^2 = 2(\ell(\hat\beta)-\ell(\beta_0))\)Full + restricted model
Score\(U_{SC}^2 = U(\beta_0)^\top I^{-1}(\beta_0)U(\beta_0)\)= log-rank (no ties)

The three tests are asymptotically equivalent under \(H_0\) but can differ in finite samples. The LR test generally has better small-sample behaviour.

Local Tests — Subvector \(\beta_1\)

Partition \(\beta=(\beta_1^\top,\beta_2^\top)^\top\). Test \(H_0:\beta_1=\beta_{10}\):

\[ U_W^2 = (\hat\beta_1-\beta_{10})^\top (I^{11}(\hat\beta))^{-1}(\hat\beta_1-\beta_{10}) \approx \chi^2_q \]
\[ U_{LR}^2 = 2\!\left[\ell(\hat\beta) - \ell\!\left(\beta_{10},\hat\beta_2(\beta_{10})\right)\right] \approx \chi^2_q \]

Here \(\hat\beta_2(\beta_{10})\) is the MLE of \(\beta_2\) with \(\beta_1\) fixed at \(\beta_{10}\), and \(I^{11}\) is the \((1,1)\) block of \(I^{-1}\).

Testing marital status overall (fit2): Joint test \(H_0:\beta_\text{Married}=\beta_\text{Aloneagain}=0\) (2 df) using linearHypothesis(fit2, c("MarriedTRUE","AloneagainTRUE")): Chisq\(=23.98\), \(p=6.2\times10^{-6}\) (Wald); \(U^2_{LR}=23.28\), \(p=8.8\times10^{-6}\) (LR). Both strongly reject \(H_0\).

Linear Contrasts \(c^\top\beta=0\)

\[ U_W^2 = \frac{(c^\top\hat\beta)^2}{c^\top I^{-1}(\hat\beta)\,c} \approx \chi^2_1 \]
Testing \(H_0:\beta_\text{Married}=\beta_\text{Aloneagain}\): Equivalent to \(c^\top=(1,-1,0,0)\). Using linearHypothesis(fit2,"MarriedTRUE - AloneagainTRUE"): Chisq\(=18.40\), \(p=1.8\times10^{-5}\). The HR for Married vs Aloneagain is \(e^{-0.1942-0.7314}=e^{-0.9256}=0.396\).

Interaction Terms and Time-Varying Covariates

Interaction Between Covariates

An interaction means the effect of one covariate on the hazard depends on the level of another.

Interaction typeExtra parameters
Continuous \(\times\) categorical (\(j\) levels)\(j-1\)
Categorical (\(j\) levels) \(\times\) categorical (\(k\) levels)\((j-1)\times(k-1)\)
Gender × Marital status in schizophrenia data (fit4): Model: Gender + Married + Aloneagain + Gender:Married + Gender:Aloneagain. HR for Gender in Singles: \(e^{0.5197}=1.682\); in Married: \(e^{0.5197+0.3894}=2.482\); in Aloneagain: \(e^{0.5197-0.1456}=1.454\). Testing interactions simultaneously: \(H_0:\beta_{\text{G:Married}}=\beta_{\text{G:Aloneagain}}=0\) gives Chisq\(=1.532\), \(p=0.465\). Not significant — Gender effect is the same regardless of marital status.

Time-Varying Covariates

When a covariate \(x_i(t)\) changes over time, the extended Cox model is:

\[ h_i(t) = h_0(t)\exp(\beta^\top x_i(t)) \]
Proportionality is lost: When \(x_i(t)\) varies, the ratio \(h_i(t)/h_j(t)=\exp(\beta^\top(x_i(t)-x_j(t)))\) is no longer constant in \(t\).

The partial likelihood extends naturally — at each event time \(Y_i\), the risk set denominator uses each at-risk subject's covariate value at that time \(x_k(Y_i)\):

\[ L(\beta) = \prod_{i=1}^n \left[\frac{\exp(x_i(Y_i)^\top\beta)}{\sum_{k\in R(Y_i)}\exp(x_k(Y_i)^\top\beta)}\right]^{\delta_i} \]
Milk ureum example: Ureum concentration measured monthly; values at intermediate times obtained by linear interpolation. Result: \(\hat\beta=-0.0273\), HR\(=0.973\), \(p=0.094\). Higher ureum is weakly associated with lower insemination hazard. The hazard depends only on the current value, not history.

Primacy of science over data: Variable selection should be guided first by biological/clinical knowledge. Automated selection may inflate Type I error and produce unstable models. All decisions should ideally be pre-specified.

Schizophrenia — Forward Selection Result

Univariate LR p-values: Marital \(=6.7\times10^{-7}\), Gender \(=9.7\times10^{-5}\), Education \(=0.663\). Forward procedure selects Marital status first, then Gender; Education remains non-significant throughout. Final model: Marital + Gender.

Survival Function Estimation

\[ \hat{S}_i(t) = \hat{S}_0(t)^{\exp(\hat\beta^\top x_i)}, \qquad \hat{S}_0(t)=\exp(-\hat{H}_0(t)) \]

The baseline cumulative hazard is estimated by the extended Breslow estimator:

\[ \hat{H}_0(t) = \sum_{j: Y_{(j)}\leq t} \frac{d_{(j)}}{\sum_{k\in R(Y_{(j)})}\exp(x_k^\top\hat\beta)} \]
Important limit: This formula requires all covariates to be time-independent. With time-varying covariates, the power-law formula \(\hat S_0^{\exp(\hat\beta^\top x)}\) breaks down.

Assessing the Proportional Hazards Assumption

The Cox model is powerful but its PH assumption can fail. Three complementary tools are available.

Tool 1 — Diagnostic Plots (Univariate)

Estimate \(\hat{H}_j(t)\) via Nelson-Åalen for each level \(j\) of a categorical covariate.

PlotPH holds if
\(\log\hat{H}_j(t)\) vs \(t\)Curves are parallel (constant vertical gap)
\(\log\hat{H}_j(t)-\log\hat{H}_1(t)\) vs \(t\)Constant (horizontal) lines
\(\hat{H}_j(t)\) vs \(\hat{H}_1(t)\)Straight lines through the origin
Do not use KM survival curve plots to check PH: Even if survival curves appear non-crossing, the hazards can be non-proportional. The log-cumulative hazard plots are the correct tool.

Tool 2 — Time-by-Covariate Interaction Test

Extend the model with \(\alpha \, x_{ik} \times g(t)\) for some pre-specified function \(g(t)\):

\[ h_i(t) = h_0(t)\exp(\beta^\top x_i + \alpha\, x_{ik}\times g(t)) \]

Test \(H_0:\alpha=0\). If rejected, PH fails for covariate \(k\). The choice of \(g(t)\) must be pre-specified.

Schizophrenia (fit7): Testing Gender \(\times\,\mathbf{1}(t\geq1100)\) in the model with Marital status: \(\hat\alpha=-1.4699\), \(p=0.052\) — borderline non-significant.

Tool 3 — Schoenfeld Residuals

For each event (uncensored) subject \(i\) and covariate \(k\):

\[ r_{ik} = \delta_i\left(x_{ik} - \frac{\sum_{j\in R(Y_i)} x_{jk}\exp(\hat\beta^\top x_j)}{\sum_{j\in R(Y_i)}\exp(\hat\beta^\top x_j)}\right) \]

Each residual is the difference between the failing subject's covariate and its risk-set-weighted mean. Censored observations contribute 0. Under PH, Schoenfeld residuals are independent of time. The cox.zph function fits a smooth function of time and tests for zero slope.

Schizophrenia (fit5, cox.zph): Gender: chisq\(=1.41\), \(p=0.23\); Married: chisq\(=1.94\), \(p=0.16\); Aloneagain: chisq\(=0.86\), \(p=0.36\); GLOBAL: chisq\(=3.08\), \(p=0.38\). All non-significant — PH assumption is valid.

Stratified Semiparametric PH Model

Motivation

If PH fails for a nuisance covariate, stratification allows each stratum to have its own baseline hazard while sharing the same \(\beta\) for remaining covariates:

\[ h_{ij}(t) = h_{i0}(t)\exp(x_{ij}^\top\beta), \quad i=1,\ldots,s,\; j=1,\ldots,n_i \]

The partial likelihood factorises over strata:

\[ L(\beta) = \prod_{i=1}^s \prod_{j=1}^{n_i} \left[\frac{\exp(x_{ij}^\top\beta)}{\sum_{l\in R_i(Y_{ij})}\exp(x_{il}^\top\beta)}\right]^{\delta_{ij}} \]
Key difference from stratified nonparametric test: The stratified Cox model still estimates and tests covariate effects (the \(\beta\)'s); it merely allows the baseline hazard to differ between strata.
Limitation: The stratification variable's own effect cannot be estimated from this model.

Schizophrenia (fit9)

Stratify on Gender, estimate effect of Marital status:

coxph(Surv(Time,Censor)~Married+Aloneagain+strata(Gender), data=schizo)

Results: \(\hat{HR}_\text{Aloneagain}=2.035\), \(p=1.3\times10^{-4}\). Nearly identical to unstratified fit5 — confirming the unstratified Cox model was already adequate.

When to Use the Stratified Model

  • A variable is suspected of non-proportional hazards
  • It is a confounder not of primary interest
  • There are enough events per stratum for reliable within-stratum partial likelihoods

Short Answer 1 · Partial Likelihood Derivation
Explain, step by step, how the partial likelihood \(L(\beta)=\prod_j\exp(x_{(j)}^\top\beta)/\sum_{k\in R(Y_{(j)})}\exp(x_k^\top\beta)\) is derived from the full likelihood for data without ties. Why is \(h_0(t)\) eliminated in this process?

Step 1 — Full likelihood.

The full likelihood factors as \(\prod_j h_{0(j)}\exp(x_{(j)}^\top\beta)\cdot\prod_i\exp(-H_0(Y_i)\exp(x_i^\top\beta))\), involving both \(\beta\) and the infinite-dimensional baseline hazard \(h_0(t)\).

Step 2 — Profile over \(h_0\).

Treating \(\beta\) as fixed, differentiate with respect to each \(h_{0(j)}\) and set to zero. The solution is the Breslow estimator: \(\hat{h}_{0(j)}=1/b_j\) where \(b_j=\sum_{k\in R(Y_{(j)})}\exp(x_k^\top\beta)\). The key insight is that the maximising \(h_0\) is discrete, with mass only at observed event times.

Step 3 — Substitute back.

Plugging \(\hat{h}_{0(j)}=1/b_j\) into the full likelihood, terms involving only \(h_{0(j)}\) (and not \(\beta\)) cancel. What remains is the partial likelihood \(L(\beta)\).

Why \(h_0\) is eliminated.

The Breslow estimator expresses \(h_0\) as a function of \(\beta\), so substituting it removes the infinite-dimensional nuisance entirely. The partial likelihood is rank-based — each factor is the conditional probability of the observed ranking among those at risk, depending only on relative risks \(\exp(\beta^\top x_k)\) and not on the absolute time scale \(h_0(t)\). This is Cox's fundamental insight: the ordering of failure times is informative about \(\beta\) even without knowing the baseline hazard shape.

Short Answer 2 · Breslow vs Efron vs Cox Exact
Compare the Breslow, Efron, and Cox exact approaches for handling tied event times. In what sense does Efron's method improve on Breslow's? When would differences between methods matter most?

Breslow (1974).

The simplest approximation. It raises the full risk set sum \(b_j\) to the power \(d_{(j)}\), effectively pretending all tied events occurred against the same full risk set. This over-counts risk set members for the 2nd, 3rd, … tied event, inflating the denominator and biasing \(\hat\beta\) slightly towards zero.

Efron (1977).

Corrects Breslow by subtracting an increasing fraction of the tied events' contribution from the denominator. For the \(h\)-th tie it uses \(b_j - \tfrac{h-1}{d_{(j)}}\sum_\text{tied}\exp(x_l^\top\beta)\), interpolating between Breslow (simultaneous ties) and the exact method (sequential). More accurate at far lower computational cost. Default in R.

Cox exact method.

Averages over all \(\binom{r_{(j)}}{d_{(j)}}\) possible orderings of tied events — theoretically correct but combinatorially explosive (e.g. 20 ties from 100 at risk yields ~\(5\times10^{20}\) terms).

When differences matter.

All three are nearly identical when ties are rare (continuous-time data). Differences become meaningful when a large fraction of event times are tied, e.g. daily clinical records where many patients die on the same day. In the schizophrenia data, Efron and Breslow yield virtually identical \(\hat\beta\), confirming ties are not a major issue there.

Short Answer 4 · Wald, LR, and Score Tests — Theory
State the formulas for the Wald, LR, and score tests for \(H_0:\beta=\beta_0\) in the Cox model. Explain geometrically why each uses a different aspect of the log-likelihood surface, and why the LR test is often preferred in finite samples.

Formulas.

Wald: \(U_W^2=(\hat\beta-\beta_0)^\top I(\hat\beta)(\hat\beta-\beta_0)\). Measures how far the MLE is from \(\beta_0\), scaled by curvature at \(\hat\beta\). LR: \(U_{LR}^2=2(\ell(\hat\beta)-\ell(\beta_0))\). Measures the drop in log-likelihood from the MLE to the null point. Score: \(U_{SC}^2=U(\beta_0)^\top I^{-1}(\beta_0)U(\beta_0)\). Measures how steep the log-likelihood is at \(\beta_0\).

Geometric intuition.

The Wald test works outward from the peak \(\hat\beta\), approximating the likelihood as quadratic around the MLE. The LR test directly compares two heights on the likelihood surface, making no shape assumption. The Score test works outward from the null point \(\beta_0\), requiring only the null model.

Why LR is preferred in finite samples.

The LR test is invariant to reparameterisation (unlike Wald, which depends on how \(\beta\) is parameterised) and does not rely on the local quadratic approximation at \(\hat\beta\) (which can be poor when \(\hat\beta\) is far from \(\beta_0\) or in small samples). Simulation studies consistently show better Type I error control for the LR test. The score test is computationally attractive (only the null model is needed) but has weaker finite-sample properties.

Short Answer 5 · Schoenfeld Residuals in Depth
Define the Schoenfeld residual \(r_{ik}\), explain what it measures, why it should be unrelated to time under PH, and describe how cox.zph converts these residuals into a formal PH test.

Definition.

For uncensored subject \(i\) and covariate \(k\): \(r_{ik}=x_{ik}-\sum_{j\in R(Y_i)}x_{jk}\exp(\hat\beta^\top x_j)/\sum_{j\in R(Y_i)}\exp(\hat\beta^\top x_j)\). It is the difference between the failing subject's covariate value and the risk-set weighted mean of that covariate. For censored subjects, \(r_{ik}=0\).

What it measures.

Whether the subject who fails at \(Y_i\) has a systematically higher or lower covariate value than expected given the risk set composition. Under PH there should be no systematic pattern over time — the residuals should fluctuate randomly around zero.

Under PH.

Grambsch and Therneau (1994) showed that scaled Schoenfeld residuals \(\approx \hat\beta(t)-\hat\beta\), where \(\hat\beta(t)\) is a time-varying estimate. Under PH, \(\hat\beta(t)=\text{const}\), so the scaled residuals have zero slope in time.

The cox.zph test.

For each covariate \(k\), cox.zph fits a smooth function of time to \(r_{ik}\) and tests \(H_0:\) slope \(=0\). A significant positive slope means the HR for covariate \(k\) increases over time; negative means it decreases. The GLOBAL row tests all covariates simultaneously. Both the test statistic and the smooth plot (with confidence bands) indicate the nature and timing of any PH violation. In the schizophrenia fit5, all p-values (\(\geq 0.16\)) and the global p=0.38 confirm no PH violation.

Short Answer 6 · Non-Proportional Hazards — Consequences
The Dutch gastric cancer trial (Putter et al., 2005) compared D2 vs D1 surgery. A standard Cox model gave HR=0.97, p=0.73 — "no effect." Explain what the non-proportional hazards plot revealed and why the standard Cox model produced a misleading conclusion. How should such data be analysed?

What the plot revealed.

The KM survival curves crossed at approximately 53 months. Before crossing, D2 patients had higher hazard (more peri-operative deaths from the aggressive procedure). After crossing, D2 patients had lower hazard (better long-term cancer control). The time-varying HR plot showed HR starting above 2 at early follow-up and declining below 1 after ~48 months.

Why the Cox model was misleading.

The standard Cox model estimates a single constant HR — a weighted average of the time-varying HR over the entire follow-up. Because early excess mortality (HR>1) and the late benefit (HR<1) cancelled, the weighted average HR≈1 and p=0.73, giving a completely false "no treatment effect" conclusion and hiding a clinically important pattern of initial harm followed by long-term benefit.

Correct analysis approaches.

(1) Time-by-treatment interaction: model \(\beta(t)=\beta_0+\alpha\cdot g(t)\) with a pre-specified \(g(t)\). (2) Piecewise constant HR: fit separate HRs before and after the crossing point. (3) Flexible Cox model: use spline functions of time for the treatment effect. (4) Stratified analysis by time window: test treatment effect separately in early and late periods. All approaches confirm early harm and late benefit — a far more informative and accurate summary than a single misleading average HR.

Short Answer 7 · Stratified Cox vs Stratified Log-Rank
Compare the stratified semiparametric Cox model with the stratified nonparametric log-rank test. What does each provide? When is each preferable, and what is the key limitation of each?

Stratified log-rank test.

Tests whether \(k\) groups have the same hazard function within strata using weighted observed-minus-expected counts. Produces a single p-value. Requires no distributional assumptions and no model fitting. However, it provides no effect size estimate, no confidence interval, no adjustment for continuous confounders, and cannot handle multiple covariates simultaneously.

Stratified Cox model.

Allows each stratum its own baseline hazard (maximum flexibility for the nuisance variable) while estimating shared \(\beta\) coefficients across strata. Provides point estimates, CIs, and significance tests for all modelled covariates. Can handle multiple continuous and categorical covariates. The stratification variable's own effect cannot be estimated, and the model still assumes PH for the non-stratified covariates.

Preference.

Use stratified log-rank when: simplicity is paramount, a single binary treatment comparison is needed, no covariate quantification is required (e.g. primary analysis in an RCT with a pre-specified stratification factor). Use stratified Cox when: multiple covariates need adjustment, continuous confounders are present, effect estimates are required for clinical or regulatory purposes, or a variable's PH violation would otherwise bias regression estimates.

Short Answer 8 · Complete Analysis Strategy
You are analysing an RCT with 280 cancer patients comparing two chemotherapy regimens (A vs B). Dataset: time-to-death, censoring indicator, and baseline covariates: treatment (binary), age (continuous), ECOG score (3-level: 0,1,2), biomarker (continuous). Describe a complete pre-specified analysis strategy including: (a) primary model, (b) hypothesis tests, (c) PH verification, (d) survival function estimation.

(a) Model specification.

Fit a Cox PH model with treatment (binary), age (continuous), two ECOG indicator variables (ECOG=1 vs 0; ECOG=2 vs 0), and biomarker (continuous). If strong a priori evidence suggests a treatment-by-biomarker interaction, pre-specify and include it. Use Efron's method for ties (R default): coxph(Surv(T,D)~treatment+age+I(ECOG==1)+I(ECOG==2)+biomarker).

(b) Hypothesis tests.

Primary: Wald test for \(H_0:\beta_\text{treatment}=0\) with \(\hat{HR}\) and 95% CI. Secondary: (1) Global LR/Wald/Score tests for the whole model (3+1+1=5 df total — but reported as 5 df). (2) Local LR test for ECOG overall (\(H_0:\beta_{\text{ECOG=1}}=\beta_{\text{ECOG=2}}=0\), 2 df) via anova(fit_full, fit_no_ECOG). (3) Wald test for biomarker. All tests pre-specified in the statistical analysis plan with their degrees of freedom.

(c) PH verification.

(1) Log-cumulative hazard plots \(\log\hat{H}(t)\) vs \(t\) for treatment and each ECOG level. (2) cox.zph(fit) for all Schoenfeld residual tests plus GLOBAL — report individual and global p-values. (3) If a specific pattern is anticipated (e.g. delayed treatment effect), pre-specify a time-by-treatment interaction test with \(g(t)=\mathbf{1}(t\geq t^*)\) for a clinically motivated \(t^*\). If PH fails for ECOG but not treatment, stratify on ECOG and re-estimate the treatment HR.

(d) Survival function estimation.

Use survfit(fit, newdata=...) to compute predicted survival curves for clinically meaningful profiles (e.g. young vs old patients on A vs B at median biomarker). Present curves with 95% CIs and report median survival times. This is valid only for time-fixed covariates — if biomarker were time-varying, present landmark analyses instead.
