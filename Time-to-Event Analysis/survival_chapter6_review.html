📖 Study Notes — Cure Models
1. Classical Survival Models vs. Cure Models

In classical survival analysis, all individuals are assumed to eventually experience the event of interest, so the survival function is proper:

$$\lim_{t \to \infty} S(t) = 0, \qquad \text{where } S(t) = P(T > t)$$

This assumption is realistic for events such as all-cause mortality, machine failure, or retirement — events that will occur with probability 1.

When does the classical assumption fail? In many settings, a fraction of the population will never experience the event of interest — for example: cancer recurrence (some patients are permanently cured), loan default (some borrowers never default), re-arrest (some released prisoners never re-offend), and time to a second child (some parents have only one child).
2. The Two-Population Structure

A cure model partitions the population into two latent groups:

  • Cured (immune) individuals — will never experience the event; their time-to-event \(T\) is set to \(\infty\).
  • Susceptible individuals — will eventually experience the event.
Improper Survival Function. Because a positive fraction of the population never experiences the event, the population survival function satisfies: $$\lim_{t \to \infty} S(t) > 0$$ This is called an improper (or defective) survival function. The cure rate is defined as: $$1 - p = \lim_{t \to \infty} S(t)$$ where \(p = P(B = 1)\) is the probability of being susceptible, and \(B = I(T < \infty)\) is the latent cure indicator.

On a Kaplan–Meier plot, the cure fraction appears as a plateau at the right tail of the estimated survival curve — the height of this plateau estimates \(1 - p\).

3. Censoring in Cure Models

We assume random right censoring. Instead of observing \(T\) directly, we observe:

$$Y = \min(T, C), \qquad \Delta = I(T \leq C)$$

where \(C\) is the (independent) censoring time. Note the key asymmetry:

When \(\Delta = 1\): the event was observed → the individual is definitely susceptible.
When \(\Delta = 0\): the observation is censored → the individual could be either cured or susceptible (status unknown).

The cure indicator \(B = I(T < \infty)\) is therefore latent — it is never directly observed. This is the fundamental challenge of cure model estimation.

4. Nonparametric Identification and Estimation

Let \(S_u(t) = P(T > t \mid B = 1)\) be the proper survival function of the susceptibles, and \(F_u = 1 - S_u\). The mixture decomposition of the population survival function is:

$$S(t) = (1 - p) + p \, S_u(t)$$
Identifiability Condition (Maller & Zhou 1992). Let \(\tau_F\) denote the right endpoint of the support of any distribution \(F\), and let \(G\) be the censoring distribution. If $$\tau_{F_u} \leq \tau_G,$$ then the cure model is nonparametrically identified. This condition is called sufficient follow-up: the follow-up period must be long enough to cover the full support of the susceptibles' failure time distribution. It is crucial for consistent inference.
4a. Nonparametric Estimator of \(1 - p\)

When \(\tau_{F_u} \leq \tau_G\), the cure rate can be estimated by the tail value of the Kaplan–Meier estimator \(\hat{S}\) evaluated at the largest observation \(Y_n\):

$$1 - \hat{p} = \hat{S}(Y_n)$$

This exploits the fact that \(1 - p = \lim_{t \to \infty} S(t)\).

Consistency (Maller & Zhou 1992). Assume \(0 < p < 1\) and \(S\) is continuous at \(\tau_G\) if \(\tau_G < \infty\). Then: $$\hat{S}(Y_n) \xrightarrow{P} 1 - p \quad \iff \quad \tau_{F_u} \leq \tau_G$$
Asymptotic Normality (Maller & Zhou 1992). Assume \(0 < p < 1\) and \(\tau_G = \infty\). If \(\int_0^\infty \frac{dF(s)}{1 - G(s^-)} < \infty\), then: $$n^{1/2}\bigl\{\hat{S}(Y_n) - (1-p)\bigr\} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \sigma^2)$$ where $$\sigma^2 = (1-p)^2 \int_0^\infty \frac{dF(s)}{\{1-F(s)\}\{1-F(s^-)\}\{1-G(s^-)\}}$$ Estimating \(\sigma^2\) by replacing \(p, F, G\) with their Kaplan–Meier counterparts yields the confidence interval: $$1 - \hat{p} \;\pm\; z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\sqrt{n}}$$

6. Mixture Cure Regression Models

With covariates, the general mixture cure model is:

$$S(t \mid x, z) = p(z)\,S_u(t \mid x) + 1 - p(z)$$

The model has two distinct components:

  • Incidence: \(p(z) = P(B = 1 \mid Z = z)\) — models the long-term probability of being susceptible, capturing a treatment's curative effect. Most often a logistic regression model: $$p(z) = \frac{\exp(\alpha_0 + \alpha^T z)}{1 + \exp(\alpha_0 + \alpha^T z)}$$
  • Latency: \(S_u(t \mid x) = P(T > t \mid X = x, B = 1)\) — models the short-term survival of the susceptibles. Choices include parametric models, Cox PH, or AFT models.
The mixture cure model was originally proposed by Boag (1949), Berkson & Gage (1952), and Farewell (1982). Covariates \(X\) and \(Z\) may be equal, partially overlapping, or completely different. Importantly, there is no proportional hazards assumption at the population level — PH holds only within the susceptible subgroup.
7. Logistic / Cox PH Mixture Cure Model

The latency takes a Cox PH form:

$$S_u(t \mid x) = S_{u,0}(t)^{\exp(\beta^T x)}$$

where \(S_{u,0}(t) = P(T > t \mid B = 1, X = 0)\) is left unspecified. The key difficulty is that the PH assumption does not hold at the population level, so the baseline hazard \(h_{u,0}(\cdot)\) cannot be isolated in the likelihood — the partial likelihood trick from standard Cox PH is not applicable.

EM Algorithm (Peng & Dear 2000; Sy & Taylor 2000). Since \(B_i = I(T_i < \infty)\) is latent, one augments the data with \(B_i\) and uses the EM algorithm. The complete-data likelihood factorises as: $$L_c(\alpha, \beta, S_{u,0}) = L_1(\alpha) \times L_2(\beta, S_{u,0})$$ where \(L_1\) depends only on \(\alpha\) (incidence) and \(L_2\) depends only on \(\beta, S_{u,0}\) (latency), enabling separate estimation of the two parts.
E-step: Replace the unobserved \(B_i\) by its conditional expectation given the current parameter estimates \(\theta^{(m-1)} = (\alpha, \beta, S_{u,0})^{(m-1)}\) and the observed data \(V_i = (Y_i, \Delta_i, X_i, Z_i)\).

M-step: Maximise the expected complete-data log-likelihood. The incidence part reduces to a standard weighted logistic regression; the latency part is solved via partial or marginal likelihood approaches.

8. Logistic / AFT Mixture Cure Model

The latency is modelled via a semiparametric accelerated failure time (AFT) model (in contrast to the fully parametric AFT of Chapter 5):

$$S_u(t \mid x) = S_0\bigl(t\,\exp(-\beta^T x)\bigr)$$

where \(S_0\) is an unknown baseline survival function. Equivalently:

$$\log T^* = \beta_0 + \beta^T X + \epsilon$$

where \(\epsilon\) is independent of \(X\) with unknown distribution, and \(T = BT^* + (1-B)\infty\). The EM algorithm (Zhang & Peng 2007) is used; the R package smcure provides implementation.

Cox vs. AFT for Latency: Under the Cox model, the support of \(T\) does not depend on \(X\). Under AFT, the support is scaled by \(\exp(\beta^T X)\), which is more realistic in settings where patients with worse prognosis die faster (e.g., aggressive vs. slowly-growing tumours).
9. Promotion Time Cure Regression Models

An alternative to the mixture cure model is the promotion time (or bounded cumulative hazard / PH cure) model:

$$S(t \mid x) = \exp\bigl\{-\theta(x)\,F(t)\bigr\}$$

where \(\theta(x) = \exp(\beta_0 + \beta^T x) > 0\) and \(F(\cdot)\) is an unknown distribution function. Key properties:

  • The model has a proportional hazards structure at the population level.
  • The cure rate is \(P(B = 0 \mid X = x) = \exp\{-\theta(x)\}\).
  • The cumulative hazard \(H(t) = \theta F(t)\) is bounded, justifying the name "bounded cumulative hazard model."
Biological Motivation (Yakovlev et al. 1996; Tsodikov 1998). After cancer treatment, let \(N \sim \text{Poisson}(\theta(X))\) be the number of residual carcinogenic cells. Each cell \(k\) produces a detectable tumour at time \(W_k\) (i.i.d. with distribution \(F\), independent of \(N\)). Then: $$T = \min(W_1, \ldots, W_N), \quad N = 0 \Rightarrow \text{cured}$$ $$S(t \mid X) = \exp[-\theta(X)F(t)]$$ Increasing \(\theta(x)\) means more residual cells → lower cure probability and shorter survival for susceptibles.
Link to Cox PH Model (Portier et al. 2017). The promotion time model is a special Cox PH model with a bounded cumulative baseline hazard \(H(t) = \exp(\beta_0)F(t)\). It can be shown that \(\hat{\beta}_{PT} = \hat{\beta}_{PH}\) and \(\exp(\hat{\beta}_{0,PT})\hat{F}_{PT}(t) = \hat{H}_{PH}(t)\), so estimation can be performed using standard Cox software with a zero-tail constraint.
10. Extensions and Other Cure Models

Beyond the standard mixture and promotion time models, the literature includes flexible extensions such as probit or complementary log-log link functions for incidence; single-index incidence models \(p(z) = g(\alpha^T z)\) with unspecified \(g\) (Amico et al. 2019); nonparametric latency combined with logistic incidence (Patilea & Van Keilegom 2020); and fully nonparametric mixture cure models (Xu & Peng 2014; López-Cheda et al. 2017). In economics, cure models are called "split population models"; in engineering, "limited-failure population life models."

✏️ Short Answer Questions
SA1. Explain what it means for the survival function to be improper. Contrast it with the classical case and state the mathematical definition of the cure rate.

In classical survival analysis, \(S(t)\) is a proper distribution: \(\lim_{t\to\infty}S(t)=0\), meaning every individual eventually experiences the event with probability 1.

In a cure model, a fraction \(1-p>0\) never experiences the event (\(T=\infty\)), so: $$\lim_{t\to\infty}S(t)=P(T=\infty)=1-p>0$$ The survival function is called improper (or defective). The cure rate is \(1-p=\lim_{t\to\infty}S(t)\), visually identifiable as the plateau in the KM plot.

SA2. State and explain the formal nonparametric identifiability condition. What happens if it is violated?

The condition \(\tau_{F_u}\leq\tau_G\) — sufficient follow-up — requires that the censoring distribution's support extends at least as far as the susceptibles' failure time support. When it holds, \(\hat S(Y_n)\xrightarrow{P}1-p\). If violated, censored susceptibles are indistinguishable from cured individuals, causing the KM tail to systematically overestimate the cure fraction.

SA3. Outline the EM algorithm for the logistic/Cox PH mixture cure model, clearly describing the E-step and M-step.

The EM algorithm is needed because \(B_i\) is latent. The complete-data likelihood factorises as \(L_c=L_1(\alpha)\times L_2(\beta,S_{u,0})\), but \(B_i\) is unobserved.

E-step: For \(\Delta_i=1\): \(w_i=1\). For \(\Delta_i=0\): $$w_i^{(m)}=\frac{p^{(m-1)}(Z_i)\,S_u^{(m-1)}(Y_i\mid X_i)}{1-p^{(m-1)}(Z_i)+p^{(m-1)}(Z_i)\,S_u^{(m-1)}(Y_i\mid X_i)}$$

M-step: Update \(\alpha\) via weighted logistic regression (weights \(w_i\)); update \(\beta,S_{u,0}\) via weighted partial likelihood. Iterate until convergence. Variances by bootstrap.

SA5. Interpret \(\hat\alpha_3=2.777\) and \(\hat\beta_3=2.469\) from the logistic/Cox PH cure model. Explain the distinction between long-term and short-term effects.

Incidence (\(\hat\alpha_3=2.777\)) — long-term effect: The odds ratio for being susceptible in Stage 4 vs Stage 1 is \(\exp(2.777)\approx16.07\). Stage 4 patients have approximately 16 times the odds of eventually experiencing the event, meaning their cure probability drops from ~81% to ~21%.

Latency (\(\hat\beta_3=2.469\)) — short-term effect: Among susceptible (uncured) patients only, Stage 4 patients face a hazard \(\exp(2.469)\approx11.81\) times that of susceptible Stage 1 patients.

Key distinction: The mixture cure model separates whether someone will ever experience the event (incidence) from how quickly susceptible individuals progress (latency). A standard Cox PH model conflates these two effects into a single hazard ratio.
