3.2 Summarizing the Posterior by Probabilities

The simplest characterization of a posterior \(p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) is to compute probabilities of the form \(P(a < \theta < b \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) for chosen \(a\) and \(b\).

Stroke Study (Example III.1): Posterior is \(\text{Beta}(19, 133)\). We can compute, e.g., \(P(\theta > 0.20 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = 0.0062\) and \(P(\theta < 0.088 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = 0.072\). This shows the incidence is very unlikely to exceed the interim rate of 0.20.

While useful, posterior probabilities alone are not sufficient — we also need point and interval estimates, analogous to frequentist analyses.

3.3.2 Posterior Interval Estimation (Credible Intervals)

A 100(1 − α)% credible interval \([a, b]\) satisfies \(P(a \le \theta \le b \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = 1 - \alpha\). Two important types exist.

Equal Tail Credible Interval

\[P(\theta \le a \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = \alpha/2 \quad \text{and} \quad P(\theta \ge b \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = \alpha/2\]

  • Constructed using the posterior CDF only (quantile function).
  • ✅ Invariant under monotone transformations: if \([a,b]\) is an equal tail CI for \(\theta\), then \([h(a), h(b)]\) is an equal tail CI for \(\psi = h(\theta)\).
  • Some values inside may have lower density than values outside — a limitation.

Highest Posterior Density (HPD) Interval

For all \(\theta_1 \in [a,b]\) and \(\theta_2 \notin [a,b]\): \(p(\theta_1 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \ge p(\theta_2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\)

  • Contains the most plausible parameter values — constructed by a horizontal line intersecting the posterior density.
  • ✅ Shortest interval with \(P(a \le \theta \le b \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = 1 - \alpha\).
  • Requires the posterior density (PDF), not just the CDF.
  • ❌ Not invariant under monotone transformations (e.g., the HPD for \(\theta\) does not map to the HPD for \(\log \theta\)).
Coincidence: For a unimodal symmetric posterior, the equal tail CI and the HPD interval are identical.

Bayesian vs. Frequentist Interpretation

A 95% credible interval contains the 95% most plausible parameter values a posteriori. A 95% frequentist CI either contains or does not contain the true value for any given dataset — the 95% refers to long-run coverage, not a probability about the current data.

3.4 Predictive Distributions

Given observed data \(\boldsymbol{y}\) and a future observation \(\tilde{y}\), the posterior predictive distribution (PPD) integrates out parameter uncertainty:

\[p(\tilde{y} \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = \int p(\tilde{y} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\, d\theta\]

This relies on hierarchical independence: \(p(\tilde{y} \mid \theta, \boldsymbol{y}) = p(\tilde{y} \mid \theta)\) — given the true \(\theta\), past data cannot inform the future observation.

The prior predictive distribution is \(p(\tilde{y}) = \int p(\tilde{y} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta\) — this is also the denominator of Bayes' theorem (marginal/integrated likelihood).

Key point: The Bayesian PPD automatically accounts for parameter uncertainty, resulting in more variability than the frequentist plug-in predictive distribution \(p(\tilde{y} \mid \hat{\theta})\).

3.5 Exchangeability

The classical assumption of conditional independence given \(\theta\) leads — when \(\theta\) is given a prior — to a weaker unconditional concept:

Definition: \(y_1, \ldots, y_n\) are exchangeable if their joint distribution is invariant to any permutation \(\pi\):

\[p(y_1, \ldots, y_n) = p(y_{\pi(1)}, \ldots, y_{\pi(n)})\]

PropertyIndependence → Exchangeability?Exchangeability → Independence?
General relation✅ Yes (if identical marginals)❌ No, in general
de Finetti's Representation Theorem (Theorem 3.5.1): If \(y_1, y_2, \ldots\) is an infinitely exchangeable sequence of binary random variables, then there exists a CDF \(Q\) such that: \[p(y_1, \ldots, y_n) = \int_0^1 \prod_{i=1}^n \theta^{y_i}(1-\theta)^{1-y_i}\, dQ(\theta)\] This is equivalent to assuming the \(y_i\) are conditionally i.i.d. Bernoulli(\(\theta\)) with \(\theta\) drawn from prior \(Q\). Hence exchangeability naturally leads to a Bayesian model.

Partial/conditional exchangeability: Subjects may be exchangeable within subgroups (e.g., within age–gender strata) but not across groups.

3.6 Normal Approximation to the Posterior (Bayesian CLT)

When a non-conjugate prior is used, or when computation is complex, a normal approximation to the posterior is valid for large sample sizes.

Theorem 3.6.1 (Bayesian CLT): Under suitable regularity conditions, as \(n \to \infty\):

\[p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \xrightarrow{\;d\;} N\!\left(\hat{\theta},\; \sigma^2_{\hat{\theta}}\right)\]

where \(\hat\theta\) is the MLE and \(\;\sigma^2_{\hat\theta} = \displaystyle -\!\left(\frac{d^2 \log L(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})}{d\theta^2}\bigg|_{\theta = \hat\theta}\right)^{-1}\) (inverse observed Fisher information).

  • Laplace proved this for inverse probability in 1785 — before the classical CLT.
  • For large \(n\): the likelihood dominates the prior, so the posterior is essentially determined by the data regardless of the prior chosen.
  • Analytical posteriors can only always be derived with a conjugate prior; otherwise, numerical methods are required.
Practical importance: The Bayesian CLT enables Bayesian inference via a normal approximation when conjugate analysis is not possible, especially useful in complex models.

3.7 Numerical Techniques to Determine the Posterior

3.7.1 Numerical Integration

The general integral \(\int_a^b f(\theta)\, d\theta \approx \sum_{m=1}^{M+1} w_m f(\theta^*_m)\) can be approximated by several methods:

  • Rectangle (midpoint) rule: \(w_m = \delta\) (equal spacing)
  • Trapezoidal rule: Piecewise linear approximation
  • Simpson's rule: Piecewise quadratic — more accurate
  • Gaussian quadrature: Uses Hermite polynomial roots as grid points; exactly reproduces polynomials of degree \(\le 2M-1\)
  • Laplace approximation: For one quadrature point — approximates \(\int e^{h(\theta)}\, d\theta \approx (2\pi)^{d/2} |A|^{1/2} e^{h(\hat\theta_M)}\), where \(A\) is minus the inverse Hessian at the mode.

3.7.2 Sampling from the Posterior

In Monte Carlo integration, if \(\{\theta^1, \ldots, \theta^K\}\) are sampled from \(p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\), then:

\[\int t(\theta)\, p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\, d\theta \approx \bar{t}_K = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K t(\theta^k)\]

Monte Carlo error: \(s_t / \sqrt{K}\), where \(s_t\) is the SD of the sampled \(t(\theta^k)\) values.

3.8 Bayesian Hypothesis Testing

3.8.1 Credible Intervals and the Contour Probability

For \(H_0 : \theta = \theta_0\): reject if \(\theta_0\) lies outside the 95% credible interval.

More formally, the contour probability \(p_B\) is defined as:

\[P\!\bigl(p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y}) > p(\theta_0 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\bigr) \equiv 1 - p_B\]

\(p_B\) = complement of the AUC of the smallest HPD interval containing \(\theta_0\). Small \(p_B\) means \(H_0\) is not well-supported a posteriori.

The contour probability is the Bayesian analogue of the two-sided frequentist P-value (first suggested by Box & Tiao, 1973).

3.8.2 The Bayes Factor

\[\text{BF}(\boldsymbol{y}) = \frac{p(\boldsymbol{y} \mid H_0)}{p(\boldsymbol{y} \mid H_a)}\]

Transforms prior odds to posterior odds: \(\dfrac{p(H_0 \mid \boldsymbol{y})}{p(H_a \mid \boldsymbol{y})} = \text{BF}(\boldsymbol{y}) \times \dfrac{p(H_0)}{p(H_a)}\)

BF(y) valueJeffreys' classification (evidence for H₀)
BF(y) > 100Decisive
32 < BF(y) ≤ 100Very strong

3.8.3 Bayesian vs. Frequentist Hypothesis Testing

P-value vs. posterior probability: The P-value does not equal the probability that \(H_0\) is true (the "P-value fallacy"). The Bayesian posterior probability \(p(H_0 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) does provide this.

Minimum Bayes factor (Gaussian case): \(\text{BF}_{\min}(\boldsymbol{y}) = \exp(-0.5\, z^2)\)

When P-value = 0.05 (z = 1.96): \(\text{BF}_{\min} = 0.15\), corresponding to minimum posterior probability 0.204 for \(H_0\) under equal prior probabilities. P-values exaggerate evidence against \(H_0\) in a simple hypothesis test.

Jeffreys–Lindley–Bartlett Paradox: For a sharp null hypothesis (e.g., \(H_0: \theta = \theta_0\) vs. \(H_a: \theta \ne \theta_0\)) with a diffuse alternative prior, it is possible for a small P-value to coexist with a Bayes factor that favors \(H_0\) — especially at large sample sizes. This occurs because the Bayesian test compares averaged likelihoods (not maximized), and averaging over an unrealistically broad alternative weakens the evidence for \(H_a\).

Short Answer 1 of 8 — Posterior Mode Properties
State the three key properties of the posterior mode \(\hat\theta_M\). Why does it equal the MLE when the prior is flat?
Answer:
The three properties of the posterior mode are:
  1. Computationally simple: Computing \(\hat\theta_M\) only requires maximizing \(L(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,p(\theta)\) — no full normalization of the posterior is needed.
  2. Equals MLE for flat priors: When \(p(\theta) \propto c\), then \(p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \propto L(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\), so maximizing the posterior is identical to maximizing the likelihood. Hence \(\hat\theta_M = \hat\theta_\text{MLE}\).
  3. Not transformation invariant: For a monotone transformation \(h\), the posterior mode of \(\psi = h(\theta)\) is generally not equal to \(h(\hat\theta_M)\), i.e., \(\hat\psi_M \ne h(\hat\theta_M)\).
\[\hat\theta_M = \arg\max_\theta\; p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = \arg\max_\theta\; L(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,p(\theta)\]
Short Answer 2 of 8 — Equal Tail CI vs. HPD Interval
Explain the difference between the equal tail credible interval and the HPD interval. State two advantages of each, and describe when they coincide.
Answer:

Equal tail CI \([a, b]\): satisfies \(F(a) = \alpha/2\) and \(1 - F(b) = \alpha/2\). Advantages: (1) requires only the posterior CDF (quantile function), easy to compute; (2) invariant under monotone transformations — if \([a,b]\) is the equal tail CI for \(\theta\), then \([h(a), h(b)]\) is the equal tail CI for \(\psi = h(\theta)\). Disadvantage: some values inside the interval can have lower density than some values outside.

HPD interval \([a, b]\): every point inside has higher posterior density than every point outside. Advantages: (1) it is the shortest interval with \(P(a \le \theta \le b \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = 1 - \alpha\); (2) contains only the most plausible (highest density) values. Disadvantage: requires the posterior PDF and numerical optimization; not invariant under non-linear monotone transformations.

They coincide when the posterior is unimodal and symmetric (e.g., a normal posterior), because equal tails automatically yield the highest density region.

Short Answer 3 of 8 — Deriving the Binomial–Beta PPD
Derive the posterior predictive distribution for a future binomial count \(\tilde y\) in \(m\) trials, given a \(\text{Beta}(\bar\alpha, \bar\beta)\) posterior for \(\theta\). Name the resulting distribution.
Answer:

Starting from the PPD formula \(p(\tilde y \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = \int_0^1 p(\tilde y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,d\theta\), substitute the Binomial likelihood for \(\tilde y\) and the Beta posterior:

p(ỹ | y) = ∫₀¹ C(m, ỹ) θ^ỹ (1−θ)^(m−ỹ) · [θ^(ᾱ−1)(1−θ)^(β̄−1) / B(ᾱ,β̄)] dθ = C(m,ỹ) / B(ᾱ,β̄) · ∫₀¹ θ^(ỹ+ᾱ−1)(1−θ)^(m−ỹ+β̄−1) dθ = C(m,ỹ) · B(ỹ+ᾱ, m−ỹ+β̄) / B(ᾱ, β̄)

This defines the Beta-Binomial distribution \(\text{BB}(m, \bar\alpha, \bar\beta)\), first derived by Laplace. It exhibits more variability than the binomial \(\text{Bin}(m, \hat\theta)\) from the frequentist approach, because it accounts for uncertainty in \(\theta\).

Short Answer 4 of 8 — de Finetti's Representation Theorem
State de Finetti's Representation Theorem and explain its significance for justifying the Bayesian approach.
Answer:

Theorem (de Finetti, 1937): Let \(y_1, y_2, \ldots\) be an infinitely exchangeable sequence of binary random variables. Then there exists a cumulative distribution function \(Q\) such that:

\[p(y_1, \ldots, y_n) = \int_0^1 \prod_{i=1}^n \theta^{y_i}(1-\theta)^{1-y_i}\, dQ(\theta)\]

where \(Q(\theta) = \lim_{n\to\infty} F(s_n/n \le \theta)\), with \(s_n = y_1 + \cdots + y_n\).

Significance: The theorem shows that exchangeability — a natural, weaker assumption than independence — is mathematically equivalent to a Bayesian hierarchical model: the observations are conditionally i.i.d. Bernoulli(\(\theta\)) given \(\theta\), and \(\theta\) has prior distribution \(Q\). Therefore, assuming that subjects in an experiment are "similar" (exchangeable) immediately leads to a Bayesian model combining a likelihood with a prior distribution, providing a philosophical justification for the Bayesian paradigm.

Short Answer 5 of 8 — Accept–Reject Algorithm
Describe the accept–reject (AR) sampling algorithm. What is required for it to be valid, and how is its efficiency measured?
Answer:

The AR algorithm generates samples from a target distribution \(p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) using a proposal distribution \(q(\theta)\).

Requirements: There must exist a finite constant \(A < \infty\) such that \(p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y}) < A\,q(\theta)\) for all \(\theta\) (the "envelope condition"). Also, the support of \(p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) must be contained within the support of \(q(\theta)\).

Algorithm:

  1. Sample \(\tilde\theta \sim q(\theta)\) and independently \(u \sim U(0,1)\).
  2. Accept \(\tilde\theta\) if \(u \le p(\tilde\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,/\,[A\,q(\tilde\theta)]\); otherwise reject.
  3. Repeat until \(K\) accepted values are obtained.

It can be shown that accepted values form a valid sample from \(p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\), and this holds even when only \(p(\theta)\,L(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) (the unnormalized posterior) is available.

Efficiency: The probability that \(\tilde\theta\) is accepted equals \(1/A\). The closer \(A\) is to 1, the more efficient the algorithm. Choosing \(q(\theta)\) as close as possible to \(p(\theta \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) minimizes \(A\) and maximizes efficiency.

Short Answer 6 of 8 — The Bayes Factor
Define the Bayes factor. State Jeffreys' classification. How does the Bayes factor relate prior odds to posterior odds? What role does the prior play in the composite alternative hypothesis case?
Answer:

The Bayes factor \(\text{BF}(\boldsymbol{y})\) is the ratio of the averaged (marginal) likelihoods under \(H_0\) and \(H_a\):

\[\text{BF}(\boldsymbol{y}) = \frac{p(\boldsymbol{y} \mid H_0)}{p(\boldsymbol{y} \mid H_a)} = \frac{\int p(\boldsymbol{y} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta \mid H_0)\,d\theta}{\int p(\boldsymbol{y} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta \mid H_a)\,d\theta}\]

It transforms prior odds into posterior odds: posterior odds = BF(y) × prior odds. When \(\text{BF}(\boldsymbol{y}) > 1\), the data increase the odds for \(H_0\); when \(< 1\), they favor \(H_a\).

Jeffreys' classification: BF > 100 = decisive; 32–100 = very strong; 10–32 = strong; 3.2–10 = substantial; 1–3.2 = not worth more than a bare mention.

Role of the prior under composite \(H_a\): When \(H_a\) is composite (e.g., \(\theta \ne \theta_0\)), the averaged likelihood under \(H_a\) depends on the chosen prior \(p(\theta \mid H_a)\). Different prior choices yield different Bayes factors — reflecting that the prior specification is part of defining the hypothesis. This contrasts with the frequentist likelihood ratio test, which uses maximized (not averaged) likelihoods.

Short Answer 7 of 8 — Jeffreys–Lindley–Bartlett Paradox
Explain the Jeffreys–Lindley–Bartlett paradox. What causes it, and what does it imply about the comparison between P-values and the Bayes factor for sharp null hypotheses?
Answer:

The Jeffreys–Lindley–Bartlett paradox describes a situation where a small P-value rejects \(H_0\) in a frequentist analysis, yet the Bayes factor favors \(H_0\).

Cause: The paradox arises from the different ways frequentist and Bayesian tests compare hypotheses. The frequentist likelihood ratio test compares maximized likelihoods under \(H_0\) and \(H_a\). The Bayes factor, by contrast, compares averaged likelihoods. When the alternative prior \(p(\theta \mid H_a)\) is very diffuse (spread over a large, unrealistic range of \(\theta\) values), the average likelihood under \(H_a\) is diluted, weakening evidence for \(H_a\) and potentially making BF favour \(H_0\).

Example (Lindley): For \(y_i \sim N(\theta, \sigma^2)\) testing \(H_0: \theta = \theta_0\) with prior \(p(\theta) = N(\theta_0, \sigma^2)\), the posterior probability of \(H_0\) increases from 0.33 to 0.82 as \(n\) grows from 5 to 1000 (holding \(z_\text{obs} = 1.96\), P-value = 0.05). This demonstrates that in a simple hypothesis test, P-values exaggerate evidence against \(H_0\) relative to the Bayesian posterior probability. For P = 0.05, the minimum Bayes factor is \(\text{BF}_{\min} = e^{-0.5 z^2} = 0.15\), and the minimum posterior probability of \(H_0\) (under equal priors) is 0.204 — much less extreme than what 0.05 suggests.

Short Answer 8 of 8 — Exchangeability in Practice
Define exchangeability and explain how it differs from independence. Give a concrete example of a sequence of random variables that is exchangeable but not independent. Why is the concept of exchangeability particularly important in clinical trial settings?
Answer:

Exchangeability: Random variables \(y_1, \ldots, y_n\) are exchangeable if their joint distribution is invariant to any permutation of indices: \(p(y_1,\ldots,y_n) = p(y_{\pi(1)},\ldots,y_{\pi(n)})\) for any permutation \(\pi\).

Difference from independence: Independence (with identical marginals) implies exchangeability — but the converse is false. The product and the integral in \(p(y_1,\ldots,y_n) = \int \prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta\) cannot be exchanged, so variables that are conditionally independent given \(\theta\) are marginally dependent (and exchangeable). Independence is a stronger condition.

Concrete example: Sampling without replacement from an urn with 10 red and 5 white marbles: \(R_1, \ldots, R_{15}\) are exchangeable (\(P(R_i) = 10/15\) for all \(i\)) but not independent, because once a marble is drawn, the composition of the urn changes. With replacement, they are both exchangeable and independent.

Clinical trial importance: In a sequential clinical trial, patients enrolled over time may be considered exchangeable if there is no systematic trend in recruitment (e.g., no change in patient profile over time). Exchangeability licenses the use of a prior distribution \(Q\) (via de Finetti) and thus the full Bayesian framework. However, differences in patient characteristics (age, gender, disease severity) can make patients non-exchangeable, necessitating conditioning on covariates — leading to the concept of conditional exchangeability.
