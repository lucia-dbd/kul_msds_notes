4.2 Joint versus Marginal Posterior Inference

With a d-dimensional parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_d)^T\), Bayes theorem yields the joint posterior:

\[p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = \frac{L(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\int L(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}}\]

The posterior mode \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_M\) and posterior mean \(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\) generalize directly. The posterior median is not uniquely defined in dimensions \(d > 1\). The HPD region \(R\) of content \((1-\alpha)\) satisfies: (a) \(P(\boldsymbol{\theta} \in R \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = 1-\alpha\) and (b) for \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_1 \in R\) and \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_2 \notin R\): \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_1 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \ge p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\).

Marginal Posterior Distribution

\[p(\theta_1 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = \int p(\theta_1, \theta_2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,d\theta_2 = \int p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2, \boldsymbol{y})\,p(\theta_2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,d\theta_2\]

The marginal posterior of \(\theta_1\) is a weighted average of conditional posteriors \(p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2, \boldsymbol{y})\), weighted by the marginal posterior \(p(\theta_2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\). When \(\theta_2\) are nuisance parameters, marginalizing automatically incorporates uncertainty about \(\theta_2\) — analogous to integrating out, not maximizing.

Key distinction from frequentist approach: The classical profile likelihood removes nuisance parameters by maximization: \(pL(\theta_1) = \max_{\theta_2} L(\theta_1, \theta_2)\). The Bayesian approach removes nuisance parameters by integration. Both "deal with uncertainty" but in fundamentally different ways: Bayesian via averaging, frequentist via maximizing.
Important result: The posterior mean of \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) equals the vector of marginal posterior means — but this result does NOT hold for the posterior mode.

4.3 The Normal Distribution with μ and σ² Unknown

For \(\boldsymbol{y} = \{y_1, \ldots, y_n\}\) i.i.d. from \(\mathrm{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), the likelihood is:

\[L(\mu, \sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{n/2}} \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \mu)^2\right]\]

Using \(\sum(y_i-\mu)^2 = (n-1)s^2 + n(\bar{y}-\mu)^2\), so \(\bar{y}\) and \(s^2\) are the sufficient statistics for \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\).

Case 1: Noninformative (NI) Prior — \(p(\mu, \sigma^2) \propto \sigma^{-2}\)

Joint posterior: \(\displaystyle p(\mu, \sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \propto \frac{1}{\sigma^{n+2}} \exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left[(n-1)s^2 + n(\bar{y}-\mu)^2\right]\right\}\)

Note: \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) are independent a priori but dependent a posteriori.

Conditional/Marginal PosteriorDistributionKey Implication
\(p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y})\)\(\mathrm{N}(\bar{y},\; \sigma^2/n)\)Exact normal regardless of \(n\)
\(p(\mu \mid \boldsymbol{y})\)\(t_{n-1}(\bar{y},\; s^2/n)\)\((\mu - \bar{y})\,/\,(s/\sqrt{n}) \sim t_{n-1}\)
\(p(\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\)\(\mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(n-1,\; s^2)\)\((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2(n-1)\)

The joint posterior is the normal-scaled-inverse chi-squared distribution N-Inv-\(\chi^2(\bar{y},\, n,\, n-1,\, s^2)\).

Posterior Summary Measures (NI Prior)

ParameterLocationVariance95% Equal Tail CI
\(\mu\) All equal to \(\bar{y}\) (symmetric) \(\dfrac{n-1}{n(n-2)}s^2\) \(\bigl[\bar{y} - t_{0.025;n-1}\,s/\sqrt{n},\;\; \bar{y} + t_{0.025;n-1}\,s/\sqrt{n}\bigr]\)
\(\sigma^2\) Mean: \(\dfrac{n-1}{n-3}s^2\); Mode: \(\dfrac{n-1}{n+1}s^2\); Median: \(\dfrac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{0.5,n-1}}\) \(\dfrac{2(n-1)^2}{(n-3)^2(n-5)}s^4\) \(\left[\dfrac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{0.975,n-1}},\;\; \dfrac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{0.025,n-1}}\right]\)
Important coincidence: The credible intervals for both \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) under the NI prior are identical to the corresponding classical 95% confidence intervals. This demonstrates that with appropriate (NI) priors, a Bayesian analysis can reproduce frequentist results.

PPD (NI prior): \(\tilde{y} \mid \boldsymbol{y} \sim t_{n-1}\!\left(\bar{y},\; s^2\!\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{n}\right)\right)\). This is wider than the posterior of \(\mu\) because it also accounts for the variability of individual observations.

Example IV.1 (ALP study, \(n = 250\)): Posterior for \(\mu\): all location measures = 7.11, variance = 0.0075, 95% CI = [6.94, 7.28]. For \(\sigma^2\): mean = 1.88, mode = 1.85, median = 1.87, 95% equal tail CI = [1.58, 2.24], 95% HPD = [1.56, 2.22]. PPD: \(t_{249}(7.11, 1.37)\), 95% normal range for \(y\) = [4.41, 9.80].

Case 2: Historical (Conjugate) Prior — N-Inv-\(\chi^2(\mu_0, \kappa_0, \nu_0, \sigma_0^2)\)

The prior structure is \(\mu \mid \sigma^2 \sim \mathrm{N}(\mu_0, \sigma^2/\kappa_0)\) and \(\sigma^2 \sim \mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(\nu_0, \sigma_0^2)\), where \(\mu_0 = \bar{y}_0\), \(\kappa_0 = n_0\), \(\nu_0 = n_0-1\), \(\sigma_0^2 = s_0^2\). Combining with the likelihood yields:

\[\bar{\mu} = \frac{\kappa_0\mu_0 + n\bar{y}}{\kappa_0 + n}, \qquad \bar{\kappa} = \kappa_0 + n, \qquad \bar{\nu} = \nu_0 + n\]

\[\bar{\nu}\bar{\sigma}^2 = \nu_0\sigma_0^2 + (n-1)s^2 + \frac{\kappa_0\,n}{\kappa_0+n}(\bar{y}-\mu_0)^2\]

The posterior mean \(\bar{\mu}\) is again a weighted average of prior mean and sample mean — exhibiting shrinkage toward the prior mean. The posterior precision of \(\mu\) is not automatically increased when prior and data are in conflict (see Example IV.2).

Example IV.2 (Conflicting priors): Using retrospective data (prior mean = 5.25) versus prospective sample mean (7.11): posterior variance of \(\mu\) = 0.0083 exceeds 0.0075 (NI prior). The prior information increased uncertainty because the prior mean was remote from the sample mean. Expression (4.13) shows the posterior variance includes a term \(\kappa_0 n/(\kappa_0+n)(\bar{y}-\mu_0)^2\) that grows with the prior-data discrepancy.

Case 3: Expert Knowledge — Product Prior (Non-Conjugate)

Taking \(\mu \sim \mathrm{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\) and \(\sigma^2 \sim \mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(\nu_0, \tau_0^2)\) independently yields the product prior \(\mathrm{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\,\mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(\nu_0, \tau_0^2)\). This is not a normal-scaled-inverse chi-squared distribution — in the conjugate prior, \(\mu\) is dependent on \(\sigma^2\), but the product prior makes them independent a priori. Consequently:

  • \(p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{N}(\bar{\mu}, \bar{\sigma}^2)\) is tractable (formula 4.18).
  • \(p(\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) has no closed-form analytical expression — the integration constant is unknown.
  • The posterior must be approximated numerically (e.g., grid, sampling).

4.4 Multivariate Distributions

4.4.1 Multivariate Normal (MVN)

\[p(\boldsymbol{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T \Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right]\]

Notation: \(\mathrm{N}_p(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)\). Key property: all marginal and conditional distributions of an MVN are again normal, as are all linear combinations of the components.

Multivariate Student's t-distribution

Allows heavier tails than MVN. Notation: \(T_\nu(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)\). Arises as the marginal posterior of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) in Bayesian linear regression with NI prior.

Wishart Distribution

The multivariate extension of the \(\chi^2\)-distribution. If \(S^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2\), then \(S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2(n-1)\). When \(S^2\) is replaced by the matrix \(\mathbf{S} = \sum_{i=1}^n (\boldsymbol{y}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{y}})(\boldsymbol{y}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{y}})^T\), the Wishart distribution \(\mathrm{Wishart}(\Sigma, \nu)\) with \(\nu = n-1\) degrees of freedom is obtained.

\[p(\mathbf{S} \mid \nu, \Sigma) = c\,|\Sigma|^{-\nu/2}\,|\mathbf{S}|^{(\nu-p-1)/2} \exp\!\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{S})\right]\]

The inverse Wishart: \(R \sim \mathrm{IW}(\Sigma, \nu)\) if \(R^{-1} \sim \mathrm{Wishart}(\Sigma, \nu)\). It is the conjugate prior for \(\Sigma\) in the MVN model.

4.4.2 The Multinomial Distribution

\[p(\boldsymbol{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{n!}{y_1!\, y_2!\cdots y_k!}\prod_{j=1}^k \theta_j^{y_j}\]

with \(n = \sum_{j=1}^k y_j\), \(\theta_j > 0\), and \(\sum_{j=1}^k \theta_j = 1\).

  • Binomial is a special case with \(k=2\).
  • Marginal distribution of \(y_j\) is \(\mathrm{Bin}(n, \theta_j)\).
  • Conjugate prior: Dirichlet \(\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\), yielding posterior \(\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \boldsymbol{y})\).
  • 1-D marginals of \(\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\) are Beta distributions: \(\theta_{ij} \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_{ij},\, \sum_{kl}\alpha_{kl} - \alpha_{ij})\).
Sampling from Dirichlet (key result): If \(W_{ij} \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_{ij}, 1)\) independently and \(T = \sum_{ij} W_{ij}\), then \(Z_{ij} = W_{ij}/T \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\). This provides an efficient way to sample the cross-ratio \(\psi = \theta_{11}\theta_{22}/(\theta_{12}\theta_{21})\) from its posterior.
Example IV.3 (Young adults, \(n = 501\)): Noninformative Dirichlet Dir(1,1,1,1) prior; posterior median for \(\psi\) = 1.299, 95% equal tail CI = [0.839, 2.014] — no conclusive evidence of positive association.

4.5 Frequentist Properties of Bayesian Inference

Although it is not a primary Bayesian concern whether procedures work well if a study is repeated, it is important to understand the long-run properties of Bayesian interval estimators.

  • There is no guarantee that a 100(1−α)% credible interval achieves correct frequentist coverage in small samples.
  • For large samples, the Bayesian CLT ensures asymptotic correct coverage when there is a finite number of parameters.
  • For the NI prior on the normal model (Section 4.3.1), the credible intervals exactly coincide with classical 95% CIs — so coverage is guaranteed.
  • With strong subjective priors, coverage can deviate from 100(1−α)%; however, a well-chosen prior may yield better (higher) coverage than the nominal level.
Agresti & Min (2005) findings for 2×2 tables: For the risk difference, all NI priors gave approximately correct frequentist coverage. For the risk ratio and odds ratio, the uniform prior gave too-low coverage. The recommendation is to use the Jeffreys prior for binomial parameters when correct frequentist coverage is desired.

4.6 Sampling from the Posterior: The Method of Composition

The Method of Composition uses the factorization of the joint posterior into marginal and conditional distributions:

\[p(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_d \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = p(\theta_d \mid \boldsymbol{y})\; p(\theta_{d-1} \mid \theta_d, \boldsymbol{y})\;\cdots\; p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_d, \ldots, \theta_2, \boldsymbol{y})\]

Algorithm: Repeat \(K\) times: (1) sample \(\tilde{\theta}_d\) from \(p(\theta_d \mid \boldsymbol{y})\); (2) sample \(\tilde{\theta}_{d-1}\) from \(p(\theta_{d-1} \mid \tilde{\theta}_d, \boldsymbol{y})\); …; etc. This yields a sample of \(K\) independent observations from the joint posterior, and automatically from all marginal posteriors.

PropertyDetails
OutputIndependent sample from the joint posterior AND all marginals
Key advantageUseful when marginal sampling is hard but conditional sampling is easy
OrderingAny ordering is valid in principle; choose the one yielding simplest sampling
For PPDAdd a final step: sample \(\tilde{y}\) from \(p(\tilde{y} \mid \tilde{\theta}, \boldsymbol{y})\)
LimitationCannot handle non-standard conditional distributions without auxiliary algorithms

MoC for Normal Posterior — NI Prior (Case 1)

To sample from \(p(\mu, \sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\), repeat for \(k = 1, \ldots, K\):

  1. Sample \(\tilde{\chi}_k \sim \chi^2(n-1)\), then set \((\tilde{\sigma}^2)_k = (n-1)s^2/\tilde{\chi}_k\)
  2. Sample \(\tilde{\mu}_k \sim \mathrm{N}(\bar{y},\; (\tilde{\sigma}^2)_k/n)\)

The resulting \(\tilde{\mu}_1, \ldots, \tilde{\mu}_K\) form an independent sample from the marginal \(t_{n-1}(\bar{y}, s^2/n)\). For the PPD, add step 3: sample \(\tilde{y}_k \sim \mathrm{N}(\tilde{\mu}_k,\; (\tilde{\sigma}^2)_k)\).

Expert knowledge case (Case 3): Since \(p(\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) has no closed form with the product prior, standard routines cannot sample from it directly. Weighted resampling (importance sampling/SIR from Chapter 3) is used, with an approximating distribution (e.g., an Inv-\(\chi^2\) with matched mean and variance) as the proposal.
Example IV.5 (NI prior, \(K = 1000\)): Posterior mean (95% MC CI) for \(\mu\): 7.11 ([7.106, 7.117]); for \(\sigma^2\): 1.88 ([1.869, 1.890]). 95% equal tail CI for \(\mu\) = [6.95, 7.27]; for \(\sigma^2\) = [1.58, 2.23]. To increase accuracy, simply increase \(K\).

4.7 Bayesian Linear Regression Models

4.7.1 Model Setup

Model: \(\boldsymbol{y} = \boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\), \(\;\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathrm{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \sigma^2 \boldsymbol{I})\)

Likelihood: \(L(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y}, \boldsymbol{X}) = \dfrac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{n/2}} \exp\!\left[-\dfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^T(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})\right]\)

MLE = LSE: \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{y}\); \(\quad s^2 = S/(n-d-1)\) where \(S = (\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^T(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\)

4.7.2 Noninformative Prior \(p(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma^2) \propto \sigma^{-2}\)

DistributionForm
Joint: \(p(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\)\(\mathrm{N}_{d+1}\!\bigl[\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\, \sigma^2(\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\bigr] \times \mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(\sigma^2 \mid n-d-1, s^2)\)
Conditional: \(p(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y})\)\(\mathrm{N}_{d+1}\!\bigl[\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\, \sigma^2(\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\bigr]\)
Marginal: \(p(\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\)\(\mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(\sigma^2 \mid n-d-1, s^2)\)
Marginal: \(p(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\)\(T_{n-d-1}\!\bigl[\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\, s^2(\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\bigr]\) — multivariate \(t\)-distribution

4.7.3 Posterior Summary Measures (NI Prior)

  • Mean/mode/median of \(\beta_j\) = LSE \(\hat{\beta}_j\) (coincides with frequentist estimate).
  • 95% HPD interval for \(\beta_j\): \(\hat{\beta}_j \pm s\,(\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})_{jj}^{-1/2}\,t(0.025;\, n-d-1)\) — same as classical.
  • Mode of \(\sigma^2\): \(\dfrac{n-d-1}{n-d+1}s^2\) (not equal to MLE \(s^2\)).
  • Mean of \(\sigma^2\): \(\dfrac{n-d-1}{n-d-3}s^2\).
  • HPD region for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\): \(C_\alpha(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \left\{\boldsymbol{\beta}: (\boldsymbol{\beta}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})^T(\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) \le d\,s^2\,F_\alpha(d+1, n-d-1)\right\}\)
  • PPD for future \(\tilde{y}\) with covariate vector \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\): \(t_{n-d-1}\) with location \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^T\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) and scale \(s^2(1 + \tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^T(\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\); equivalently, \(\dfrac{\tilde{y} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^T\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{s\sqrt{1 + \tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^T(\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}} \sim t_{n-d-1}\).
Method of Composition for regression (2 steps): (1) Sample \(\tilde{\sigma}^2 \sim \mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(n-d-1, s^2)\); (2) Sample \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \sim \mathrm{N}_{d+1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\, \tilde{\sigma}^2(\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})^{-1})\). The 2-step MoC is simpler than directly sampling from the multivariate \(t\)-distribution \(p(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\).
Example IV.8 (Osteoporosis, \(n = 234\)): MoC with \(K = 1000\): sampled mean \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (0.816, 0.0403)\). 95% equal tail CIs: \(\beta_0\): [0.594, 1.040]; \(\beta_1\): [0.0317, 0.0486]. Posterior correlation of \(\beta_0, \beta_1\) ≈ −0.99 (high collinearity reflected in ridge in joint posterior).

4.8 Bayesian Generalized Linear Models (BGLIM)

A GLIM extends linear regression to a wide class of models. It has three components:

ComponentDescriptionExample
Distributional part \(p(y \mid \theta; \phi) = \exp\!\left[\dfrac{y\theta - b(\theta)}{a(\phi)} + c(y;\phi)\right]\) — exponential family in canonical parameter \(\theta\). Mean: \(\mu = b'(\theta)\); Var: \(a(\phi)V(\mu)\) where \(V(\mu) = b''(\theta)\). Normal, Poisson, Bernoulli
Link function Monotone differentiable \(g\): \(g(\mu) = \eta = \boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{\beta}\). Canonical link: \(\eta = \theta\). Inverse: \(h = g^{-1}\). Identity, log, logit
Variance function \(V(\mu)\) describes how variance depends on the mean (via covariates through \(\mu\)). \(\phi\) is the dispersion/scale parameter. \(V(\mu)=1\), \(V(\mu)=\mu\), \(V(\mu)=\mu(1-\mu)\)
ModelDistributionLink\(\phi\)\(V(\mu)\)
Normal regression\(\mathrm{N}(\mu_i, \sigma^2)\)Identity: \(g(\mu)=\mu\)1\(\sigma^2\)
Poisson regression\(\mathrm{Poisson}(\mu_i)\)Log: \(g(\mu)=\log\mu\)1\(\mu\)
Logistic regression\(\mathrm{Bern}(\mu_i)\)Logit: \(g(\mu)=\log[\mu/(1-\mu)]\)1\(\mu(1-\mu)\)

A BGLIM is obtained by specifying a prior distribution on all parameters of the GLIM. Binary response models (especially logistic regression) constitute the most important subclass in medical applications. For complex models (censoring, truncation, random effects), analytical posteriors generally do not exist — Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods (Chapter 6) are needed.

Short Answer 1 of 8 — Marginal Posterior and Nuisance Parameters
Explain how the Bayesian approach handles nuisance parameters using marginal posterior distributions. How does this differ from the classical profile likelihood approach? Write the key formula for the marginal posterior of \(\theta_1\) and interpret it.
Answer:

The marginal posterior of \(\theta_1\) is obtained by integrating the joint posterior over the nuisance parameter \(\theta_2\):

\[p(\theta_1 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = \int p(\theta_1, \theta_2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,d\theta_2 = \int p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2, \boldsymbol{y})\,p(\theta_2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,d\theta_2\]

The second form shows the marginal posterior as a weighted average of conditional posteriors \(p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2, \boldsymbol{y})\), where the weights are determined by the marginal posterior of \(\theta_2\). This means inference on \(\theta_1\) automatically accounts for the uncertainty in \(\theta_2\).

The classical profile likelihood removes \(\theta_2\) by maximization: \(pL(\theta_1) = \max_{\theta_2} L(\theta_1, \theta_2)\), effectively substituting the best-fitting value of \(\theta_2\) for each fixed \(\theta_1\). This does not propagate uncertainty in \(\theta_2\).

The fundamental difference: Bayesian = integration (averaging over uncertainty); frequentist = maximization (conditioning on the "best" nuisance value). Bayesian marginalizing tends to produce wider and more honest intervals when there is genuine uncertainty about \(\theta_2\).

Short Answer 2 of 8 — Normal Model Under NI Prior
State all the key distributional results for the normal model under the NI prior \(p(\mu,\sigma^2) \propto \sigma^{-2}\): the joint posterior, both marginal posteriors, the key standardized pivots, and the 95% equal tail credible interval for \(\mu\). How do the credible intervals compare to classical CIs?
Answer:

Joint posterior:

\[p(\mu,\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \propto \frac{1}{\sigma^{n+2}} \exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left[(n-1)s^2 + n(\bar{y}-\mu)^2\right]\right\} = \text{N-Inv-}\chi^2(\bar{y},\, n,\, n-1,\, s^2)\]

Conditional posterior of \(\mu\): \(p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{N}(\bar{y}, \sigma^2/n)\)

Marginal posterior of \(\mu\): \(p(\mu \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = t_{n-1}(\bar{y}, s^2/n)\), with pivot \((\mu - \bar{y})/(s/\sqrt{n}) \sim t_{n-1}\)

Marginal posterior of \(\sigma^2\): \(p(\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(n-1, s^2)\), with pivot \((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2(n-1)\)

95% equal tail CI for \(\mu\):

\[\left[\bar{y} - t_{0.025;\,n-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},\;\; \bar{y} + t_{0.025;\,n-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\right]\]

Comparison: Both credible intervals (for \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\)) are identical to the corresponding classical 95% confidence intervals. This demonstrates that with the NI prior, the Bayesian analysis reproduces frequentist results, though the interpretation differs — the Bayesian CI is a probability statement about \(\mu\) given the data, whereas the frequentist CI has long-run coverage properties.

Note on posterior measures: Prior independence (\(\mu \perp \sigma^2\)) is lost a posteriori — they become dependent in the joint posterior.

Short Answer 3 of 8 — Effect of a Conflicting Informative Prior
Explain why, in Example IV.2 (ALP study), the posterior variance of \(\mu\) under the informative conjugate prior (0.0083) was larger than under the NI prior (0.0075). Use the relevant formula to support your answer.
Answer:

The posterior variance for \(\mu\) is derived from the posterior scale parameter \(\bar{\sigma}^2\), which is determined by:

\[\bar{\nu}\bar{\sigma}^2 = \nu_0\sigma_0^2 + (n-1)s^2 + \frac{\kappa_0\, n}{\kappa_0 + n}(\bar{y} - \mu_0)^2\]

The third term on the right — \(\dfrac{\kappa_0\, n}{\kappa_0 + n}(\bar{y} - \mu_0)^2\) — is a conflict term that penalizes discrepancy between the prior mean \(\mu_0\) and the sample mean \(\bar{y}\).

In Example IV.2, \(\mu_0 = 5.25\) (from historical data) while the prospective sample mean was \(\bar{y} = 7.11\), a difference of 1.86. With \(\kappa_0 = 65\) and \(n = 250\), this conflict term adds substantial variance to the posterior estimate of \(\sigma^2\), which in turn inflates the posterior variance of \(\mu = \bar{\sigma}^2/\bar{\kappa}\).

The intuition: when prior and data are in conflict, the posterior cannot resolve the disagreement efficiently — it becomes less certain about \(\mu\) than if no prior had been used. The NI prior simply avoids this penalty entirely.

Conclusion: An informative prior does not always reduce posterior uncertainty — it can increase it when the prior and data are discordant. This is an important distinction from the univariate known-\(\sigma^2\) case (Chapter 2) where an informative prior always increased precision.

Short Answer 4 of 8 — Method of Composition Algorithm
Describe the Method of Composition in general terms. State the key factorization it relies on, write out the algorithm for 3 steps (\(d = 3\)), and explain two key advantages over direct marginal sampling.
Answer:

The Method of Composition is a general step-by-step procedure to generate independent samples from a multivariate distribution by exploiting its factorization into marginal and conditional distributions.

Key factorization (for \(d\) parameters):

\[p(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_d \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = p(\theta_d \mid \boldsymbol{y})\; p(\theta_{d-1} \mid \theta_d, \boldsymbol{y})\;\cdots\; p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_d, \ldots, \theta_2, \boldsymbol{y})\]

Algorithm for \(d = 3\) (repeated \(K\) times, iteration \(k\)):

  1. Sample \(\tilde{\theta}_3^{(k)} \sim p(\theta_3 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\)
  2. Sample \(\tilde{\theta}_2^{(k)} \sim p(\theta_2 \mid \tilde{\theta}_3^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})\)
  3. Sample \(\tilde{\theta}_1^{(k)} \sim p(\theta_1 \mid \tilde{\theta}_3^{(k)}, \tilde{\theta}_2^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})\)

Two key advantages:

(1) Handles difficult marginals: If \(p(\theta_3 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) is not a standard distribution, it may still be accessible via importance sampling or ARS. The conditional \(p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_3, \boldsymbol{y})\) and \(p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2, \theta_3, \boldsymbol{y})\) are often much simpler (e.g., normal), making the sequential approach tractable even when direct multivariate sampling is not.

(2) Provides all marginal samples simultaneously: A single run of the MoC yields not only a sample from the joint posterior but automatically also from every marginal posterior \(p(\theta_j \mid \boldsymbol{y})\). In contrast, direct sampling from the multivariate \(t\)-distribution \(p(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) in regression requires specialized functions, while the two-step MoC (normal then Inv-\(\chi^2\)) uses only standard distributions.

Short Answer 5 of 8 — Bayesian Linear Regression with NI Prior
Write out the four posterior distributions for the Bayesian linear regression model with NI prior \(p(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma^2) \propto \sigma^{-2}\). State the posterior mean and mode of \(\sigma^2\) and explain why they are not equal to \(s^2\). Also describe the PPD for a scalar future observation \(\tilde{y}\).
Answer:

Four posterior distributions:

Joint: p(β, σ²|y) = N_{d+1}[β | β̂, σ²(X^T X)⁻¹] × Inv-χ²(σ² | n−d−1, s²) Conditional: p(β | σ², y) = N_{d+1}[β | β̂, σ²(X^T X)⁻¹] Marginal σ²: p(σ² | y) = Inv-χ²(σ² | n−d−1, s²) Marginal β: p(β | y) = T_{n−d−1}[β | β̂, s²(X^T X)⁻¹]

The jth marginal of \(p(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) is a univariate \(t_{n-d-1}\)-distribution with location \(\hat{\beta}_j\) and scale \(s(\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})_{jj}^{-1/2}\).

Posterior mode of \(\sigma^2\): \(\dfrac{n-d-1}{n-d+1}s^2\) < \(s^2\) — the mode undershoots \(s^2\) because the Inv-\(\chi^2\) density is right-skewed.

Posterior mean of \(\sigma^2\): \(\dfrac{n-d-1}{n-d-3}s^2\) > \(s^2\) — the mean overshoots \(s^2\) due to the heavy right tail of the Inv-\(\chi^2\). Both differ from the MLE \(s^2\) because the posterior is asymmetric.

PPD for \(\tilde{y}\): A \(t_{n-d-1}\)-distribution with location \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^T\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) and scale \(s^2\!\left(1 + \tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^T(\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\):

\[\frac{\tilde{y} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^T\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}{s\sqrt{1 + \tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^T(\boldsymbol{X}^T\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}} \sim t_{n-d-1}\]

This is the exact Bayesian prediction interval formula — and coincides with the classical prediction interval, illustrating again that the NI prior reproduces frequentist results.

Short Answer 6 of 8 — Product Prior vs. Conjugate Prior for Normal Model
Compare the N-Inv-\(\chi^2(\mu_0,\kappa_0,\nu_0,\sigma_0^2)\) conjugate prior and the product prior \(\mathrm{N}(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2)\,\mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(\nu_0,\tau_0^2)\) for the normal model. What is the structural difference between them, and why does the product prior not yield an analytical posterior?
Answer:

Structural difference: In the conjugate N-Inv-\(\chi^2(\mu_0,\kappa_0,\nu_0,\sigma_0^2)\) prior, the prior for \(\mu\) depends on \(\sigma^2\): \(\mu \mid \sigma^2 \sim \mathrm{N}(\mu_0, \sigma^2/\kappa_0)\). The variance of \(\mu\) is proportional to \(\sigma^2\) — \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) are a priori dependent.

The product prior \(\mathrm{N}(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2)\,\mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(\nu_0,\tau_0^2)\) makes \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) a priori independent, with the prior variance of \(\mu\) fixed at \(\sigma_0^2\) regardless of \(\sigma^2\). This is more natural for expert elicitation (experts specify information on \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) separately), but breaks the conjugacy structure.

Why no analytical posterior: With the product prior, the conditional posterior of \(\mu\) given \(\sigma^2\) is tractable: \[p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{N}(\bar{\mu}, \bar{\sigma}^2)\] where \(\bar{\mu}\) and \(\bar{\sigma}^2\) depend on \(\sigma^2\) (formula 4.18). However, for \(\sigma^2\), one must compute \(p(\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \propto \int p(\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y}, \mu)\,p(\mu)\,d\mu\), which involves \(p(\sigma^2 \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \propto \bar{\sigma}\,\mathrm{N}(\bar{\mu} \mid \mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\,\mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(\sigma^2 \mid \nu_0, \tau_0^2)\,\prod_i\mathrm{N}(y_i \mid \bar{\mu}, \sigma^2)\). The integration constant of this expression is not analytically tractable.

Consequence: The posterior can only be evaluated on a grid or sampled (e.g., using SIR from Chapter 3.7.2), as illustrated in Example IV.6.

Short Answer 7 of 8 — Wishart and Multinomial Distributions
Describe the Wishart distribution as a multivariate generalization of the chi-squared distribution. Then state the multinomial distribution and its conjugate prior. Describe how to sample from the Dirichlet distribution.
Answer:

Wishart distribution: In the univariate case, \(S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2(n-1)\) where \(S^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2\). Replacing \(S^2\) by the sample cross-product matrix \(\mathbf{S} = \sum_{i=1}^n (\boldsymbol{y}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{y}})(\boldsymbol{y}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{y}})^T\) yields the Wishart distribution \(\mathrm{Wishart}(\Sigma, \nu)\) with \(\nu = n-1\) degrees of freedom and scale matrix \(\Sigma\). Its density is:

\[p(\mathbf{S} \mid \nu, \Sigma) = c\,|\Sigma|^{-\nu/2}\,|\mathbf{S}|^{(\nu-p-1)/2}\exp\!\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{S})\right]\]

The inverse Wishart \(\mathrm{IW}(\Sigma, \nu)\) is the conjugate prior for the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) in MVN models.

Multinomial distribution: For \(y = (y_1,\ldots,y_k)^T\) frequencies across \(k\) categories with total \(n = \sum_{j=1}^k y_j\): \[p(\boldsymbol{y} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{n!}{\prod_j y_j!}\prod_{j=1}^k \theta_j^{y_j}\]

Conjugate prior: The Dirichlet distribution \(\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\), yielding posterior \(\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \boldsymbol{y})\) after observing \(\boldsymbol{y}\).

Sampling from Dirichlet: Generate \(W_j \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_j, 1)\) independently for \(j = 1,\ldots,k\), compute \(T = \sum_{j=1}^k W_j\), and set \(Z_j = W_j/T\). Then \((Z_1, \ldots, Z_k) \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\). This technique was used in Example IV.3 to sample 10,000 values of the cross-ratio \(\psi\) from its posterior.

Short Answer 8 of 8 — Bayesian GLIMs
Describe the three components of a Generalized Linear Model (GLIM) and write the distributional part in exponential family form. For the Poisson regression and logistic regression models, identify the distribution, link function, and variance function. Explain what a BGLIM is and what its limitation is in practice.
Answer:

A GLIM consists of three parts:

(1) Distributional part: The response \(y\) belongs to the one-parameter exponential family:

\[p(y \mid \theta; \phi) = \exp\!\left[\frac{y\theta - b(\theta)}{a(\phi)} + c(y;\phi)\right]\]

where \(\theta\) is the canonical parameter, \(a(\phi) = \phi/w\) is a dispersion function, and \(b(\theta), c(y;\phi)\) are known functions. For \(\phi\) known: \(E(y) = \mu = b'(\theta)\) and \(\mathrm{var}(y) = a(\phi)V(\mu)\) with \(V(\mu) = b''(\theta)\).

(2) Link function: A monotone differentiable function \(g\) linking the mean \(\mu\) to linear predictors: \(g(\mu) = \eta = \boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{\beta}\). The canonical link sets \(\eta = \theta\). The inverse \(h = g^{-1}\) is a CDF for binary models.

(3) Variance function: \(V(\mu)\) describes how variance depends on the mean. The parameter \(\phi\) serves as an extra dispersion/scale parameter: \(\mathrm{var}(y) = a(\phi)V(\mu)\).

BGLIM: A Bayesian GLIM (BGLIM) is obtained by specifying a prior distribution on all parameters \((\boldsymbol{\beta}, \phi)\) of the GLIM. Since GLIMs generally do not have conjugate priors for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\), the posterior typically has no analytical form.

Limitation: For complex models (censoring, truncation, random effects) and non-normal likelihoods, analytical posteriors almost invariably do not exist, and even simple numerical methods may be insufficient. In practice, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods (Chapter 6) are required for general Bayesian inference in GLIMs and beyond.
