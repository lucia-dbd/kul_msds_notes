5.1 Introduction — Why MCMC?

Bayes' theorem yields the posterior distribution \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \propto L(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\), but evaluating it requires the normalizing constant:

\[p(\boldsymbol{y}) = \int L(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}\]

This integral is analytically intractable for most non-conjugate models and computationally prohibitive for high-dimensional \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) even with numerical integration. Sampling is the solution: instead of computing the posterior exactly, draw a large sample from it and use that sample for inference.

ApproachLimitation
Analytical solutionOnly feasible for conjugate models; too restrictive in practice
Numerical integrationCurse of dimensionality: cost grows exponentially with \(d = \dim(\boldsymbol{\theta})\)
MCMC samplingScales to high dimensions; requires only the un-normalized posterior \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{y}) \propto L\cdot p\)
Two main MCMC families: (1) Gibbs sampler — samples each parameter from its full conditional distribution, cycling through all parameters. Requires the ability to sample from each full conditional. (2) Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm — proposes candidate values from a proposal density and accepts or rejects them; far more general than Gibbs. Both generate a dependent Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the target posterior.

5.2 The Gibbs Sampler

Introduced by Geman & Geman (1984) for image processing; brought to Bayesian statistics by Gelfand & Smith (1990).

5.2.1 Bivariate Gibbs Sampler

The joint posterior \(p(\theta_1, \theta_2 \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) is completely determined by the two full conditional distributions (full conditionals):

\[p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2, \boldsymbol{y}) \qquad \text{and} \qquad p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1, \boldsymbol{y})\]

Gibbs sampling algorithm (bivariate case): Starting from initial values \(\theta_1^{(0)}, \theta_2^{(0)}\), at iteration \(k+1\):

Step 1: Sample \(\theta_1^{(k+1)} \sim p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})\)

Step 2: Sample \(\theta_2^{(k+1)} \sim p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1^{(k+1)}, \boldsymbol{y})\)    (uses the updated \(\theta_1\)!)

This yields the chain \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} = (\theta_1^{(k)}, \theta_2^{(k)})^T,\; k = 1, 2, \ldots\) of dependent samples. The Markov property holds:

\[p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k-1)}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{y}) = p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})\]

Burn-in (initial portion): The early part of the chain depends on the starting values and is not representative of the posterior. This portion must be discarded. Under mild regularity conditions, from iteration \(k_0\) onwards the chain samples from the true posterior: \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \overset{d}{\to} p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\).

Visual signature — Zigzag pattern: In the \((\theta_1, \theta_2)\) plane, the Gibbs path forms a characteristic zigzag (horizontal and vertical moves only), since each step updates only one parameter.

Example VI.1 — SAP Study (NI Prior)

Data: \(n = 250\) ALP measurements. Likelihood: \(\mathrm{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\). NI prior: \(p(\mu, \sigma^2) \propto \sigma^{-2}\).

Full conditionals:

\[p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{N}\!\left(\bar{y},\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\]

\[p(\sigma^2 \mid \mu, \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2\!\left(n,\; s_\mu^2\right), \quad s_\mu^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \mu)^2\]

Result: Burn-in = 500, total chain = 1,500. Marginal histograms closely match the true analytical posteriors (solid lines). The zigzag in the \((\mu, \sigma^2)\) plane is clearly visible; each complete Gibbs step consists of two sub-steps.

Example VI.2 — Mixed Discrete-Continuous Distribution

Joint distribution \(f(x, y) \propto \binom{n}{x} y^{x+\alpha-1}(1-y)^{n-x+\beta-1}\) with \(x\) discrete, \(y \in [0,1]\).

Full conditional 1: \(f(x \mid y) = \mathrm{Binomial}(n, y)\)

Full conditional 2: \(f(y \mid x) = \mathrm{Beta}(x + \alpha,\; n - x + \beta)\)

The Gibbs sampler handles mixed discrete-continuous joint distributions transparently — no special treatment required.

Example VI.3 — SAP Study (Informative Prior)

Semi-conjugate (product) prior: \(\mu \sim \mathrm{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\) and \(\sigma^2 \sim \mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(\ell_0, \pi_0^2)\) independently.

\[p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{N}(\mu_k, \tilde{\sigma}^2_k), \quad \text{where } \frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^2_k} = \frac{n}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}, \quad \mu_k = \tilde{\sigma}^2_k\!\left(\frac{n\bar{y}}{\sigma^2} + \frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2}\right)\]

\[p(\sigma^2 \mid \mu, \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2\!\left(\ell_0 + n,\; \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i - \mu)^2 + \ell_0\pi_0^2}{\ell_0 + n}\right)\]

Derivation of \(p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y})\): Multiplying the likelihood contribution \(\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{n}{2\sigma^2}(\mu - \bar{y})^2\right)\) by the prior \(\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma_0^2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2\right)\) gives a Gaussian kernel in \(\mu\). Precisions add: the posterior precision of \(\mu\) is \(n/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_0^2\), and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of \(\bar{y}\) and \(\mu_0\). This is a general result for combining two Gaussian sources of information.

5.2.2–5.2.3 The General Gibbs Sampler

For a \(d\)-dimensional parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_d)^T\), the Gibbs sampler at iteration \(k+1\) sequentially draws:

\(\theta_1^{(k+1)} \sim p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2^{(k)}, \theta_3^{(k)}, \ldots, \theta_d^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})\)

\(\theta_2^{(k+1)} \sim p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1^{(k+1)}, \theta_3^{(k)}, \ldots, \theta_d^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})\)

\(\quad \vdots\)

\(\theta_d^{(k+1)} \sim p(\theta_d \mid \theta_1^{(k+1)}, \theta_2^{(k+1)}, \ldots, \theta_{d-1}^{(k+1)}, \boldsymbol{y})\)

Each step conditions on the most recently updated values of all other parameters. Under mild regularity conditions, the chain ultimately yields samples from \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\).

Example VI.4 — British Coal Mining Disasters (Change-Point Model)

Dataset: annual counts of severe accidents in British coal mines, 1851–1962 (\(n = 112\)). A change-point at year \(k\) (approximately 1890) is modelled as:

\(y_i \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda),\; i = 1, \ldots, k \qquad y_i \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\mu),\; i = k+1, \ldots, n\)

Priors: \(\lambda \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_1, b_1)\), \(\mu \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_2, b_2)\), \(k \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(1, n)\), \(b_j \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(c_j, d_j)\)

The five-dimensional parameter vector \((\lambda, \mu, k, b_1, b_2)\) has tractable full conditionals:

ParameterFull Conditional
\(\lambda \mid \cdot\)\(\mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(a_1 + \sum_{i=1}^k y_i,\; k + b_1\right)\)
\(\mu \mid \cdot\)\(\mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(a_2 + \sum_{i=k+1}^n y_i,\; n - k + b_2\right)\)
\(b_1 \mid \cdot\)\(\mathrm{Gamma}(a_1 + c_1,\; \lambda + d_1)\)
\(b_2 \mid \cdot\)\(\mathrm{Gamma}(a_2 + c_2,\; \mu + d_2)\)
\(k \mid \cdot\)Discrete: \(p(k \mid \cdot) \propto \mathcal{L}(y \mid k, \lambda, \mu) = \exp[k(\mu - \lambda)](\lambda/\mu)^{\sum_{i=1}^k y_i}\)
Results (settings: \(a_1 = a_2 = 0.5\), \(c_1 = c_2 = 0\), \(d_1 = d_2 = 1\)): Posterior mode of \(k\) = 1891 (historically, the year of major safety reforms). Posterior mean of \(\lambda/\mu = 3.42\), 95% CI = [2.48, 4.59], indicating the pre-change accident rate was approximately 3.4× the post-change rate.

Example VI.5 — Osteoporosis Study (Bayesian Linear Regression)

Model: \(\mathrm{tbbmc}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\mathrm{bmi}_i + \varepsilon_i\), \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathrm{N}(0, \sigma^2)\), \(n = 234\). NI prior: \(p(\beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma^2) \propto \sigma^{-2}\).

\(p(\sigma^2 \mid \beta_0, \beta_1, \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{Inv}\text{-}\chi^2(n,\; s_\alpha^2)\), \quad \(s_\alpha^2 = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2\)

\(p(\beta_0 \mid \sigma^2, \beta_1, \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{N}(r_1,\; \sigma^2/n)\), \quad \(r_1 = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum(y_i - \beta_1 x_i)\)

\(p(\beta_1 \mid \sigma^2, \beta_0, \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{N}(r_0,\; \sigma^2/\boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{x})\), \quad \(r_0 = \sum(y_i - \beta_0)x_i / \boldsymbol{x}^T\boldsymbol{x}\)

Trace plot vs Index plot — slow mixing: For \(\sigma^2\), the trace plot closely resembles the index plot from Method of Composition (MoC) — indicating near-independent sampling. For \(\beta_1\), the trace plot shows slow mixing (high autocorrelation), meaning the Gibbs chain takes many steps to explore the posterior as effectively as MoC. This is because \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) are correlated a posteriori (correlation ≈ −0.99 due to collinearity).

Autocorrelation in MCMC Chains

Autocorrelation of lag \(m\): \(\rho_m = \mathrm{corr}(\theta^{(k)},\, \theta^{(k-m)})\)

  • High autocorrelation → longer burn-in needed; chain "forgets" its starting point slowly
  • High autocorrelation → effective sample size (ESS) much smaller than chain length
  • ESS = \(K / (1 + 2\sum_{m=1}^\infty \rho_m)\), where \(K\) = post-burn-in chain length

5.2.4 Remarks — Theoretical Foundations of the Gibbs Sampler

Full Conditionals Determine the Joint Distribution

By Besag (1974) and Hammersley & Clifford (1971), under mild positivity conditions the full conditionals uniquely determine the joint distribution. For the bivariate case (Proof sketch):

1. \(p(\theta_1, \theta_2) = p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)\,p_1(\theta_1) = p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)\,p_2(\theta_2)\)

2. Dividing: \(\dfrac{p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)}{p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)} = \dfrac{p_2(\theta_2)}{p_1(\theta_1)}\), which on integrating over \(\theta_2\) gives \(\dfrac{1}{p_1(\theta_1)} = \int \dfrac{p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)}{p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)}\,d\theta_2\)

3. Therefore: \(p(\theta_1, \theta_2) = p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)\Big/\int \dfrac{p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)}{p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)}\,d\theta_2\)

Existence is not enough: That the joint distribution exists does not by itself determine it. One must also verify that the conditionals are consistent (i.e., they actually arise from some joint distribution). In the Gibbs sampler, the fixed-point equation \(p_1(\theta_1) = \int K(\nu_1, \theta_1)\,p_1(\nu_1)\,d\nu_1\) with \(K(\nu_1, \theta_1) = \int p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)\,p(\theta_2 \mid \nu_1)\,d\theta_2\) must be satisfied.

Transition Kernel

The transition kernel is the engine moving the chain from \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}\) to \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}\). For the \(d\)-dimensional Gibbs sampler:

\[K(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\phi}) = p(\phi_1 \mid \theta_2, \ldots, \theta_d) \times p(\phi_2 \mid \phi_1, \theta_3, \ldots, \theta_d) \times \cdots \times p(\phi_d \mid \phi_1, \ldots, \phi_{d-1})\]

In the bivariate case, the marginal transition kernel is \(K_1(\nu_1, \theta_1) = \int p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)\,p(\theta_2 \mid \nu_1)\,d\theta_2\), representing the probability of moving from \(\nu_1\) to \(\theta_1\) via all possible intermediate values of \(\theta_2\).

5.2.5–5.2.6 Gibbs Sampler Variants and Slice Sampler

Sampling the Full Conditionals

When a full conditional has a recognizable standard form, classical samplers are used. When it does not, general-purpose algorithms are applied:

  • SAS® GENMOD/LIFEREG/PHREG: ARMS algorithm (Adaptive Rejection Metropolis Sampling)
  • WinBUGS: uses a variety of samplers depending on the full conditional's shape

This removes the high posterior correlation between \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) that causes slow mixing when updated individually.

5.2.6 The Slice Sampler

The slice sampler converts the problem of sampling from a density \(f(x)\) on \([a,b]\) into sampling from a bivariate uniform distribution on the region \(A = \{(x, y) : 0 < y < f(x)\}.\)

  • Auxiliary variable: \(y\) is introduced artificially; marginalizing over \(y\) recovers \(f(x)\).
  • For the unimodal case (a bivariate Gibbs algorithm):
    • \(y \mid x \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,\, f(x))\)
    • \(x \mid y \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(\min_y, \max_y)\), where the interval \(S(y) = [\min_y, \max_y]\) is the slice at level \(y\)
  • For the multimodal case, slices \(S(y)\) become unions of intervals rather than single intervals.
Example VI.6 — Slice sampling the standard normal:

\(y \mid x \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\!\left(0,\; e^{-x^2/2}\right)\)

\(x \mid y \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\!\left(-\sqrt{-2\log y},\; \sqrt{-2\log y}\right) \quad \Rightarrow \quad S(y) = [-\sqrt{-2\log y},\, \sqrt{-2\log y}]\)

Implementation: WinBUGS implements the slice sampler for the finite-support case. The key insight is that the area beneath a density curve is uniform — slicing at a uniformly chosen height gives a uniform draw from the corresponding \(x\)-interval, which marginalizes back to the target density.

5.3 The Metropolis(-Hastings) Algorithm

The Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm is a general MCMC technique that does not require full conditional distributions to be of standard form — making it far more widely applicable than the Gibbs sampler.

  • Metropolis algorithm: Metropolis et al. (1953) — symmetric proposal density
  • Metropolis-Hastings algorithm: Hastings (1970) — asymmetric proposal density
  • Gained wide use in Bayesian statistics after Gelfand & Smith (1990)
  • Further extended by Green (1995): Reversible Jump MCMC

5.3.1 The Metropolis Algorithm

Given the chain is at \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}\), the Metropolis algorithm generates \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}\) as follows:

Step 1: Sample a candidate \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \sim q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})\), where \(q\) is a symmetric proposal density: \(q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = q(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^*)\).

Step 2: Set \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)} = \boldsymbol{\theta}^*\) with probability \(\alpha(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{\theta}^*)\), else \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)} = \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}\).

\[\alpha(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{\theta}^*) = \min\!\left(r,\; 1\right), \qquad r = \frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{y})}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{y})}\]

Interpretation of the acceptance rule: If the candidate \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*\) has higher posterior density than the current point (\(r \geq 1\)), it is always accepted. If it has lower posterior density (\(r < 1\)), it is accepted with probability \(r\) (the ratio of posterior densities). This probabilistic acceptance allows the chain to escape local modes and explore the full posterior. When rejected, the chain stays at \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}\) — recording the current point again (unlike the AR algorithm, which simply tries again).

Effect of Proposal Scale — Example VI.7

Proposal: \(q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}) = \mathrm{N}_2(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathrm{diag}(\delta^2, \delta^2)\).

Proposal variance \(\delta^2\)Acceptance rateConsequence
\(\delta^2 = 0.03\) (appropriate)≈ 40%Good exploration; accurate marginal distributions
\(\delta^2 = 0.001\) (too small)≈ 84%Nearly all proposals accepted, but tiny steps — very slow mixing; poor approximation
Optimal acceptance rates (tuning rule): For a 1-dimensional target, the optimal acceptance rate is approximately 45%. For high-dimensional targets (\(d \gg 1\)), it is approximately 23.4%. WinBUGS auto-tunes in the first 4,000 iterations to achieve 20–40%; SAS® PROC MCMC uses multiple adaptive loops.

5.3.2 The Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm

Generalizes the Metropolis algorithm to asymmetric proposal densities \(q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}) \neq q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^*)\):

\[\alpha(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{\theta}^*) = \min\!\left(\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{y})\; q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^*)}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\; q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})},\; 1\right)\]

The additional ratio \(q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^*) / q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})\) corrects for the asymmetry of the proposal: if the proposal favors moving from \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}\) to \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*\) more than the reverse, the acceptance probability is reduced accordingly.

Reversibility condition: The MH algorithm constructs a chain satisfying the detailed balance condition: the probability flux from \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) to \(\boldsymbol{\phi}\) equals the flux from \(\boldsymbol{\phi}\) to \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\): \[p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,K(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\phi}) = p(\boldsymbol{\phi} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\,K(\boldsymbol{\phi}, \boldsymbol{\theta})\] A chain satisfying this is called reversible. The MH acceptance probability is precisely chosen (by Hastings, 1970) to satisfy this condition optimally.

Gibbs Sampler as a Special Case of MH

Define the proposal kernel for the \(j\)-th Gibbs step as \(q_j^G(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}) = p(\theta_j^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})\) when \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^* = \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)}\) (zero otherwise). Substituting into the MH ratio:

\[r = \frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{y})\;p(\theta_j^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^*, \boldsymbol{y})}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\;p(\theta_j^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})} = \frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{y})\;/\;p(\theta_j^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\;/\;p(\theta_j^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^*, \boldsymbol{y})} = 1\]

The ratio equals 1, so every Gibbs proposal is accepted. The Gibbs sampler is therefore a special case of MH with acceptance rate identically equal to 1.

5.3.4 Review of Metropolis-Hastings Approaches

Random-Walk MH (RWMH)

Proposal: \(q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}) = q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* - \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})\), symmetric in the increment. Common choices:

  • Multivariate normal: \(\mathrm{N}_d(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) — used in WinBUGS and SAS® procedures
  • Multivariate \(t\)-distribution: SAS® PROC MCMC for long-tailed posteriors

Optimal acceptance rates: 45% for \(d = 1\); 23.4% for \(d \gg 1\).

Independent MH

Proposal does not depend on the current position: \(q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}) = q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)\). A candidate \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*\) is accepted when \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{y})/q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) > p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{y})/q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})\). Key results:

  • If \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \leq A\,q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) for all \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\), the chain enjoys excellent convergence (geometric ergodicity)
  • Under this condition, the expected acceptance rate exceeds that of the AR algorithm

Block MH (Metropolis-within-Gibbs)

Combines Gibbs and MH: parameters are divided into blocks; within each block, a MH step is used when the full conditional is non-standard. WinBUGS uses this strategy (regression coefficients in one block, variance parameters in another). SAS® PROC MCMC allows user-specified blocks.

Reversible Jump MCMC (RJMCMC)

A special case of MH that allows jumps between parameter spaces of different dimension — the "trans-dimensional" case. Proposed by Green (1995). Key construction: a bijective map between the spaces of different dimensions is used, and the MH ratio is computed with the appropriate Jacobian to ensure the detailed balance condition holds.

5.4 Justification of MCMC Approaches

Ergodicity — The Convergence Guarantee

For an MCMC chain to converge to the target posterior, it must be ergodic, which requires:

PropertyMeaning
IrreducibilityThe chain can reach every region of positive posterior probability from any starting point
AperiodicityThe chain has no cyclic behavior (it does not return to a state only at multiples of some fixed period)
Positive recurrenceThe chain visits every region infinitely often and the expected return time to any region is finite

Autocovariance and Autocorrelation

Autocovariance of lag \(m\): \(\tau_m = \mathrm{cov}(t(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}),\, t(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+m)}))\)

Autocorrelation of lag \(m\): \(\rho_m = \tau_m / \tau_0\)

Even though consecutive chain elements are conditionally independent given the immediate past (Markov property), they are unconditionally dependent.

Ergodic Theorems for Markov Chains

Markov Chain LLN: For an ergodic chain with stationary distribution \(\pi\): \[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K t(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}) \xrightarrow{K \to \infty} E_\pi[t(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\]

Markov Chain CLT: For a uniformly (or geometrically) ergodic chain: \[\sqrt{K}\left(\bar{t}_K - E[t(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathrm{N}(0, \pi^2)\] where the asymptotic variance is: \[\pi^2 = \tau_0\!\left[1 + 2\sum_{m=1}^\infty \rho_m\right]\]

Interpretation of \(\pi^2\): The asymptotic variance \(\pi^2\) equals the variance \(\tau_0\) multiplied by \((1 + 2\sum \rho_m)\). For an independent sample all \(\rho_m = 0\), so \(\pi^2 = \tau_0\) (minimal variance). For a correlated chain, \(\pi^2 > \tau_0\): the effective sample size is \(K_{\mathrm{eff}} = K / (1 + 2\sum \rho_m) < K\). High autocorrelation inflates the variance of MCMC estimates.

MH Algorithm Creates a Reversible Chain — Proof (Discrete Case)

Let \(\pi_i = p(\theta = x_i)\) and \(q_{ij}\) be the proposal probability from \(x_i\) to \(x_j\). The acceptance probability is \(\alpha_{ij} = \min(1,\, \pi_j q_{ji} / \pi_i q_{ij})\). The transition probability is \(p_{ij} = \alpha_{ij} q_{ij}\). Detailed balance:

\[\pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_i \alpha_{ij} q_{ij} = \pi_i \min\!\left(1, \frac{\pi_j q_{ji}}{\pi_i q_{ij}}\right) q_{ij} = \min(\pi_i q_{ij}, \pi_j q_{ji}) = \pi_j p_{ji}\]

Therefore \(\pi\) is the stationary distribution: the MH algorithm produces a Markov chain with the target distribution as its stationary distribution. ✓

5.6 The Reversible Jump MCMC Algorithm

RJMCMC (Green, 1995) extends the standard MH algorithm to target distributions on spaces of varying dimension — the trans-dimensional case. It constructs a Markov chain that moves both within a parameter space of fixed dimension and between spaces of different dimensions.

Key Applications

  • Mixture models with an unknown number of components
  • Change-point problems with unknown number/location of change-points
  • Bayesian variable selection — most important application in practice
  • Analysis of quantitative trait locus (QTL) data
  • Hidden Markov models with unknown number of states

Core Idea

To move between model \(\mathcal{M}_1\) (dimension \(d_1\)) and \(\mathcal{M}_2\) (dimension \(d_2 > d_1\)): augment the lower-dimensional model with auxiliary random variables \(\boldsymbol{u}\) of dimension \(d_2 - d_1\) to create a bijective (invertible) map \((\boldsymbol{\theta}_1, \boldsymbol{u}) \leftrightarrow \boldsymbol{\theta}_2\). The MH acceptance ratio includes the Jacobian of this map, ensuring the detailed balance condition holds.

Example VI.10 — Poisson vs Negative Binomial (Caries Study)

Data: dmft-index (count of decayed/missing/filled teeth) for all children. Mean = 2.24, variance = 7.93 → overdispersion (variance ≫ mean), suggesting the Poisson model is inadequate.

Short Answer 1 of 8 — Gibbs Sampler: Full Conditionals and the Joint Distribution
State the Besag-Hammersley-Clifford result for the bivariate case. Provide the proof sketch showing how the full conditionals \(p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)\) and \(p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)\) uniquely determine the joint distribution \(p(\theta_1, \theta_2)\). Why is existence of the joint distribution alone not sufficient?
Answer:

Result (Besag, 1974; Hammersley-Clifford, 1971): Under mild positivity conditions (the joint distribution is strictly positive on its support), the full conditional distributions \(p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)\) and \(p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)\) uniquely determine the joint distribution \(p(\theta_1, \theta_2)\).

Proof sketch:

1. From the joint distribution: \(p(\theta_1, \theta_2) = p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)\,p_1(\theta_1) = p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)\,p_2(\theta_2)\).

2. Dividing the two expressions: \(\dfrac{p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)}{p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)} = \dfrac{p_2(\theta_2)}{p_1(\theta_1)}\).

3. Integrating both sides over \(\theta_2\): \[\int \frac{p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)}{p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)}\,d\theta_2 = \frac{1}{p_1(\theta_1)}\int p_2(\theta_2)\,d\theta_2 = \frac{1}{p_1(\theta_1)}\]

\[p(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \frac{p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)}{\int \frac{p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1)}{p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)}\,d\theta_2}\]

This shows the joint is uniquely recoverable from the two full conditionals.

Why existence alone is not sufficient: One must verify that the specified conditionals are consistent — i.e., that they actually arise from some valid joint distribution. The Gibbs sampler solves this by finding the fixed-point solution of the integral equation \(p_1(\theta_1) = \int K_1(\nu_1, \theta_1)\,p_1(\nu_1)\,d\nu_1\) with kernel \(K_1(\nu_1, \theta_1) = \int p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2)\,p(\theta_2 \mid \nu_1)\,d\theta_2\). Two conditionals may be specified without arising from any common joint distribution — in that case the Gibbs sampler will not converge to a valid posterior.

Short Answer 2 of 8 — Informative Prior Full Conditional for μ
For the normal likelihood \(y_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with independent informative prior \(\mu \sim \mathrm{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\), derive the full conditional distribution \(p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y})\). Identify the posterior mean and precision, and give the interpretation in terms of information combining.
Answer:

Derivation: The full conditional is proportional to the product of the likelihood contribution (in \(\mu\)) and the prior on \(\mu\):

\[p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y}) \propto \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i - \mu)^2\right] \cdot \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma_0^2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2\right]\]

Expanding \(\sum(y_i - \mu)^2 = \sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2 + n(\bar{y} - \mu)^2\) and dropping terms not involving \(\mu\):

\[p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y}) \propto \exp\!\left[-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}(\mu - \bar{y})^2 - \frac{1}{2\sigma_0^2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2\right]\]

Completing the square: the exponent is quadratic in \(\mu\). Collecting terms in \(\mu^2\) and \(\mu\): \[\text{Posterior precision: } \tilde{\tau}^{-2} = \frac{n}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}\] \[\text{Posterior mean: } \mu_k = \tilde{\tau}^2\!\left(\frac{n\bar{y}}{\sigma^2} + \frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2}\right)\]

\[p(\mu \mid \sigma^2, \boldsymbol{y}) = \mathrm{N}(\mu_k,\; \tilde{\tau}^2)\]

Interpretation: The posterior precision is the sum of the data precision (\(n/\sigma^2\)) and the prior precision (\(1/\sigma_0^2\)) — "precisions add." The posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the sample mean \(\bar{y}\) (weighted by data precision) and the prior mean \(\mu_0\) (weighted by prior precision). With more data (\(n\) large), the data dominate; with strong prior (\(\sigma_0^2\) small), the prior dominates. This is the general Gaussian information combination formula.

Short Answer 3 of 8 — Coal Mining Change-Point Model Full Conditionals
In Example VI.4 (British coal mining disasters), describe the Bayesian change-point model for Poisson data. State all five full conditional distributions and explain how the discrete full conditional of \(k\) is derived and sampled.
Answer:

Model: \(y_i \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)\) for \(i = 1,\ldots,k\) and \(y_i \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)\) for \(i = k+1,\ldots,n\), where \(k\) is an unknown change-point. Priors: \(\lambda \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_1, b_1)\), \(\mu \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_2, b_2)\), \(b_j \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(c_j, d_j)\), \(k \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(1,n)\).

Five full conditionals:

\[p(\lambda \mid \cdot) = \mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(a_1 + \sum_{i=1}^k y_i,\; k + b_1\right)\] \[p(\mu \mid \cdot) = \mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(a_2 + \sum_{i=k+1}^n y_i,\; n - k + b_2\right)\] \[p(b_1 \mid \cdot) = \mathrm{Gamma}(a_1 + c_1,\; \lambda + d_1)\] \[p(b_2 \mid \cdot) = \mathrm{Gamma}(a_2 + c_2,\; \mu + d_2)\] \[p(k \mid \cdot) \propto \mathcal{L}(y \mid k, \lambda, \mu) = \exp[k(\mu - \lambda)]\left(\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\right)^{\sum_{i=1}^k y_i}\]

Derivation of \(p(k \mid \cdot)\): The log-likelihood of \(k\) is \[\log\mathcal{L} = k(\log\lambda - \log\mu) + \log\lambda\sum_{i=1}^k y_i - k\lambda - (n-k)\mu + \mathrm{const}\] Rearranging: \(\propto \exp[k(\mu - \lambda)](\lambda/\mu)^{\sum_{i=1}^k y_i}\).

Sampling from \(p(k \mid \cdot)\): Compute the unnormalized probability for each value \(k = 1, \ldots, n\) (only \(n\) evaluations), normalize to a proper probability mass function, and sample using the inverse-CDF method. This is exact since the support of \(k\) is finite.

Short Answer 4 of 8 — Proof that the MH Algorithm Satisfies Detailed Balance
For the discrete Metropolis-Hastings algorithm with target distribution \(\boldsymbol{\pi} = (\pi_1, \ldots, \pi_r)\), proposal matrix \(\boldsymbol{Q} = (q_{ij})\), and acceptance probability \(\alpha_{ij} = \min(1, \pi_j q_{ji}/\pi_i q_{ij})\), prove that the resulting transition probabilities \(p_{ij} = \alpha_{ij} q_{ij}\) satisfy the detailed balance condition \(\pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_j p_{ji}\).
Answer:

Proof: We need to show \(\pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_j p_{ji}\) for all \(i \neq j\).

The transition probability is \(p_{ij} = \alpha_{ij}\,q_{ij}\), with \(\alpha_{ij} = \min\!\left(1, \dfrac{\pi_j q_{ji}}{\pi_i q_{ij}}\right)\).

Consider the product \(\pi_i p_{ij}\): \[\pi_i p_{ij} = \pi_i \alpha_{ij} q_{ij} = \pi_i \min\!\left(1, \frac{\pi_j q_{ji}}{\pi_i q_{ij}}\right) q_{ij} = \min(\pi_i q_{ij},\; \pi_j q_{ji})\]

By symmetry of the \(\min\) function: \[\pi_j p_{ji} = \pi_j \alpha_{ji} q_{ji} = \pi_j \min\!\left(1, \frac{\pi_i q_{ij}}{\pi_j q_{ji}}\right) q_{ji} = \min(\pi_j q_{ji},\; \pi_i q_{ij}) = \min(\pi_i q_{ij},\; \pi_j q_{ji})\]

\[\therefore \quad \pi_i p_{ij} = \min(\pi_i q_{ij}, \pi_j q_{ji}) = \pi_j p_{ji} \quad \checkmark\]

Conclusion: The detailed balance condition is satisfied, so \(\pi\) is the stationary distribution of the MH chain. This proof generalizes to the continuous case. One must additionally verify irreducibility and aperiodicity (usually mild conditions on the proposal density) to guarantee convergence.

Short Answer 5 of 8 — Gibbs Sampler as a Special Case of MH
Show explicitly that the Gibbs sampler is a special case of the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm with acceptance probability identically equal to 1. Define the proposal kernel, compute the MH ratio \(r\), and simplify.
Answer:

Setup: Consider the \(j\)-th sub-step of the Gibbs sampler, which samples \(\theta_j^{(k+1)}\) from the full conditional \(p(\theta_j \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})\). A "proposal" in MH terms proposes a new parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*\) that matches \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}\) in all components except \(j\).

Proposal kernel: \[q_j^G(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}) = \begin{cases} p(\theta_j^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y}) & \text{if } \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^* = \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]

MH ratio computation: \[r = \frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{y})\;q_j^G(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^*)}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\;q_j^G(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})}\]

Using the factorization \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{y}) = p(\theta_j \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}, \boldsymbol{y})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\): \[r = \frac{p(\theta_j^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})\;p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\;p(\theta_j^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})}{p(\theta_j^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})\;p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\;p(\theta_j^* \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}_{-j}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{y})} = 1\]

\[\alpha(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}, \boldsymbol{\theta}^*) = \min(r, 1) = \min(1, 1) = 1\]

Conclusion: The Gibbs sampler always accepts its proposal. It is therefore a Metropolis-Hastings algorithm with acceptance probability \(\equiv 1\) — the most efficient possible acceptance behavior. The efficiency gain over the general MH comes at the cost of requiring the full conditionals to be in a form that can be sampled directly.

Short Answer 6 of 8 — Markov Chain CLT and Effective Sample Size
State the Markov Chain Central Limit Theorem (CLT). Define the asymptotic variance \(\pi^2\), explain how it relates to the autocorrelations \(\rho_m\), and define the effective sample size (ESS). Explain why high autocorrelation is problematic for MCMC-based inference.
Answer:

Markov Chain CLT: For a uniformly (or geometrically) ergodic Markov chain \(\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}\}\) with stationary distribution \(\pi\), and a function \(t(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) with \(t^2(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) integrable under \(\pi\): \[\sqrt{K}\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K t(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}) - E_\pi[t(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathrm{N}(0,\; \pi^2)\]

Asymptotic variance: \[\pi^2 = \tau_0\!\left[1 + 2\sum_{m=1}^\infty \rho_m\right]\] where \(\tau_0 = \mathrm{var}_\pi(t(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\) is the marginal variance and \(\rho_m\) is the autocorrelation of lag \(m\).

Effective sample size (ESS): The equivalent number of independent observations that would give the same estimation precision: \[K_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{K}{1 + 2\sum_{m=1}^\infty \rho_m} = \frac{K\tau_0}{\pi^2}\]

Why high autocorrelation is problematic:

  • High \(\rho_m\) → large \(\pi^2\) → large variance of the MCMC estimator → poor precision
  • \(K_{\mathrm{eff}} \ll K\): a chain of length 10,000 with autocorrelation factor 50 is equivalent to only 200 independent observations
  • High autocorrelation → longer burn-in (chain "forgets" its starting position slowly)
  • Posterior summary measures (means, credible intervals) are less accurate for a given computational effort

Remedies: Block sampling to remove high posterior correlations; thinning (retaining every \(m\)-th draw); re-parameterization; or switching to a more efficient algorithm (e.g., HMC, slice sampler).

Short Answer 7 of 8 — Reversible Jump MCMC: Methodology and Application
Describe the core theoretical challenge of trans-dimensional MCMC. Explain how RJMCMC (Green, 1995) addresses this challenge using bijective maps and Jacobian corrections. Illustrate with the Poisson-vs-Negative-Binomial example (VI.10), identifying the four types of moves and summarizing the results.
Answer:

Core challenge: Standard MH operates in a fixed-dimensional parameter space. Bayesian model comparison (e.g., between models of different complexity) requires sampling from a mixture of spaces of different dimensions. A naive MH ratio \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \mid \boldsymbol{y}) / p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \mid \boldsymbol{y})\) is undefined when \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*\) and \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}\) lie in spaces of different dimension — there is no common dominating measure.

RJMCMC solution: To jump from model \(\mathcal{M}_1\) (dimension \(d_1\)) to \(\mathcal{M}_2\) (dimension \(d_2 > d_1\)):

  • Augment the current state with auxiliary variables \(\boldsymbol{u} \sim q(\boldsymbol{u})\) of dimension \(d_2 - d_1\)
  • Define a bijective (invertible) map \((\boldsymbol{\theta}_1, \boldsymbol{u}) \mapsto \boldsymbol{\theta}_2\)
  • The MH acceptance ratio includes the Jacobian \(|\partial(\boldsymbol{\theta}_2)/\partial(\boldsymbol{\theta}_1, \boldsymbol{u})|\) of this map, ensuring detailed balance is maintained on the union of all spaces

Example VI.10 — Four types of RJMCMC moves:

(1) Poisson → Poisson: classical within-model update of \(\lambda\) \\ (2) Poisson → NB: trans-dimensional "birth" — propose \(\phi\) from a prior/auxiliary distribution; apply bijective map \((\lambda, u) \to (\lambda, \phi)\) \\ (3) NB → Poisson: trans-dimensional "death" — remove \(\phi\) \\ (4) NB → NB: classical within-model update of \((\lambda, \phi)\)

Results: The chain spent 56–74% of time in the NB model (over four different prior settings). Proposed moves from Poisson to NB were always accepted (the data strongly disfavor the Poisson model given the observed overdispersion: mean = 2.24, variance = 7.93). Trans-dimensional moves accounted for 32–55% of all moves, indicating active model switching. Conclusion: Bayesian model comparison via RJMCMC provides a fully probabilistic assessment of model uncertainty, not merely a point comparison via a criterion like AIC or BIC.
