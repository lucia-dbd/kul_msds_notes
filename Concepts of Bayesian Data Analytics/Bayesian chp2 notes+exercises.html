2.2–2.5 Versions of Bayes Theorem

The Binary Version (2.2)

Generalizing from the diagnostic test setting: let θ = 1 (diseased) or θ = 0 (healthy), and y = 1 (positive test) or y = 0 (negative test).

Shorthand form: p(θ | y) = p(y | θ) · p(θ) / p(y) Key components: p(θ) = prior probability of θ p(y | θ) = likelihood (probability of data given θ) p(y) = averaged/marginal likelihood (normalizing constant) p(θ | y) = posterior probability of θ
Core insight: Bayes had the ingenious idea — novel 250 years ago — of treating parameters as stochastic quantities. In classical statistics, θ is fixed and unknown. In Bayesian statistics, θ is stochastic, representing our uncertainty about its true value θ₀.

The Categorical Version (2.4)

When θ can take K > 2 values (θ₁, θ₂, ..., θ_K):

p(θₖ | y) = p(y | θₖ) · p(θₖ) / Σₘ p(y | θₘ) · p(θₘ)

This is the foundation of Bayesian classification models — assigning individuals to diagnostic classes based on prior beliefs and observed data.

The Continuous Version (2.5)

For a continuous parameter θ with prior density p(θ) and likelihood L(θ|y):

p(θ | y) = L(θ | y) · p(θ) / ∫ L(θ | y) · p(θ) dθ Proportionality form (most useful for computations): p(θ | y) ∝ L(θ | y) · p(θ) [Posterior ∝ Likelihood × Prior] The denominator ∫ L(θ|y)p(θ)dθ is called the "averaged likelihood" — a weighted average of L over all θ-values, weighted by the prior p(θ).
When is θ supported a posteriori? Only when both the prior AND the likelihood support a given θ-value. If either the prior or the likelihood assigns low probability to a θ-value, the posterior will also assign it low probability.

2.3 Probability in a Bayesian Context

TypeDefinitionExample
Objective (Frequentist)Long-run proportion of times an event occurs in repeated experimentsProbability 0.50 that a fair coin shows heads — convergence over many tosses
Subjective (Bayesian)Personal degree of belief that an event will happen or has happenedA physician's prior belief (e.g., 10%) that a specific patient has diabetes
Requirement: Subjective probabilities must still satisfy the axioms of a coherent probability system (0 ≤ p(A) ≤ 1; p(S) = 1; additivity for mutually exclusive events; conditional probability rule). Lindley (2006) argues that probability is intrinsically personal — it depends on who is observing — hence "your probability" rather than "the probability."

When prior information is personal (e.g., a physician's clinical intuition), the resulting posterior probabilities also carry a subjective flavor and do not have a long-run frequency interpretation. This is a fundamental departure from classical statistics.

2.6 The Binomial Case — Beta–Binomial Conjugacy

Example II.1 ECASS 3 stroke study: monitoring incidence of symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage (SICH) under rt-PA treatment. ECASS 2 provided prior data (y₀ = 8 successes out of n₀ = 100). ECASS 3 interim analysis: y = 10 SICH events out of n = 50 patients.

Step 1: Specifying the Prior from ECASS 2

The kernel of the binomial likelihood θ^y₀(1−θ)^(n₀−y₀) is proportional to a Beta density:

Beta density: p(θ) = [1 / B(α₀, β₀)] · θ^(α₀−1) · (1−θ)^(β₀−1) where B(α, β) = Γ(α)Γ(β) / Γ(α+β) = ∫ θ^(α−1)(1−θ)^(β−1) dθ For ECASS 2 prior: α₀ = y₀ + 1 = 9, β₀ = n₀ − y₀ + 1 = 93 → Prior: Beta(9, 93)

Step 2: Constructing the Posterior

Numerator: L(θ|y) · p(θ) ∝ θ^(α₀+y−1) · (1−θ)^(β₀+n−y−1) This is the kernel of Beta(ᾱ, β̄) where: ᾱ = α₀ + y (= 9 + 10 = 19) β̄ = β₀ + n − y (= 93 + 50 − 10 = 133) → Posterior: Beta(19, 133)

Key Properties of the Beta–Binomial Posterior

PropertyDetails
CompromisePosterior mode is weighted average: θ̂_M = [n₀/(n₀+n)]·θ₀ + [n/(n₀+n)]·θ̂ — posterior lies between prior mode and MLE
Shrinkageθ₀ ≤ θ̂_M ≤ θ̂ when y₀/n₀ ≤ y/n (posterior pulled toward prior when data show higher rate)
More concentratedPosterior is more peaked than either prior or likelihood (except when they strongly conflict)
Likelihood dominatesAs n → ∞, prior parameters α₀, β₀ become negligible relative to y and n−y
ConjugacyBeta prior + Binomial likelihood → Beta posterior (same family)

Equivalence: Prior = Imaginary Data

A Beta(α₀, β₀) prior is equivalent to adding (α₀ − 1) imaginary successes and (β₀ − 1) imaginary failures to the observed data. This allows frequentist software to be used for Bayesian estimation by augmenting the dataset.

Noninformative (Flat) Prior

When no prior information is available, use the uniform prior U(0,1) = Beta(1,1). This yields a posterior equal to the scaled likelihood — only the observed data influence the inference.

2.7 The Gaussian Case — Normal–Normal Conjugacy

Example II.2 IBBENS dietary study: n₀ = 563 bank employees, mean cholesterol ȳ₀ = 328 mg/day, SD = 120.3 mg/day. Follow-up IBBENS-2 (n = 50, ȳ = 318 mg/day) provides the data.

Normal Likelihood (σ known)

L(µ | y) ∝ exp[−½·(µ − ȳ)²/(σ²/n)] This is the kernel of N(µ | ȳ, σ²/n) — the likelihood for µ is a normal curve centered at the sample mean ȳ with variance σ²/n.

Normal Prior (from IBBENS)

p(µ) = N(µ | µ₀, σ₀²) where µ₀ = ȳ₀ = 328 and σ₀ = σ/√n₀ = 5.072

Normal Posterior

p(µ | y) = N(µ | µ̄, σ̄²) Posterior mean (precision-weighted average): µ̄ = (w₀·µ₀ + w₁·ȳ) / (w₀ + w₁) Posterior variance: σ̄² = 1 / (w₀ + w₁) where: w₀ = 1/σ₀² (prior precision) w₁ = n/σ² (sample precision) Posterior precision = Prior precision + Sample precision: 1/σ̄² = w₀ + w₁
Counterintuitive result: The posterior precision always equals the SUM of prior and sample precisions — even when prior and likelihood are in conflict. This means the posterior is always MORE concentrated (less uncertain) than either the prior or the likelihood alone. This only holds for the unrealistic case of known σ!

Precision in WinBUGS

WinBUGS (and much Bayesian software) parameterizes the normal as N(µ, 1/σ²) — using precision (= 1/variance) rather than variance. Be aware of this notation difference when interpreting software output.

2.8 The Poisson Case — Gamma–Poisson Conjugacy

Example II.3 Signal-Tandmobiel® study: n = 4351 children, caries experience measured by dmft-index, MLE = ȳ = 2.24.

Poisson Distribution

p(y | θ) = θʸ · e^(−θ) / y! (for count data with equal exposure times) Extended form for unequal exposure tᵢ: p(yᵢ | θ) = (θtᵢ)^yᵢ · e^(−θtᵢ) / yᵢ! Mean = Variance = θ MLE: θ̂ = ȳ

Gamma Prior and Posterior

Gamma(α, β) prior: p(θ) = [β^α / Γ(α)] · θ^(α−1) · e^(−βθ) E(θ) = α/β, Var(θ) = α/β² For ST study: α₀ = 3, β₀ = 1 → Prior mean = 3, fairly diffuse Posterior: Gamma(ᾱ, β̄) where: ᾱ = Σyᵢ + α₀ (= 9758 + 3 = 9761) β̄ = n + β₀ (= 4351 + 1 = 4352) Note: prior effect is minimal given the large sample size.

Equivalence: Prior = Imaginary Data

The Gamma(α₀, β₀) prior corresponds to an imaginary experiment of size β₀ where counts sum to (α₀ − 1). For the ST study prior Gamma(3,1): experiment of size 1 with count = 2.

Overdispersion Warning

The dmft-index showed var(dmft)/mean(dmft) = 3.53 — far exceeding 1.0 (the Poisson mean = variance requirement). This Poisson overdispersion arises because caries in the same mouth are correlated. More appropriate distributions (e.g., negative binomial) should be used in practice.

Transformation of Parameters (Section 2.9)

If ψ = h(θ) is a monotone transformation, then by the change-of-variables rule: p(ψ | y) = p(h⁻¹(ψ)) · |dθ/dψ| Example (stroke study): ψ = log(θ) transforms the Beta(19,133) posterior for θ into a posterior for ψ = log(θ), useful for modeling on the log scale.
Parameterization matters! Unlike the pure likelihood approach (which is invariant to monotone reparameterization), the Bayesian posterior changes when you put a flat prior on h(θ) versus on θ. This was Fisher's main objection to the Bayesian approach.

Bayesian satisfies both LPs: If L₂(θ|y) = c·L₁(θ|y), then p₂(θ|y) = c·L₁(θ|y)·p(θ) / ∫c·L₁p(θ)dθ = p₁(θ|y). The constant c cancels. Thus the Bayesian posterior is invariant to proportional changes in the likelihood, satisfying LP2.

Short Answer 1
In the ECASS 3 stroke study (binomial case), fully derive the posterior distribution for θ (probability of SICH). Use: prior Beta(α₀, β₀) with α₀ = 9, β₀ = 93; data y = 10 successes out of n = 50. Show all algebraic steps, identify the posterior as a named distribution, and state all three key properties of the posterior (compromise, concentration, dominance).
Model Answer:

Step 1 — Likelihood:
L(θ | y) = C(n,y) · θʸ(1−θ)^(n−y) ∝ θ¹⁰(1−θ)⁴⁰

Step 2 — Prior:
p(θ) = Beta(9, 93) ∝ θ^(9−1)(1−θ)^(93−1) = θ⁸(1−θ)⁹²

Step 3 — Numerator (posterior kernel):
p(θ|y) ∝ L(θ|y) · p(θ) ∝ θ¹⁰(1−θ)⁴⁰ · θ⁸(1−θ)⁹² = θ^(10+8)(1−θ)^(40+92) = θ^18(1−θ)^132 = θ^(ᾱ−1)(1−θ)^(β̄−1) where ᾱ = 19, β̄ = 133 This is the kernel of a Beta(19, 133) distribution.

Step 4 — Normalizing constant:
p(y) = C(n,y) · B(ᾱ, β̄) / B(α₀, β₀), which ensures p(θ|y) integrates to 1.

→ Posterior: Beta(19, 133)

Three key properties:
1. Compromise: θ̂_M = (ᾱ−1)/(ᾱ+β̄−2) = 18/150 = 0.12, which lies between the prior mode θ₀ = 8/100 = 0.08 and the MLE θ̂ = 10/50 = 0.20. Numerically: θ̂_M = (n₀/(n₀+n))·θ₀ + (n/(n₀+n))·θ̂ = (100/150)·0.08 + (50/150)·0.20 = 0.053 + 0.067 = 0.12 ✓

2. Concentration: The posterior is more peaked than either the prior or the scaled likelihood, as it combines both sources of information. Posterior ᾱ + β̄ = 152 > prior α₀ + β₀ = 102 (more information → narrower distribution).

3. Likelihood dominates for large n: As n → ∞, y → ∞, the parameters α₀ = 9 and β₀ = 93 become negligible relative to ᾱ = Σy+α₀ and β̄ = n−y+β₀, so the posterior converges to a distribution centered entirely on the MLE.
Short Answer 2
For the Gaussian–Normal conjugate case, write the formula for the posterior mean µ̄ in terms of precisions w₀ and w₁. Then explain: (a) the concept of "precision" and why Bayesians prefer it over variance; (b) what happens to µ̄ as the sample size n → ∞; (c) what happens when σ₀² → ∞ (noninformative prior limit). Illustrate with the IBBENS dietary study numbers.
Model Answer:

Posterior mean formula: µ̄ = (w₀·µ₀ + w₁·ȳ) / (w₀ + w₁) where: w₀ = 1/σ₀² (prior precision) w₁ = n/σ² (sample precision) σ₀² = prior variance, σ²/n = variance of sampling distribution of ȳ (a) Concept of precision:
Precision = 1/variance. A high-precision distribution is narrow and concentrated (strong information); a low-precision distribution is wide and diffuse (weak information). Bayesians prefer precision because: (i) the posterior precision formula is elegantly additive (posterior precision = prior + sample precision); (ii) WinBUGS and many software packages use precision parameterization N(µ, τ) where τ = 1/σ².

(b) As n → ∞:
w₁ = n/σ² → ∞, while w₀ = 1/σ₀² remains fixed. Then:
µ̄ = (w₀·µ₀ + w₁·ȳ)/(w₀ + w₁) → ȳ as w₁ dominates w₀.
Also σ̄² = 1/(w₀ + w₁) → 0. The posterior concentrates on the data mean — likelihood dominates prior for large n.

(c) Noninformative prior (σ₀² → ∞):
w₀ = 1/σ₀² → 0, so µ̄ → ȳ and σ̄² → σ²/n regardless of n.
Posterior becomes N(µ | ȳ, σ²/n) — entirely determined by the data.

IBBENS illustration: σ₀ = 120.3/√563 = 5.072 → w₀ = 1/5.072² = 0.03887 n = 50, σ = 119.5: w₁ = 50/119.5² = 0.003500 µ̄ = (0.03887×328 + 0.003500×318) / (0.03887 + 0.003500) = (12.75 + 1.113) / 0.04237 = 13.863 / 0.04237 ≈ 327.2 ✓ σ̄² = 1/0.04237 ≈ 23.6, σ̄ ≈ 4.86 ≈ 4.79 (reported) The prior precision (0.039) is ~11× the sample precision (0.0035), so µ̄ = 327.2 is much closer to µ₀ = 328 than to ȳ = 318.
Short Answer 3
Explain the concept of "conjugacy" and its practical advantages. Using the three main examples from Chapter 2 (binomial, Gaussian, Poisson), create a summary showing: the likelihood family, the conjugate prior, and the posterior — including how the posterior parameters update. Why is conjugacy computationally important, and when might a non-conjugate prior be preferable?
Model Answer:

Definition of Conjugacy:
A prior p(θ) is conjugate to a likelihood L(θ|y) if the posterior p(θ|y) belongs to the same parametric family as the prior. This occurs when the kernel of the likelihood has the same functional form as the prior kernel in θ.

Summary Table: Likelihood → Conjugate Prior → Posterior Binomial Bin(n, θ): Prior: Beta(α₀, β₀) Posterior: Beta(α₀ + y, β₀ + n − y) Update: add y successes and (n−y) failures to prior "counts" Normal N(µ, σ²), σ known: Prior: N(µ₀, σ₀²) [precision w₀ = 1/σ₀²] Posterior: N(µ̄, σ̄²) Update: µ̄ = precision-weighted average; 1/σ̄² = w₀ + n/σ² Poisson(θ): Prior: Gamma(α₀, β₀) Posterior: Gamma(Σyᵢ + α₀, n + β₀) Update: add observed total counts Σyᵢ to α₀; add n to β₀ Computational advantages:
1. Analytical tractability: The posterior has a closed-form expression — no numerical integration or MCMC needed.
2. Iterative updating: The posterior from one dataset becomes the prior for the next dataset. This is the natural sequential Bayesian learning mechanism.
3. Frequentist software: The equivalence of the prior to "imaginary data" means standard software can be used with an augmented dataset.
4. Easy interpretation: Parameters update in a transparent, interpretable way.

When non-conjugate priors are preferable:
When the conjugate family cannot adequately represent actual prior knowledge. For example, if prior information strongly suggests a bimodal distribution for θ (e.g., two distinct populations), a conjugate unimodal prior would be inappropriate. In such cases, non-conjugate priors (e.g., mixture priors) are used, requiring numerical methods (MCMC) for posterior computation.
Short Answer 4
Compare and contrast the objective Bayesian, subjective Bayesian, and pragmatic Bayesian approaches to specifying prior distributions. For the ECASS 3 stroke study scenario, give a concrete example of what each type of Bayesian would specify as a prior for θ (the probability of SICH), and discuss the implications for posterior inference.
Model Answer:

Objective Bayesian:
The prior is chosen by a formal rule expressing ignorance — independent of the analyst's personal beliefs. For θ ∈ [0,1], this would be the flat (uniform) prior U(0,1) = Beta(1,1), or more formally, Jeffreys' prior Beta(0.5, 0.5). The prior conveys "I have no preference for any value of θ over another." This approach maintains the appearance of objectivity and reproducibility — two analysts using this approach with the same data would always reach the same conclusion. However, it ignores available ECASS 2 evidence.

Result: Posterior = Beta(1+10, 1+40) = Beta(11, 41). Posterior mean = 11/52 ≈ 0.212 — driven entirely by the ECASS 3 interim data.

Subjective Bayesian:
The prior reflects the personal beliefs of the DSMB neurologists. Based on ECASS 2 results and clinical experience, they believe SICH rates lie between 5% and 20%, centered around 8–10%. This could be encoded as Beta(9, 93) (derived from ECASS 2 data) or adjusted further — e.g., if they believe the ECASS 3 population is older and thus at higher risk, they might shift to a prior centered at ~12%: Beta(12, 88). Different experts may specify different priors, leading to different posteriors — the subjective Bayesian explicitly acknowledges this.

Result using ECASS 2-derived prior: Posterior = Beta(19, 133). Mean = 19/152 ≈ 0.125 — a compromise between the ECASS 2 rate (0.08) and ECASS 3 interim rate (0.20).

Pragmatic Bayesian:
Combines approaches flexibly. For a safety monitoring context, they would likely use the ECASS 2 data-derived prior (Beta(9,93)) as it is scientifically well-motivated, then conduct a sensitivity analysis using both the flat prior and a discounted version (e.g., Beta(4.5, 46.5) — halved information from ECASS 2) to assess robustness. They might also report the classical frequentist 95% CI alongside the Bayesian credible interval for comparison.

Implication: The choice of prior affects the DSMB's decision about whether to stop the trial. With the flat prior, the SICH rate appears alarmingly high (posterior mean ≈ 21%); with the ECASS 2 prior, it is shrunk back toward the historical rate (12.5%). The pragmatic Bayesian's sensitivity analysis reveals how robust the conclusions are to prior choice.
Short Answer 5
Explain the historical development of the Bayesian approach from Thomas Bayes to modern computational methods. Address: (a) Bayes' original contribution and why he is not solely credited; (b) Laplace's extensions; (c) why Bayesian methods were practically unavailable for nearly 200 years; (d) the breakthrough that made Bayesian methods accessible; (e) Fisher's opposition and why parameterization of the flat prior was problematic.
Model Answer:

(a) Bayes' original contribution:
Thomas Bayes (~1701–1761) was the first to formulate the problem of Inverse Probability — computing the probability of a parameter θ given observed data, rather than the probability of data given θ. He derived the distribution of the binomial parameter in a two-stage experiment and thereby discovered the beta distribution. His work was published posthumously in 1763 by Richard Price in the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society. However, attributing the theorem solely to Bayes is not fully justified — Bayes' treatment was informal and limited in scope.

(b) Laplace's extensions:
Pierre-Simon Laplace (1749–1827) is more properly the "true architect" of Bayesian theory. He: (i) derived the general categorical version of Bayes theorem; (ii) proved the asymptotic normality of posterior distributions (what we now call the CLT for inverse probability, which preceded the classical CLT); (iii) initially adopted the principle of insufficient reason (uniform priors); (iv) later generalized to nonuniform priors; (v) assigned parameters and hypotheses stochastic behavior on equal footing with observations.

(c) Why unavailable for ~200 years:
The main computational obstacle was evaluating the denominator of Bayes theorem: ∫L(θ|y)p(θ)dθ. For all but the simplest conjugate models, this integral is analytically intractable. In the early–mid 20th century, there was no reliable way to compute this high-dimensional integral for complex real problems. Additionally, Fisher, Neyman, and Pearson — the dominant figures in 20th century statistics — strongly opposed the Bayesian paradigm, which further discouraged its adoption in applied statistics.

(d) The breakthrough — MCMC:
In the late 1980s, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods were introduced to statistical computing. MCMC allows sampling from a posterior distribution without needing to compute the normalizing constant analytically. The BUGS package (1989) and its Windows version WinBUGS implemented these algorithms accessibly, allowing practitioners to fit Bayesian models for virtually any problem. This computational revolution launched the modern Bayesian renaissance.

(e) Fisher's parameterization objection:
Fisher showed that with a flat prior on θ, a reparameterization to ψ = h(θ) gives a different posterior than using a flat prior directly on ψ. Since the "flat prior" is not invariant to reparameterization, different but mathematically equivalent models give different Bayesian conclusions depending on how θ is parameterized — violating the scientific principle that conclusions should not depend on arbitrary mathematical choices. This led Fisher to conclude the Bayesian approach was inherently arbitrary. The solution (Jeffreys' noninformative prior, proportional to √|I(θ)|) was proposed later and IS parameterization-invariant, but this came after Fisher's opposition was already firmly established.
