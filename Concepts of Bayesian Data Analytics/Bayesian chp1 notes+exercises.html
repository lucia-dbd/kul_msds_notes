1.1 The Frequentist Approach

The classical statistical approach encompasses methods providing inference through the P-value, significance level (α), power (1−β), and confidence intervals (CI). The randomized controlled clinical trial (RCT) is the paradigmatic study design built on this toolbox.

Key Elements of Frequentist Inference

P-value Significance Level α Power (1−β) Type I Error Type II Error Confidence Interval

Toenail RCT — Illustrative Example

A double-blind multicenter RCT compared two oral treatments for toenail infection. Key numbers: n₁ = 131, n₂ = 133; observed mean nail lengths: 9.07 mm (A) vs 7.70 mm (B); Δ̂ = 1.38; tobs = 2.19; two-sided P = 0.030; α = 0.05 → statistically significant by Neyman–Pearson (NP).

Important distinction: The frequentist approach is not one unified theory — it is a combination of Fisher's inductive significance testing and Neyman–Pearson's deductive hypothesis testing.

1.1.2 The P-Value as a Measure of Evidence

The P-value was developed by Fisher for well-planned agricultural experiments. It is widely used but frequently misunderstood.

What the P-Value IS

The P-value is the probability of observing a test statistic as extreme or more extreme than the one observed, assuming H₀ is true.

Critical Misinterpretations

Common MisconceptionCorrect Interpretation
P = probability H₀ is trueP only measures extremeness under H₀; p(H₀|data) requires Bayes theorem
P is based only on observed dataP includes hypothetical/unobserved data more extreme than observed
Small P ⟹ large effect sizeP depends on sample size; a tiny P may reflect a trivial effect in a large study
P is an absolute measure of evidenceP does not account for prior information or study size

The P-Value Depends on Sample Space

The P-value is computed using the long-run frequency definition. The sample space — the set of hypothetical experiments considered — affects its value.

Example I.3: In a RCT with 4 planned interim analyses (Pocock's method), a final P = 0.02 is not significant (α* = 0.016 per analysis). But if no interim analyses were planned, the same P = 0.02 is significant. Identical observed data → different conclusions due to different sample spaces!

P-Value Does Not Incorporate Prior Evidence

Example I.5 (Merseyside registry, P = 0.67) illustrates that a non-significant P-value in a very small study does not mean no relationship exists — it may simply reflect insufficient power. The past evidence from Kaldor et al. showing a strong association should logically inform interpretation.

1.1.3 Confidence Intervals as Evidence

The 95% CI is increasingly preferred over P-values. From the toenail RCT: 95% CI for Δ = [0.14, 2.62].

Technical definition: In the long run, 95% of such intervals constructed from repeated samples will contain the true parameter. For any single interval, the parameter is either in it or not — there is no "95% probability" attached to a specific computed interval.
Bayesian flavor: When we tell non-statisticians "there is a 95% probability the true Δ lies in [0.14, 2.62]," we are using a Bayesian posterior credible interval interpretation — which is technically incorrect in the frequentist framework.

1.2 Statistical Inference Based on the Likelihood Function

The likelihood function L(θ | data) expresses the plausibility of parameter θ given observed data. It was introduced by Fisher (1922).

The Binomial Likelihood

f_θ(s) = C(n,s) · θˢ · (1−θ)^(n−s) As a function of θ (with s fixed): L(θ | s) = C(n,s) · θˢ · (1−θ)^(n−s) Log-likelihood: ℓ(θ | s) = c + [s·log(θ) + (n−s)·log(1−θ)] MLE: θ̂ = s/n

For s = 9, n = 12: MLE = θ̂ = 0.75

The Two Likelihood Principles (LP)

LP1 (Sufficiency Principle): All evidence about θ from an experiment is contained in the likelihood function L(θ | data). Nothing outside the likelihood is relevant.
LP2 (Relative/Proportionality Principle): Two likelihood functions for θ contain the same information about θ if they are proportional to each other.

Standardized Likelihood and Interval of Evidence

The standardized likelihood is L(θ|s) / L(θ̂|s). For θ = 0.5 vs θ̂ = 0.75, the likelihood ratio = 0.21 — meaning θ = 0.5 has only ~1/5 the evidential support of the MLE. The interval of evidence contains all θ-values with standardized likelihood ≥ 0.5.

Stopping Rules: Likelihood vs. Frequentist

Example I.8 contrasts binomial vs. negative binomial (Pascal) stopping rules. Both yield 9 successes and 3 failures, but:

Binomial (fixed n=12)Negative Binomial (wait for k=3 failures)
LikelihoodProportional → same MLE, same interval of evidence (LP2)
Frequentist P-value (H₀: θ=0.5)P = 0.0730P = 0.0337
Key result: The stopping rule does NOT affect likelihood inference but DOES affect frequentist inference. This is a fundamental criticism of the frequentist approach.

1.3 The Bayesian Approach: Basic Ideas

The Core Idea

Bayesian inference combines prior knowledge (prior probability) with observed data (likelihood) to produce an updated posterior probability.

Posterior ∝ Likelihood × Prior

Bayes Theorem — Discrete Version

p(B | A) = [p(A | B) · p(B)] / p(A) Expanded (Law of Total Probability): p(B | A) = [p(A|B)·p(B)] / [p(A|B)·p(B) + p(A|Bᶜ)·p(Bᶜ)]

Diagnostic Test Application (Example I.10)

With A = "positive test" and B = "diseased":

pred+ = (Se · prev) / [Se · prev + (1−Sp) · (1−prev)] Folin-Wu test: Se = 0.80, Sp = 0.90 At world prevalence (prev = 0.03): pred+ = 0.20 At hospital prevalence (prev = 0.10): pred+ = 0.47
Important: Even with Se = 0.80 and Sp = 0.90, at low disease prevalence most positive test results are false positives. This is why prevalence matters enormously for predictive value.

Published Research Findings (Example I.11)

Positive predictive value of a research finding: PPV = (1−β)R / [(1−β)R + α] where R = prior odds of a true relationship = 1/(G−1) (G = number of likely relationships examined, 1 truly positive)

When G is large, R is very small, and even with α = 0.05, the probability of a reported positive finding being true can be very low. This highlights the replication crisis in medical research.

✍️ Short Answer Questions (5 Questions)

Short Answer 1
Explain, using the context of Example I.3 (RCT with interim analyses), why two studies can produce an identical final P-value of 0.02 yet lead to opposite conclusions about statistical significance. What fundamental property of the frequentist approach does this illustrate?
Model Answer:

In Example I.3, a RCT with 4 planned interim analyses using Pocock's group sequential method requires a nominal significance level of α* = 0.016 at each analysis to maintain an overall α = 0.05. If the study runs to completion and yields P = 0.02, this does not reach the required per-analysis threshold of 0.016, so the result is declared non-significant.

However, if a different RCT with no planned interim analyses yields the same final P = 0.02, this result exceeds the standard α = 0.05 threshold and is declared statistically significant.

The fundamental property illustrated is dependence on the sample space. In frequentist statistics, the P-value is computed as a long-run frequency over a defined sample space — the set of hypothetical experiments considered. When interim analyses are planned, the sample space expands to include all possible outcomes across multiple time points, which changes the null distribution and thus the threshold for significance. The P-value, and hence the conclusion, depends not only on the observed data but also on unobserved experiments that could have happened. This has been a major philosophical criticism of the frequentist approach.
Short Answer 2
State both likelihood principles (LP1 and LP2) and use Example I.8 (the two surgery experiments with binomial vs. negative binomial stopping rules) to demonstrate how LP2 shows that the stopping rule is irrelevant under the likelihood approach, yet violates the frequentist approach.
Model Answer:

LP1 (Sufficiency Principle): All evidence from an experiment about an unknown quantity θ is contained in the likelihood function L(θ|data). No other aspects of the experimental design affect the inferential content.

LP2 (Relative Likelihood Principle): Two likelihood functions for θ contain the same information about θ if they are proportional to each other.

Application to Example I.8:
Surgeon 1 (binomial): L₁(θ|9) = C(12,9)·θ⁹·(1−θ)³
Surgeon 2 (negative binomial): L₂(θ|9) = C(11,9)·θ⁹·(1−θ)³

These two likelihoods differ only in their binomial coefficients — they are proportional (constant × θ⁹·(1−θ)³). By LP2, they carry the same information about θ. Both yield MLE = θ̂ = 9/12 = 0.75 and identical intervals of evidence. The stopping rule is irrelevant.

Frequentist violation: The same data give P = 0.0730 (binomial) vs P = 0.0337 (negative binomial) for H₀: θ = 0.5. The stopping rule changes the sample space, altering the null distribution and thus the P-value. This demonstrates that the conclusion of a frequentist test depends on experiments that never occurred.
Short Answer 3
Describe the difference between Fisher's significance testing approach and the Neyman–Pearson hypothesis testing approach. Why does Hubbard and Bayarri (2003) warn against conflating the P-value with the Type I error rate? Give a concrete example of how this conflation occurs in medical publications.
Model Answer:

Fisher's approach: Inductive — moves from specific data to general conclusions. Only a null hypothesis H₀ is needed. The P-value is an a posteriori measure of surprise, a continuous index of evidence against H₀. Fisher believed H₀ could never be accepted, only failed to be rejected. The significance level α is a flexible post-hoc threshold, not a pre-committed error rate.

Neyman–Pearson (NP) approach: Deductive — reasons from the general to the particular. Requires both H₀ and an alternative Hₐ. The outcome is a binary decision: reject or accept H₀. Type I error (α) and Type II error (β) are pre-specified before data collection. The power (1−β) is used to plan sample size. The goal is long-run inductive behavior — being wrong no more than α fraction of the time.

The conflation: Hubbard and Bayarri warn that using the P-value as though it were a pre-specified Type I error rate is conceptually invalid. For example, if α = 0.05 is set in advance and the study yields P = 0.023, we may say H₀ is rejected at α = 0.05. However, we cannot say H₀ is rejected at the 0.025 level — because α was not chosen as 0.025; the P-value now takes on the spurious status of a pre-specified level.

Medical example: When papers report asterisks (∗ for P < 0.05, ∗∗ for P < 0.01, ∗∗∗ for P < 0.001), readers infer that a "∗∗" result implies significance at α = 0.01. But if α was originally chosen as 0.05, claiming 0.01-level significance based on an observed P = 0.008 retroactively treats the P-value as a pre-specified error rate — a Fisherian tool misused as a Neyman–Pearson decision threshold.
Short Answer 4
A GP's elderly patient population has a 10% prevalence of diabetes. Using the Folin-Wu blood test (Se = 0.80, Sp = 0.90): (a) Write out the relevant form of Bayes theorem using sensitivity, specificity, and prevalence. (b) Compute the positive predictive value for this population. (c) Interpret the result in plain language, explaining the Bayesian nature of the calculation.
Model Answer:

(a) Bayes theorem formula:
pred+ = (Se · prev) / [Se · prev + (1 − Sp) · (1 − prev)]

(b) Calculation:
pred+ = (0.80 × 0.10) / [(0.80 × 0.10) + (0.10 × 0.90)]
= 0.080 / (0.080 + 0.090)
= 0.080 / 0.170
≈ 0.47

(c) Interpretation:
If the test returns positive for this patient, there is approximately a 47% probability that they truly have diabetes. Despite the test having 80% sensitivity and 90% specificity, fewer than half of positive tests in this population actually indicate true disease. This occurs because even a 10% false-positive rate (1 − Sp = 0.10) applies to the large non-diabetic group (90% of population), generating many false positives that dilute the true positives.

Bayesian nature: The GP started with a prior probability of 10% (prevalence = prior belief about the patient). The test result (data via Se and Sp) updated this belief to a posterior probability of 47%. The prior and the data are formally combined via Bayes theorem to produce an updated, actionable probability estimate — exactly the Bayesian prior-to-posterior updating mechanism.
Short Answer 5
Compare and contrast how a frequentist and a Bayesian statistician would approach the analysis of the Merseyside registry data (Example I.5, P = 0.67 from a very small study of 8 subjects) in light of the prior Kaldor et al. study showing a strong association between chemotherapy and leukemia. What are the philosophical and practical advantages of the Bayesian approach in this context?
Model Answer:

Frequentist approach:
A classical analysis of the Merseyside registry data in isolation yields P = 0.67 (chi-squared with continuity correction). The frequentist interpretation would be: no evidence against H₀ (no relationship between chemotherapy and leukemia). The study's small size (only 8 subjects total: 6 controls, 2 cases) means it is severely underpowered and virtually unable to detect any relationship, even if a strong one exists. Yet under the frequentist paradigm, the Kaldor et al. evidence — which showed an odds ratio of ~8 with P ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ — is not formally incorporated into the analysis. Each study is treated in isolation. The result: "no evidence of harm" from an underpowered study that could not possibly detect harm.

Bayesian approach:
A Bayesian statistician would encode the strong prior evidence from Kaldor et al. as an informative prior distribution on the odds ratio (or log-odds ratio), reflecting a high probability that the relationship is positive and large. The small Merseyside dataset would then be used to update this prior. Because the prior is strongly informative, a small sample provides little ability to overturn the prior — the posterior would still reflect a substantially elevated risk estimate. The prior knowledge is formally and transparently incorporated.

Philosophical and practical advantages:
1. Coherence: Research is not conducted in isolation; the Bayesian approach formally reflects this by using all available evidence.
2. No fictive data: Unlike P-values, the posterior depends only on observed data, not hypothetical unobserved results.
3. Meaningful probability statements: The posterior provides a direct probability that the true effect exceeds a clinically important threshold — impossible with a frequentist P-value alone.
4. Small-sample robustness: In small studies, the posterior is appropriately dominated by the prior, preventing misleading "no evidence of effect" conclusions caused solely by lack of statistical power.
