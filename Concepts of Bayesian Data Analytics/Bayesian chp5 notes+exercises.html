5.1 Introduction

In earlier chapters, the analytical calculation of the posterior distribution is often infeasible because the normalising constant cannot be determined in closed form. Numerical integration is practical only for low‑dimensional problems. Sampling methods such as accept–reject and the Method of Composition provide alternatives, but the latter requires integrating marginal distributions — impractical for most real problems with many parameters.

Why MCMC?

  • Analytical posterior: usually infeasible — intractable integrals
  • Numerical integration: practical only for few parameters
  • Accept–reject / Method of Composition: limited by needing marginal distributions
  • MCMC: no integration required — applicable to virtually all highly multivariate problems

What Is MCMC?

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is a class of sampling algorithms that generates a dependent sample from the posterior distribution by constructing a Markov chain whose stationary distribution equals the target (posterior) distribution. Once the chain has "converged," the samples constitute approximate draws from the posterior.

Key Point: MCMC revolutionised Bayesian statistics by making it possible to fit models that are hard or impossible with maximum likelihood.

Historical Impact

The introduction of these two algorithms revolutionised Bayesian statistics. They allow tackling statistical modelling problems that are hard or even impossible to solve with maximum likelihood, offering the applied statistician a rich toolbox. Without exaggeration, MCMC techniques created an immense revival of the Bayesian idea.

The Markov Property

The generated sequence $\theta^1, \theta^2, \theta^3, \dots$ forms a Markov chain — each value depends only on the immediately preceding value:

$$p\bigl(\theta^{(k+1)} \mid \theta^k, \theta^{(k-1)}, \ldots, \theta^0\bigr)=p\bigl(\theta^{(k+1)}\mid\theta^k\bigr)$$

5.2 The Gibbs Sampler

The Gibbs sampler exploits the property (proved by Besag 1974, tracing back to Hammersley & Clifford 1971) that a multivariate distribution is uniquely determined by its full conditional distributions.

5.2.1 The Bivariate Gibbs Sampler

For a bivariate posterior $p(\theta_1,\theta_2\mid y)$, uniquely determined by $p(\theta_1\mid\theta_2,y)$ and $p(\theta_2\mid\theta_1,y)$, the iterative scheme at iteration $(k+1)$ is:

Algorithm:
  1. Sample $\theta_1^{(k+1)}$ from $p(\theta_1\mid\theta_2^k,y)$
  2. Sample $\theta_2^{(k+1)}$ from $p(\theta_2\mid\theta_1^{(k+1)},y)$

Note: step 2 uses the already‑updated $\theta_1^{(k+1)}$, not the old $\theta_1^k$. This creates a zigzag pattern in the $(\theta_1,\theta_2)$‑plane.

Example VI.1 — Serum Alkaline Phosphatase (Noninformative Prior)

Posterior $p(\mu,\sigma^2\mid y)$ with $n=250$ observations:

$$p(\mu\mid\sigma^2,y)=\mathcal N\bigl(\bar y,\;\sigma^2/n\bigr)$$ $$p(\sigma^2\mid\mu,y)=\text{Inv‐}\chi^2\!\bigl(n,\;s_\mu^2\bigr),\qquad s_\mu^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\mu)^2$$

Chain: 1500 iterations total; burn‑in = 500; retained = 1000 samples. Starting value $(6.5,2)$ quickly moves to the posterior mass around $\bar y\approx7.11$. The histograms of sampled $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ match the analytically derived marginals.

Example VI.2 — Discrete × Continuous Distribution (Non‑Bayesian)

Joint: $f(x,y)\propto\binom{n}{x}\,y^{x+\alpha-1}(1-y)^{\,n-x+\beta-1}$

Full conditionals (derived by removing terms not involving each variable):

$$f(x\mid y)=\text{Bin}(n,y)$$ $$f(y\mid x)=\text{Beta}(x+\alpha,\;n-x+\beta)$$

True marginal $f(x)=\text{Beta‑Binomial BB}(n,\alpha,\beta)$ — verifiable analytically, confirming the sampler's correctness. Demonstrates the Gibbs sampler outside a Bayesian context.

5.2.2 The General d‑Dimensional Gibbs Sampler

Given $\theta^0=(\theta_1^0,\ldots,\theta_d^0)^\top$, at each iteration $(k+1)$ perform $d$ steps:

Algorithm:
  1. Sample $\theta_1^{(k+1)}$ from $p(\theta_1\mid\theta_2^k,\ldots,\theta_d^k,y)$
  2. Sample $\theta_2^{(k+1)}$ from $p(\theta_2\mid\theta_1^{(k+1)},\theta_3^k,\ldots,\theta_d^k,y)$
  3. ⋮
  4. Sample $\theta_d^{(k+1)}$ from $p(\theta_d\mid\theta_1^{(k+1)},\ldots,\theta_{d-1}^{(k+1)},y)$

The distributions $p(\theta_j\mid\text{all other parameters},y)$ are called full conditional distributions (full conditionals).

Example VI.4 — British Coal Mining Change‑Point Model

$y_i\sim\text{Poisson}(\theta)$ for $i=1,\ldots,k$ and $y_i\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)$ for $i=k+1,\ldots,112$.

Conditional conjugate priors: $\theta\sim\text{Gamma}(a_1,b_1)$, $\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(a_2,b_2)$, $k\sim\text{Uniform}\{1,\ldots,n\}$. $b_1$ and $b_2$ also given hyperpriors.

5.3 The Metropolis(–Hastings) Algorithm

The MH algorithm is a general MCMC technique that does not require the full conditionals. It uses a proposal density $q$ and an accept/reject mechanism to ensure the chain's stationary distribution is the posterior.

5.3.1 The Metropolis Algorithm

Requires a symmetric proposal density: $q(\tilde\theta\mid\theta^k)=q(\theta^k\mid\tilde\theta)$.

Formal Steps (chain at position $\theta^k$):
  1. Sample candidate $\tilde\theta$ from $q(\tilde\theta\mid\theta^k)$.
  2. Compute acceptance probability: $$\alpha(\theta^k,\tilde\theta)=\min\!\left(\frac{p(\tilde\theta\mid y)}{p(\theta^k\mid y)},\;1\right)$$
  3. Set $\theta^{k+1}=\tilde\theta$ with probability $\alpha$; otherwise $\theta^{k+1}=\theta^k$ (stay and record again).
ConditionAction
$p(\tilde\theta\mid y)>p(\theta^k\mid y)\;\Rightarrow r>1$Always accept — move to $\tilde\theta$
$p(\tilde\theta\mid y)Accept with probability $r$; else stay at $\theta^k$

No Normalising Constant Needed

The ratio $\displaystyle\frac{p(\tilde\theta\mid y)}{p(\theta^k\mid y)}=\frac{L(y\mid\tilde\theta)\,p(\tilde\theta)}{L(y\mid\theta^k)\,p(\theta^k)}$. The normalising constant $p(y)$ cancels — only the unnormalised posterior is required.

5.3.2 The Metropolis–Hastings Algorithm

Extends Metropolis to asymmetric proposal densities. To maintain reversibility, the acceptance probability becomes:

$$\alpha(\theta^k,\tilde\theta)=\min\!\left(\frac{p(\tilde\theta\mid y)\,q(\theta^k\mid\tilde\theta)}{p(\theta^k\mid y)\,q(\tilde\theta\mid\theta^k)},\;1\right)$$

The correction factor $q(\theta^k\mid\tilde\theta)/q(\tilde\theta\mid\theta^k)$ compensates for the asymmetry of the proposal — this is the reversibility condition.

Independent MH Algorithm

Special case: $q(\tilde\theta\mid\theta^k)\equiv q(\tilde\theta)$ — proposal does not depend on current position. The acceptance ratio becomes $\min\!\bigl([p(\tilde\theta\mid y)\,q(\theta^k)]/[p(\theta^k\mid y)\,q(\tilde\theta)],1\bigr)$. Best performance when $q$ is close to the posterior. Used in Example VI.8 to sample a $t_3(3,2^2)$ distribution.

5.3.3 Gibbs Sampler as a Special Case of MH

Define the Gibbs transition for the $j$th step:

$$q_j^G(\tilde\theta\mid\theta^k)=p(\tilde\theta_j\mid\theta^k_{-j},y)\;\text{if }\tilde\theta_{-j}=\theta^k_{-j},\text{ else }0$$

Computing the MH ratio $r$ for this step:

$$r=\frac{p(\tilde\theta\mid y)\,q_j^G(\theta^k\mid\tilde\theta)}{p(\theta^k\mid y)\,q_j^G(\tilde\theta\mid\theta^k)} =\frac{p(\tilde\theta\mid y)\,p(\theta^k_j\mid\theta^k_{-j},y)}{p(\theta^k\mid y)\,p(\tilde\theta_j\mid\theta^k_{-j},y)}=1$$

Since $r=1$ always, $\alpha=1$ — every Gibbs move is accepted. The Gibbs sampler is a composition of $d$ MH algorithms with acceptance probability always equal to 1.

5.4 Justification of the MCMC Approaches

The theoretical foundation explains why MCMC works: the generated chains converge to the posterior as their stationary distribution, and ergodic averages consistently estimate posterior summary measures.

Markov Chain Fundamentals

A Markov chain is a sequence of random variables $\theta$ where, given the present, past and future are independent. When transition probabilities do not depend on $k$, the chain is homogeneous; a constant transition kernel $K(x,A)=p(\theta^1\in A\mid\theta^0=x)$ describes the moves.

Ergodicity Criteria

A Markov chain is ergodic if it satisfies all three of:

  • Irreducibility: The chain can reach every possible outcome from any starting position.
  • Aperiodicity: No cyclic behaviour — the chain does not oscillate at regular intervals.
  • Positive recurrence: Every state is visited infinitely often; expected return time to any state is finite.

Practical meaning: an ergodic chain explores the posterior distribution exhaustively.

Key Theorems

Theorem 5.4.1 — Stationary Distribution

When $(\theta^k)_k$ is an ergodic Markov chain with stationary distribution $\pi$, the limiting distribution is also $\pi$.

Theorem 5.4.2 — Markov Chain Law of Large Numbers

For an ergodic Markov chain with finite expected value for $t(\theta)$, the ergodic average

$$\bar t_k=\frac1k\sum_{j=1}^k t(\theta^j)\;\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\;E_\pi[t(\theta)]$$

justifies using the converged chain to estimate posterior means, medians, standard deviations, quantiles, etc.

Theorem 5.4.3 — Markov Chain Central Limit Theorem

For a uniformly or geometrically ergodic chain,

$$\sqrt{k}\,(\bar t_k-E_\pi[t(\theta)])/\tau\;\xrightarrow{d}\;N(0,1),$$

where

$$\tau^2=\gamma_0\!\left(1+2\sum_{m=1}^\infty\rho_m\right),$$

with $\gamma_0=\operatorname{Var}(t(\theta^k))$ and $\rho_m=\text{autocorrelation of lag }m$. High autocorrelations inflate $\tau^2$ compared to i.i.d. sampling, quantifying the efficiency cost of dependence.

Reversibility and Detailed Balance

A Markov chain is reversible if the rate of moving from $x$ to $y$ equals the rate from $y$ to $x$ — the detailed balance condition:

$$\int_A\int_B K(\theta,\phi)\,d\phi\,d\theta=\int_B\int_A K(\phi,\theta)\,d\theta\,d\phi\quad\forall\,A,B$$

Both the Gibbs sampler and MH algorithm produce reversible chains. Reversibility is a sufficient condition for the chain to have the target distribution as its stationary distribution.

5.4.1 MH Algorithm Creates Reversibility (Discrete Proof)

For states $x_i,x_j$ with transition matrix $Q$ and acceptance probability $\alpha_{ij}=\min(1,\pi_j q_{ji}/\pi_i q_{ij})$:

$$\pi_i p_{ij}=\pi_i\alpha_{ij}q_{ij}=\min(\pi_i q_{ij},\pi_j q_{ji})=\pi_j p_{ji}$$

The detailed balance condition $\pi_i p_{ij}=\pi_j p_{ji}$ is satisfied → $\pi$ is the stationary distribution. Peskun (1973) proved this choice of $\alpha_{ij}$ is optimal in terms of sampling variance.

5.4.2 Gibbs Sampler Properties
The Gibbs sampler generates an ergodic chain with the posterior as stationary distribution when the full conditionals are strictly positive (Theorem 10.10, Robert & Casella 2004). This mild condition holds in essentially all practical Bayesian problems.
⚠️ Convergence ≠ Speed
The theoretical results guarantee convergence but say nothing about how fast. High autocorrelation in a chain (e.g., $\beta_1$ in the osteoporosis example) means slow convergence — requiring longer chains and larger burn‑in. Trace plots give a visual indication; formal convergence diagnostics (Gelman–Rubin, Geweke) are covered in Chapter 7.

5.6 The Reversible Jump MCMC Algorithm

The Reversible Jump MCMC (RJMCMC) algorithm (Green 1995) extends the standard MH algorithm to sample from spaces of varying dimension — enabling joint inference on model choice and model parameters.

Applications

  • Mixtures with unknown number of components
  • Change‑point problems with unknown number/location of change points
  • Bayesian model and variable selection
  • Analysis of quantitative trait locus (QTL) data

The Dimension Matching Condition

When jumping from model $m$ (parameter dimension $d$) augmented with auxiliary vector $u$ (dimension $r$) to model $\tilde m$ (dimension $\tilde d$) augmented with $\tilde u$ (dimension $\tilde r$), the bijective transformation $h_{m,\tilde m}:\mathbb R^{d+r}\to\mathbb R^{\tilde d+\tilde r}$ must satisfy:

$$d+r=\tilde d+\tilde r$$

(dimension matching condition). This ensures bijectivity and permits computation of the Jacobian needed for reversibility.

RJMCMC Acceptance Probability

The acceptance probability is:

$$\alpha(\psi_m,\tilde\psi_{\tilde m})=\min\!\bigl(1,\;A_{m,\tilde m}\bigr)$$

where

$$A_{m,\tilde m}= \frac{p(\tilde\psi_{\tilde m}\mid y)\,j_{\tilde m,m}(\tilde\psi_{\tilde m}\mid\psi_m)\,\tilde g(\tilde u)} {p(\psi_m\mid y)\,j_{m,\tilde m}(\psi_m\mid\tilde\psi_{\tilde m})\,g(u)} \times\left|\frac{\partial(\tilde\theta_m,\tilde u)}{\partial(\theta_m,u)}\right|$$

The Jacobian term accounts for the volume change when transforming between spaces of different dimensions, ensuring the detailed balance condition is satisfied.

Example VI.10 — Poisson vs. Negative Binomial for dmft‑index

dmft‑index data: mean = 2.24, variance = 7.93 (clear overdispersion). Two models:

ModelParametersDimension
$m=1$: Poisson($\lambda$)$\theta_1\equiv\lambda$$d=1$
$m=2$: NB($\lambda,\kappa$), variance = $\lambda(1+\kappa\lambda)$$\theta_2=(\lambda,\kappa)$$d=2$

Trans‑dimensional moves (Hastie & Green 2012 construction)

  • Move 2 (Poisson → NB): Generate $u\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$. Set $\tilde\theta_2=h_{1,2}(\theta_1,u)=(\theta_1,\mu\exp(u))$. Keeps $\lambda$ unchanged; creates $\kappa=\mu\exp(u)$ as a lognormal variable. Jacobian = $\mu\exp(u)$.
  • Move 3 (NB → Poisson, inverse): $h_{2,1}(\tilde\theta_2)=(\tilde\theta_1,\log(\tilde\theta_2/\mu))$ — the exact inverse of $h_{1,2}$. Jacobian = $1/\tilde\theta_2$.

Results and Sensitivity

NB preferred in all 4 scenarios tested (56%–74% of chain time in model 2). Every proposed move Poisson→NB was accepted. However, the posterior median of $\kappa$ varied from 0.78 to 1.75 across scenarios — strong sensitivity to choices of $\sigma$ and $\mu$ in $h_{m,\tilde m}$. This illustrates RJMCMC's key challenge: significant tuning is needed for the bijection function and auxiliary distributions.

Short Answer (5 Questions)

Short Answer 1

Explain the concept of "burn‑in" in MCMC sampling. Why is the initial portion of the Markov chain discarded before inference, and what factors influence the appropriate choice of burn‑in length? Use specific examples from the chapter to illustrate your answer.

Model Answer:
The burn‑in is the initial segment of an MCMC chain that is discarded before posterior inference is drawn. The chain is initialized at an arbitrary starting value $\theta^0$, which may lie far from the region of high posterior probability. During burn‑in, the chain is in a transient phase — its distribution still depends on the starting value and does not yet reflect the target posterior. Retaining these early samples would bias estimates. Once the chain has "converged" (i.e., its distribution no longer depends meaningfully on $\theta^0$), the remaining samples form a representative dependent sample from the posterior.

Factors influencing burn‑in length:
  • Distance of starting value from the posterior mass: In Example VI.3 (semi‑conjugate prior, starting values (5.5, 3)), the trace plot shows the chain immediately leaving the extreme starting position, but a burn‑in of 500 is still applied. More extreme starting values require longer burn‑in.
  • Autocorrelation of the chain: In Example VI.5 (osteoporosis regression), $\beta_1$ has much higher autocorrelation than $\sigma^2$. The trace plot for $\beta_1$ shows slow, wandering behaviour, meaning the chain takes longer to "forget" its starting position. High autocorrelation requires both a larger burn‑in and a longer retained chain.
  • Dimensionality and posterior correlation structure: Highly correlated regression parameters tend to produce slowly mixing chains. The block Gibbs sampler can mitigate this.
  • Choice of sampler and proposal width: In Example VI.7, a too‑narrow Metropolis proposal ($\tau^2=0.001$) leads to very slow exploration — effectively meaning more of the chain is wasted before it adequately explores the posterior.
In all examples, a chain of 1500 iterations with 500 burn‑in (retaining 1000) was used. Chapter 7 provides convergence diagnostics (Gelman–Rubin, Geweke, trace plots) to guide these choices rigorously.
Short Answer 2

State the bivariate Gibbs sampling scheme. Using Example VI.2 (joint distribution $f(x,y)\propto\binom{n}{x}\,y^{x+\alpha-1}(1-y)^{\,n-x+\beta-1}$), derive both full conditional distributions $f(x|y)$ and $f(y|x)$, write out the Gibbs iterative scheme, and explain why this example demonstrates that the Gibbs sampler is not restricted to Bayesian settings.

Model Answer:
Bivariate Gibbs scheme:
At iteration $(k+1)$:
1. Sample $\theta_1^{(k+1)}$ from $p(\theta_1\mid\theta_2^k,y)$
2. Sample $\theta_2^{(k+1)}$ from $p(\theta_2\mid\theta_1^{(k+1)},y)$ [uses the updated $\theta_1^{(k+1)}$]

Deriving $f(x|y)$:
Write the joint as $f(x,y)\propto\binom{n}{x}y^x(1-y)^{n-x}\times y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{\beta-1}$. To obtain $f(x|y)$, remove all factors not involving $x$. The term $y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{\beta-1}$ does not depend on $x$, so:
$$f(x|y)\propto\binom{n}{x}y^x(1-y)^{n-x}=\text{Bin}(n,y).$$

Deriving $f(y|x)$:
Remove all factors not involving $y$:
$$f(y|x)\propto y^{x+\alpha-1}(1-y)^{\,n-x+\beta-1}=\text{Beta}(x+\alpha,\;n-x+\beta).$$

Gibbs iterative scheme for Example VI.2:
  1. Sample $x^{(k+1)}$ from $\text{Bin}(n,y^k)$
  2. Sample $y^{(k+1)}$ from $\text{Beta}\bigl(x^{(k+1)}+\alpha,\;n-x^{(k+1)}+\beta\bigr)$
Non‑Bayesian context:
There is no likelihood function, no prior, no posterior — this is purely a mathematical joint distribution over a discrete variable $x$ and a continuous variable $y$. The Gibbs sampler operates entirely from the full conditionals, without any reference to Bayes' theorem. The true marginal $f(x)$ equals the Beta‑Binomial $\text{BB}(n,\alpha,\beta)$, providing an analytical benchmark. For $n=30$, $\alpha=2$, $\beta=4$ with starting values $x=0$, $y=0.5$, the Gibbs‑sampled histogram closely matches the true distribution — demonstrating correctness independent of starting values and confirming that the sampler is a general tool for exploring any distribution expressible via tractable conditionals.

Define the Gibbs transition kernel for the $j$th parameter:
$$q_j^G(\tilde\theta\mid\theta^k)=p(\tilde\theta_j\mid\theta^k_{-j},y)\;\text{if }\tilde\theta_{-j}=\theta^k_{-j},\text{ else }0.$$
The MH ratio for the $j$th Gibbs substep is:
$$r=\frac{p(\tilde\theta\mid y)\,q_j^G(\theta^k\mid\tilde\theta)}{p(\theta^k\mid y)\,q_j^G(\tilde\theta\mid\theta^k)} =\frac{p(\tilde\theta\mid y)\,p(\theta^k_j\mid\tilde\theta_{-j},y)}{p(\theta^k\mid y)\,p(\tilde\theta_j\mid\theta^k_{-j},y)}\,.$$
Since $\tilde\theta_{-j}=\theta^k_{-j}$, we have $p(\tilde\theta_{-j}\mid y)=p(\theta^k_{-j}\mid y)$, so:
$$=\frac{p(\tilde\theta\mid y)/p(\tilde\theta_j\mid\theta^k_{-j},y)}{p(\theta^k\mid y)/p(\theta^k_j\mid\theta^k_{-j},y)}=1.$$
Therefore $r=1$ and $\alpha=1$.

When to prefer MH:
  • Full conditionals are not recognisable standard distributions (e.g., logistic regression).
  • High posterior correlation causes the Gibbs sampler to mix very slowly — block MH then preferred.
  • The model does not have conjugate or semi‑conjugate structure, making full conditionals intractable.
  • When a good proposal density can be constructed that approximates the posterior well (Independent MH approach).
Short Answer 4

State the three ergodicity criteria for a Markov chain and explain their practical meaning for MCMC. Then state the Markov Chain Law of Large Numbers (Theorem 5.4.2) and the Markov Chain CLT (Theorem 5.4.3). How do these theorems together justify using MCMC to compute posterior summary measures, and what does the CLT tell us about the efficiency loss due to autocorrelation?

Model Answer:
Three ergodicity criteria and practical meaning:
  • Irreducibility: The chain can reach every possible outcome from any starting position.
    Practically: the MCMC algorithm can explore any region of the parameter space regardless of where it starts. If the posterior is multimodal, the chain can jump between modes.
  • Aperiodicity: No cyclic behaviour — the chain does not return to each state only at regular multiples of some period.
    Practically: the chain's sampling is not rhythmically biased; it can return to a region at any time.
  • Positive recurrence: Every state is visited infinitely often with finite expected return time.
    Practically: the chain does not get permanently trapped in any region; all parts of the posterior receive adequate sampling frequency over a long run.

Theorem 5.4.2 (MC‑LLN):
For an ergodic Markov chain with finite $E[t(\theta)]$, the ergodic average
$$\bar t_k=\frac1k\sum_{j=1}^k t(\theta^j)\;\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\;E_\pi[t(\theta)]$$

Theorem 5.4.3 (MC‑CLT):
For a uniformly or geometrically ergodic chain,
$$\sqrt{k}\,(\bar t_k-E_\pi[t(\theta)])/\tau\;\xrightarrow{d}\;N(0,1),$$
where $\tau^2=\gamma_0\!\bigl(1+2\sum_{m=1}^\infty\rho_m\bigr)$.

Justification for MCMC posterior inference:
  • Posterior mean: $t(\theta)=\theta\;\Rightarrow\;\bar t_k\to E[\theta\mid y]$
  • Posterior variance: $t(\theta)=\theta^2\;\Rightarrow\;\bar t_k\to E[\theta^2\mid y]$; then $\operatorname{Var}=E[\theta^2\mid y]-(E[\theta\mid y])^2$
  • Posterior quantiles: $t(\theta)=I(\theta\le c)\;\Rightarrow\;\bar t_k\to P(\theta\le c\mid y)$; invert to get credible‑interval limits
Even though chain elements are dependent (not i.i.d.), the ergodic theorem extends the classical LLN to this setting.

Efficiency loss from autocorrelation (MC‑CLT):
The asymptotic variance of $\bar t_k$ is $\tau^2/k=\gamma_0(1+2\sum\rho_m)/k$. For i.i.d. samples, this would be $\gamma_0/k$. The factor $(1+2\sum\rho_m)\ge1$ quantifies how much the variance is inflated by dependence — the "effective sample size" is $k/(1+2\sum\rho_m)$. In Example VI.5, high autocorrelation for $\beta_1$ means the effective sample size from 1000 Gibbs iterations is far smaller than 1000.
Short Answer 5

Describe the Reversible Jump MCMC (RJMCMC) algorithm and the dimension matching condition. Using Example VI.10 (choosing between a Poisson and a negative binomial distribution for the dmft‑index), describe the two trans‑dimensional move types, specify the bijection function $h$, state the acceptance probabilities $A_{1,2}$ and $A_{2,1}$, and explain the role of the Jacobian. What do the results suggest about model selection, and what are the limitations of the RJMCMC in this example?

Model Answer:
RJMCMC overview:
Green (1995) extended the standard MH algorithm to allow jumps between parameter spaces of different dimensions — the trans‑dimensional case. The target is $p(m,\theta_m\mid y)$ over $\psi_m=(m,\theta_m)\in\bigcup_m\{m\}\times\Theta_m$, enabling joint inference on both model choice and model parameters.

Dimension matching condition:
When jumping from model $m$ (dim $d$, auxiliary vector $u$ of dim $r$) to model $\tilde m$ (dim $\tilde d$, auxiliary vector $\tilde u$ of dim $\tilde r$), the bijective transformation $h_{m,\tilde m}:\mathbb R^{d+r}\to\mathbb R^{\tilde d+\tilde r}$ must satisfy
$$d+r=\tilde d+\tilde r$$
(dimension matching condition). This ensures bijectivity of $h$ and the existence of a well‑defined Jacobian.

Example VI.10 setup:
  • Model 1 ($m=1$): Poisson($\lambda$), $\theta_1\equiv\lambda$ (1 parameter)
  • Model 2 ($m=2$): NB($\lambda,\kappa$), $\theta_2=(\lambda,\kappa)$ (2 parameters)
Equal model prior probabilities: $p(m=1)=p(m=2)=0.5$.

Trans‑dimensional moves (Hastie & Green 2012):
  • Move 2 (Poisson → NB):
    Generate $u\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$. Set
    $$\tilde\theta_2=h_{1,2}(\theta_1,u)=(\theta_1,\;\mu\exp(u)).$$
    Jacobian = $\mu\exp(u)$. Acceptance: $A_{1,2}= \frac{p(2,\tilde\theta_2\mid y)}{p(1,\theta_1\mid y)}\times\frac{1}{\phi(u;0,\sigma^2)}\times\mu\exp(u)$.
  • Move 3 (NB → Poisson, reverse):
    $$h_{2,1}(\tilde\theta_2)=(\tilde\theta_1,\;\log(\tilde\theta_2/\mu))$$
    Jacobian = $1/\tilde\theta_2$. Acceptance: $A_{2,1}= \frac{p(1,\theta_1\mid y)}{p(2,\tilde\theta_2\mid y)}\times\phi(\log(\tilde\theta_2/\mu);0,\sigma^2)\times\frac{1}{\tilde\theta_2}$.
Role of the Jacobian:
The Jacobian accounts for the change of volume when the transformation changes the scale or orientation of the parameter space. Without it, the detailed balance condition would not be satisfied and the chain would not converge to the correct posterior.

Results:
The negative binomial was preferred in all 4 tested scenarios (56%–74% of chain time in model 2); every proposed Poisson→NB move was accepted. The evidence is not overwhelming.

Limitations:
  • Posterior median of $\kappa$ varied from 0.78 to 1.75 across the four $(\sigma,\mu)$ settings — high sensitivity to the choice of the bijection function $h$.
  • RJMCMC requires substantial tuning: $\sigma$ and $\mu$ must reflect what is observed in data; too large → unrealistic $\kappa$ values; too small → proposed NB distribution too similar to Poisson for meaningful discrimination.
  • The algorithm allows great flexibility in choosing $h$, the dimension $r$, and the density $g$ — this flexibility creates room for refinement but also risk of poor choices that dominate results.
