📖 Study Notes — Chapter 5: MCMC Sampling
1. Why MCMC?

Computing the posterior distribution \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid \boldsymbol{y})\) analytically requires evaluating the normalising constant:

$$p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\int p(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$
Two limitations of classical approaches:
(1) Solving analytically is often infeasible — the integration constant can rarely be computed in closed form for realistic models.
(2) Numerical integration is practical only when the parameter dimension is small (a few parameters).

The solution is sampling. Instead of computing the full posterior, we generate a large collection of draws whose empirical distribution approximates \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{y})\). Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods achieve this efficiently via two algorithms:

1. Gibbs sampler — requires the full conditional distributions of each parameter.
2. Metropolis-Hastings algorithm — requires only the ability to evaluate the posterior up to a constant; no full conditionals needed.
2. The Gibbs Sampler (Section 5.1)

The key theoretical fact underpinning the Gibbs sampler is that the joint distribution \(p(\theta_1,\theta_2\mid\boldsymbol{y})\) is completely determined by knowing its two full conditional distributions:

$$p(\theta_1\mid\theta_2,\boldsymbol{y})\qquad\text{and}\qquad p(\theta_2\mid\theta_1,\boldsymbol{y})$$

This property makes it possible to reconstruct samples from the joint by alternately sampling from these one-dimensional conditionals.

2.1 Bivariate Gibbs Algorithm
Initialisation: Choose starting values \(\theta_1^0\) and \(\theta_2^0\) (only one is strictly needed).

At iteration \(k\), generate the \((k+1)\)-th sample:
   Step 1: Sample \(\theta_1^{(k+1)}\) from \(p(\theta_1\mid\theta_2^k,\boldsymbol{y})\)
   Step 2: Sample \(\theta_2^{(k+1)}\) from \(p(\theta_2\mid\theta_1^{(k+1)},\boldsymbol{y})\)

Repeat for \(k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\)

Notice in Step 2 the newly updated value \(\theta_1^{(k+1)}\) is used immediately — this is the standard Gibbs update.

2.2 Properties of the Gibbs Chain

The result is a chain of vectors \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k = (\theta_1^k, \theta_2^k)^T,\; k=1,2,\ldots\)

Markov property. The chain satisfies: $$p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^k,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k-1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y})=p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^k,\boldsymbol{y})$$ The next state depends only on the current state, not the full history — hence it is a Markov chain.

Convergence. Under mild regularity conditions, the chain converges to the posterior (target) distribution. From iteration \(k_0\) onward, summary statistics from the chain consistently estimate the true posterior quantities.
Burn-in: The early part of the chain depends heavily on the (arbitrary) starting values and should be discarded. This initial portion is called the burn-in. In the SAP example, a burn-in of 500 iterations out of 1,500 total was used.

Visually, the bivariate Gibbs sampler produces a characteristic zigzag pattern in the parameter plane, because each step moves along only one coordinate axis at a time (horizontal then vertical).

3. Autocorrelation

Because the Gibbs chain is generated sequentially, consecutive samples are dependent. The degree of dependence is measured by autocorrelation at various lags:

LagDefinition
Lag 1Correlation of \(\beta_1^k\) with \(\beta_1^{(k-1)}\)
Lag 2Correlation of \(\beta_1^k\) with \(\beta_1^{(k-2)}\)
Lag \(m\)Correlation of \(\beta_1^k\) with \(\beta_1^{(k-m)}\)
High autocorrelation implies two practical consequences:
(1) The burn-in part must be larger, because the chain takes longer to forget its initial position.
(2) The post-burn-in chain must be longer to obtain stable posterior estimates, since successive draws carry less new information.
4. The Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm (Section 5.2)

The Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm is a more general MCMC method. Its key advantage: it only requires the ability to evaluate the (unnormalised) posterior \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{y})\propto p(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) — no full conditionals are needed.

4.1 Metropolis Algorithm (symmetric proposal)

Assume the chain is currently at \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k\). The next value \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}\) is generated as:

Step 1 — Propose. Sample a candidate \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\) from a symmetric proposal density \(q(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^k)\), meaning \(q(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^k)=q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\).

Step 2 — Accept or reject. Set $$\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}=\begin{cases}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}&\text{with probability }\alpha(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\quad\text{(accept)}\\[4pt]\boldsymbol{\theta}^k&\text{otherwise}\quad\text{(reject)}\end{cases}$$
Acceptance probability: $$\alpha(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\min\!\left(r=\frac{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mid\boldsymbol{y})}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k\mid\boldsymbol{y})},\;1\right)$$
  • If the candidate has higher posterior density (\(r>1\)): always accept (\(\alpha=1\)).
  • If the candidate has lower posterior density (\(r<1\)): accept with probability \(r\).
  • If rejected: the chain stays at \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k\), which is repeated in the chain.

Crucially, the normalising constant cancels in the ratio \(r\) — so only the unnormalised posterior is needed.

4.2 Choice of Proposal Variance — SAP Study Example

In Example VI.7, the proposal was \(\mathbf{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k, \Sigma)\). The variance \(\Sigma\) critically affects performance:

Proposal \(\Sigma\)Acceptance RateChain BehaviourQuality
\(\text{diag}(0.03, 0.03)\)~40%Good mixing, jumps across parameter space✅ Good approximation
\(\text{diag}(0.001, 0.001)\)~84%Tiny steps, chain barely moves❌ Poor approximation — concentrated near starting value
Tuning rule of thumb: Target an acceptance rate around 20–50% for a single-parameter update (or ~23% for high-dimensional problems). Too-small steps \(\Rightarrow\) high acceptance but slow exploration. Too-large steps \(\Rightarrow\) low acceptance and many rejections.

✏️ Short Answer Questions
SA1. Explain why MCMC methods are needed in Bayesian inference. What are the limitations of the two classical alternatives?

The posterior distribution is: $$p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\int p(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}}$$

Limitation 1 — Analytical solution: Computing the normalising constant (denominator) analytically is usually infeasible. Closed-form posteriors only arise for conjugate prior-likelihood pairs, which cover a narrow class of models.

Limitation 2 — Numerical integration: Quadrature methods can evaluate the integral numerically, but their computational cost grows exponentially with the number of parameters. They become impractical as soon as more than a few parameters are involved.

Solution — MCMC: Instead of computing the posterior analytically, MCMC generates a large sample whose empirical distribution approximates the posterior. Since the normalising constant cancels in computations like the MH acceptance ratio, high-dimensional posteriors become tractable.

SA2. Describe the bivariate Gibbs sampler algorithm in full detail. State the Markov property and explain the role of burn-in.

Setup: The joint posterior \(p(\theta_1,\theta_2\mid\boldsymbol{y})\) is completely determined by the two full conditionals \(p(\theta_1\mid\theta_2,\boldsymbol{y})\) and \(p(\theta_2\mid\theta_1,\boldsymbol{y})\).

Algorithm: Initialise with \(\theta_1^0\) (one value is sufficient). At iteration \(k\):
— Step 1: Sample \(\theta_1^{(k+1)}\sim p(\theta_1\mid\theta_2^k,\boldsymbol{y})\)
— Step 2: Sample \(\theta_2^{(k+1)}\sim p(\theta_2\mid\theta_1^{(k+1)},\boldsymbol{y})\)

Result: A chain of vectors \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k=(\theta_1^k,\theta_2^k)^T\), \(k=1,2,\ldots\), which satisfies the Markov property: $$p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^k,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k-1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y})=p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^k,\boldsymbol{y})$$ The future state depends only on the current state, not the full history.

Burn-in: The initial segment of the chain is heavily influenced by the arbitrary starting values. This portion is discarded (burn-in). After iteration \(k_0\), under mild regularity conditions, the chain has converged to the target posterior and summary statistics computed from the remaining chain consistently estimate the true posterior quantities.

SA3. Define autocorrelation in the context of MCMC chains and explain its practical consequences for chain length and burn-in.

Definition: Because MCMC chains are generated sequentially, consecutive samples are dependent. The autocorrelation at lag \(m\) is the correlation between \(\beta_1^k\) and \(\beta_1^{(k-m)}\) across all iterations \(k\). A slowly decaying autocorrelation function indicates the chain moves slowly through the parameter space.

Practical consequences of high autocorrelation:

(1) Larger burn-in required: The chain takes longer to "forget" its starting position and converge to the target distribution. The burn-in period must be extended accordingly.

(2) Longer post-burn-in chain required: Because successive draws carry less independent information (they are highly correlated), a much larger number of post-burn-in samples is needed to obtain stable, precise posterior estimates.

This is visible in trace plots: a fast-mixing chain (low autocorrelation) looks like white noise, while a slow-mixing chain (high autocorrelation) shows smooth, slowly wandering behaviour, as seen for \(\beta_1\) in the second trace plot example.

SA4. State the Metropolis algorithm in full. Explain why the normalising constant is not needed, and what happens to the chain when a proposal is rejected.

Algorithm: Suppose the chain is at \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k\).

Step 1: Sample a candidate \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\) from the symmetric proposal density \(q(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^k)\), i.e., \(q(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^k)=q(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\).

Step 2: Compute the acceptance probability: $$\alpha(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\min\!\left(\frac{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mid\boldsymbol{y})}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k\mid\boldsymbol{y})},\;1\right)$$ Set \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\) with probability \(\alpha\), otherwise \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k+1)}=\boldsymbol{\theta}^k\).

Normalising constant cancellation: The acceptance ratio is a ratio of posteriors. Writing \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{y})=c\cdot p(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\), the constant \(c\) appears in both numerator and denominator and cancels: $$r=\frac{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mid\boldsymbol{y})}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k\mid\boldsymbol{y})}=\frac{c\cdot p(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{c\cdot p(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}^k)p(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k)}$$ Only the likelihood and prior (both available) are needed.

Rejection: When the proposal is rejected, the current value \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k\) is repeated as the next element of the chain. This is not wasted effort — the repeated value is a valid draw from the stationary distribution and contributes to posterior estimates.

SA5. Using the SAP study example, explain how the choice of proposal variance affects the Metropolis algorithm. What is an appropriate acceptance rate to aim for?

In Example VI.7, the proposal was \(\mathbf{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^k,\Sigma)\) with two choices of \(\Sigma\):

Case 1 — \(\Sigma=\text{diag}(0.03, 0.03)\), acceptance rate ≈ 40%: The proposal variance is well-calibrated. Candidates are proposed far enough from the current state to adequately explore the posterior, yet close enough that many are in high-density regions and accepted. The resulting histogram closely matches the true posterior (solid red curve).

Case 2 — \(\Sigma=\text{diag}(0.001, 0.001)\), acceptance rate ≈ 84%: The proposal variance is far too small. Proposed candidates are extremely close to the current state, so almost all are accepted. However, the chain moves so slowly that even after 1,500 iterations it has barely moved from its starting point, producing a very poor approximation of the true posterior — the histogram is far too concentrated.

Practical guidance: An excessively high acceptance rate (like 84%) signals underexploration, while an excessively low rate signals that proposals are too bold and mostly rejected. A rule of thumb for a \(d\)-dimensional target is to aim for an acceptance rate around 20–50% (often ~23% is quoted for high-dimensional models). The proposal variance must be tuned accordingly to balance exploration and acceptance.
